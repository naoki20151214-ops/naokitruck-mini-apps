LEVEL 1 Understanding through everyday examples / Everyday examples

01

What work is hidden in a single photo?

We break down an everyday action, from receiving light to saving the photo.

After reading: You can explain where semiconductors work in smartphones and cars.

You press the shutter. You brighten your friend's face. You save it. The actions on the screen are short, but inside, "reading light," "calculating," and "keeping data" are all happening. Semiconductors are the materials and parts that share this work. Follow along, step by step, to see what happens where.

Sense Receive light
The camera turns light into electrical information. It is the entry point, like the human eye. A part that reads changes in its surroundings like this is called a sensor. What it shares with the human eye is the role of receiving light. The sensor itself does not understand who is in the picture.
Calculate Brighten the photo
Using the information received, it calculates the colors and brightness to show on the screen. Following the app's instructions, it also decides what to do next.
Remember Work on it and save it
There is a place to hold data temporarily while editing, and a place to keep photos even after the power is off. "Using it now" and "keeping it for later" are different jobs.
Manage power Deliver the electricity needed
It adjusts the electricity from the battery or charger into a form each part can use. Calculating parts alone cannot run the whole device.

You can look at cars the same way. They sense the surroundings and the car's condition, decide how to move, and, in electric vehicles, manage the electricity sent to the motor. Semiconductors do not turn the wheels directly. They work together with the motor and other parts.

Even in the large computers that run generative AI, the core is "calculating" and "delivering the data needed." They are much bigger than smartphones, but if you think in terms of roles, you can see the connection. Later on, we will look closely at how this data is moved.

These four are not a complete list that covers every product. For example, light-emitting diodes (LEDs), which turn electricity into light, are also parts that use semiconductors. For now, use the four as markers for seeing what each part is in charge of inside everyday devices.

LEVEL 2 How it works, one step at a time / From electricity to circuits

02

How can tiny switches do calculations?

We connect the properties of semiconductor materials to transistors and circuits.

After reading: You understand how materials, transistors, circuits, and dies are related.

Let's check what the name "semiconductor" really means. A substance like copper that lets electricity flow easily is called a conductor, and one like glass that does not is called an insulator. Semiconductor materials such as silicon have properties in between. By adding tiny amounts of impurities or applying voltage or light, you can change how electricity flows through them. It does not mean "lets only half the electricity through." In everyday speech, the small parts that use this property are also called "semiconductors."

The key is being able to control this property exactly where you want. The material is processed to create paths for electricity, turning it into devices that can let current through or stop it. When these are combined with wiring, they become circuits that calculate and remember.

Silicon is the material. A chip is a small piece with circuits built into it. A small piece of circuitry cut out of the wafer is also called a "die." A package is a die that has been connected to the outside and protected. In products and conversation, the whole package is sometimes called a "chip" or "IC," so thinking separately about the material, the contents, and the outside helps avoid confusion.

How does it switch with no moving parts? — Transistors

The basic unit for controlling the flow of electricity in a tiny space is the transistor. A "device" here means an element that does a basic job like this. In this article, we distinguish current, the amount of charge that flows per unit of time, from voltage, the strength of the push that makes electricity flow.

In the MOS transistor, which is widely used in computing circuits, the voltage applied to a control part called the "gate" makes it easy or hard for current to flow between two other terminals. No parts move, as they would in a mechanical switch.

Imagine the gate as the handle of a faucet that controls the flow of electricity. The similarity is that turning a handle changes the flow somewhere else. In reality, though, there is no water and no moving valve. The gate's voltage changes how easily current flows.

In digital circuits, a low voltage range and a high voltage range are matched to "0" and "1." By combining transistors, you can build logic circuits whose output changes depending on the input. Combine them further, and you can add numbers, make decisions, and hold data. Stacking up huge numbers of tiny electrical controls leads to complex information processing. Transistors can also be used to amplify signals, so not all of them are only for 0s and 1s.

If you want to go one step deeper into "how switches become calculations": Read about how voltage, 0s and 1s, and logic circuits connect. It fills in how material properties turn into information processing.

A material becomes "a system that handles information"
The material's properties are used in transistors, which are combined into circuits. 0 and 1 correspond to voltage states. So how are the "calculating" role and the "remembering" role divided among these circuits?

LEVEL 2 How it works, one step at a time / Division of roles among parts

03

Can't one part do both calculating and remembering?

We think about CPU, GPU, DRAM, and NAND through photo editing.

After reading: You can tell apart ways of calculating, and working memory from storage.

A part that brightens the photo, and a part that keeps the photo. Both are circuits, but they are asked to do different jobs. Here, we give names to the four roles we saw at the start. If you look at "what each is good at" before the name, spec sheets become easier to read.

Why are there two kinds of calculators? — CPU and GPU

The CPU (central processing unit) is the main calculating part. It handles many kinds of instructions and conditional branches and moves processing forward flexibly. For example, it carries out the next task according to an app's instructions.

The GPU (graphics processing unit) is good at doing similar calculations on lots of data in parallel. Besides drawing the screen, it is also well suited to AI matrix calculations, where numbers arranged in rows and columns are calculated all at once.

In kitchen terms, the CPU is the cook who changes the plan to fit each order, and the GPU is a big crew doing the same prep work all at once. The similarity is in how the work is handed out. CPUs can also handle several tasks in parallel, and GPUs are not machines that can only do one fixed task.

Adding a GPU does not make everything faster. Some tasks cannot move on until the previous result is ready, and some spend a lot of time moving data. CPUs also have parallel processing features, and in real devices the two share the work. Smartphones use an SoC (system on a chip) that combines the CPU, GPU, and more into one, so the number of roles and the number of parts do not match one to one.

What is the difference between memory that stays when the power is off and memory that disappears?

DRAM (pronounced "dee-ram") is working memory that quickly reads and writes the data used in calculations. A typical smallest unit of memory (memory cell) combines a capacitor, a tiny part that stores electricity, with the transistor we saw in the previous section.

The amount of stored electricity is called charge. Because this charge slowly leaks away, an operation called refresh is needed to keep the memory even while the power is on. When the power is turned off, the memory is lost. Think of it as spreading out the information you are using right now on a work desk.

NAND flash is memory that keeps information even when the power is off. It uses a structure that holds charge and is where photos and apps are stored. It is also used in SSDs, the storage devices in PCs and other machines. If DRAM is the work desk, this is the storage room.

This "desk and storage room" is a metaphor for their roles. Unlike papers on a desk, the information in DRAM disappears when the power is turned off. NAND does not guarantee permanent storage either. There are limits to how it holds charge and how many times it can be rewritten.

It differs from DRAM in the units for reading, writing, and erasing, in speed, and in how many rewrites it can handle, so one cannot simply replace the other. In an SSD, not only NAND but also a control circuit (controller) that manages reading, writing, and error correction is at work. Keep the memory parts separate from how the whole storage device works.

When you edit a photo, the needed data is read from NAND storage, placed in DRAM or similar memory, processed by the CPU, GPU, and so on, and the result is saved again. Even if calculation is fast, waiting time occurs if the needed data does not arrive. This relationship will also matter in the later discussion of AI semiconductors.

If you want to check the difference between "adding memory" and "adding storage": Read about how the CPU, memory, and storage work together. You can review it using everyday actions.

Can calculating alone handle charging and taking pictures?

Power semiconductors handle high voltage and current and are responsible for converting electricity and adjusting its supply. In chargers, they are used in circuits that change voltage. In electric vehicles, they are used in circuits that control the current sent to the motor. The less energy lost in conversion, the easier it is to keep heat down.

Depending on the use, different devices are chosen, such as diodes, which mainly let current flow in one direction, and power MOSFETs and IGBTs, which switch large amounts of power. They work together with the surrounding circuits, so adding a single device does not make a charger or a motor.

The voltage a device can handle without breaking is called its breakdown voltage (voltage rating), and it is considered together with efficiency and heat dissipation. Depending on the use, materials such as silicon carbide (SiC) and gallium nitride (GaN) are used in addition to silicon. Newer materials are not better for every use. They are chosen to match the performance needed.

Sensors handle the "sense" role we saw at the start. A camera's image sensor turns light into electrical signals. Also, the technology of building tiny mechanical structures with semiconductor processing techniques is called MEMS (pronounced "mems"), and a MEMS accelerometer reads changes in that structure as a signal.

Even an excellent computing chip cannot know what is going on around it without input information. Sensors sense, circuits decide, and power semiconductors drive the device. This is how the roles connect.

LEVEL 2 How it works, one step at a time / Stacking thin layers

04

How are tiny circuits built in?

On the wafer, adding, shaping, removing, and measuring are repeated.

After reading: You understand the difference between processes that make thin films and processes that shape them.

Add → Decide where → Remove → Flatten → Check

These roles are combined to make one layer, then repeated for the next layer.

Why make tiny chips on a big disk? — The wafer

The base on which many circuits are made at once is the silicon wafer. It is a thin slice of high-purity silicon crystal with its surface polished flat, and it is usually disk-shaped. Many identical circuit areas are laid out and processed on it, and at the end it is cut into individual dies. Processing a whole wafer at a time makes many circuits at once, rather than carving small parts one by one.

Picture laying out cookie shapes on one big sheet of dough, baking them all together, and then separating them. The similarity is making many from one sheet. With a wafer, though, cutting out shapes alone does not make circuits. Many processing steps are layered on before it is cut apart.

The starting wafer does not already have finished circuits on it. Following the designed pattern, the needed materials are added, locations are chosen and processed, and transistors and wiring are formed. The basic idea is stacking thin layers with different roles on a flat base.

Why stack thin films over and over? — Deposition

The process of making thin films suited to a purpose on the wafer surface is called deposition. There are metal films that become wiring, insulating films that separate electrical paths, films that protect the material below during later processing, and more. Without films, you could not create separate places to connect and places to insulate.

A good way to picture the cross-section is a mille crêpe cake. The similarity is the stacking of thin layers. In a chip, though, films are not just stacked. Patterns are processed where needed, and places that electrically connect the layers are also made. The films change in role and shape as they are built up into a 3D structure.

There are also different ways to make films. CVD (chemical vapor deposition) forms films from gases through chemical reactions. PVD (physical vapor deposition) physically sends material flying onto the surface. ALD (atomic layer deposition) builds up films little by little by repeating surface reactions. For now, it is enough to think, "even within deposition, the way material is delivered differs."

Which method is used depends on the material needed and the shape, such as whether it is a flat area or the inside of a deep hole. Differences in film thickness and uniformity change the electrical characteristics, so this links not only to the next processing step but also to later metrology.

How are tiny patterns transferred? — Lithography

Lithography is the process that decides where to process, including the position and shape. First, a light-sensitive material called photoresist is applied. Next comes exposure, which transfers the pattern with light, followed by development, which uses chemicals to leave the needed shape.

Transferring a pattern with light is similar to printing a photo. But the pattern is transferred not onto photo paper but onto light-sensitive resist. What is mainly made at this stage is a pattern that decides where to process later. Shining light alone does not turn the material below into a finished circuit.

If the position is off from the layer made earlier, wiring that is supposed to connect will not connect. So in addition to the ability to transfer fine patterns, aligning the layers with each other is also important.

How is the pattern turned into trenches and holes? — Etching

Etching is the process of selectively removing exposed material. It uses chemicals or plasma, a gas given energy so that it reacts more easily. Using the pattern prepared by lithography as a guide, trenches and holes are formed in the actual material.

In printmaking, drawing the sketch and carving away the unneeded parts are separate steps, right? Etching plays a role close to the second one. But instead of carving line by line with a blade, it uses differences between protected and exposed areas and differences in how materials react.

If too much is removed, cutting into the protective film, or if a hole is too shallow, you will not get the intended shape or electrical connection. Not only the depth of removal but also how far it spreads sideways affects the result.

In some processes, the next material is filled into the processed holes and trenches. "Deposition, which adds material," "lithography, which decides where to process," and "etching, which removes material to make shapes" are separate tasks, but they work together to build a single structure.

If you wonder what is left after a pattern is transferred with light: Read more about resist, exposure, and development. You can check the difference between the process that decides shapes and the process that removes material.

Why polish many times along the way? — CMP

CMP (chemical mechanical polishing) is the process that removes excess material from the surface and makes it flat. It is also called Chemical Mechanical Planarization. It combines the chemical action of slurry chemicals with the force of polishing to create a surface ready for the next process.

However, after deposition or filling, steps and excess film may remain. You want to build the next layer on top, but as is, it becomes harder to focus the exposure and processing variation increases. So CMP is used to even out the surface. What is needed is not a shiny look but flatness that allows the next process.

It is like leveling the floor before building the next story of a building. But CMP does not fill in low spots. It removes excess material through chemical action and polishing. If too much is removed, you lose even the structures that should remain, which is why "measuring" becomes necessary next.

Planarization makes it easier to build the layers above accurately. But more polishing is not always better. It is important to leave the material that is needed. After CMP, cleaning removes polishing particles and residue so they do not contaminate the next process.

How do you know too much was not removed? — Inspection and metrology

Inspection looks for problem spots such as particles, scratches, and missing parts of patterns. Metrology, on the other hand, checks things like film thickness, line width, and layer misalignment as numbers. In practice, some tools and terms overlap, but separating "looking for defects" from "measuring dimensions and properties" makes the purpose clear.

Here, the processes connect into one line. Stacking films creates steps. You want to make it flat. But you do not want to remove too much. That is why the technology for making and the technology for measuring are needed together.

If results fall outside the standard, the equipment condition and process conditions are investigated. If a defect is found only after processing is finished, all the materials and time up to that point are lost too, so catching problems along the way is important. The share of products that turn out good is called yield, and inspection and metrology data are also used to improve yield.

If you want to check the difference between looking for defects and measuring dimensions: Read about the difference between inspection and metrology. What you check tells you how the roles of tools and processes are divided.

Going through this sequence once does not finish the chip.

In practice, cleaning, heat treatment, ion implantation (adding impurities to change a material's properties), and more are also combined, and processing and checking are repeated for each layer needed. This is to form the transistors and build many layers of wiring on top. The order and number of steps vary by product and structure.

"Transferring" and "removing" are different jobs
Transfer the pattern, process the material, check it, and move on to the next layer. Repeating this creates circuits and wiring. But can a die be used in a smartphone just by cutting it out of the wafer?

LEVEL 2 How it works, one step at a time / Connecting to devices

05

How is a finished circuit connected to a smartphone?

In the back-end process, the tiny die is given ways to connect, be protected, and release heat.

After reading: You can explain why packages handle connection, protection, and heat dissipation.

Even when circuits are finished on the wafer, they cannot simply be mounted on a smartphone or car circuit board as is. The small, delicate die needs to be protected, electrically connected to the outside, and able to release heat. This is handled by assembly and packaging in the back-end process.

If it were only about protecting the die, you could imagine a container like a lunch box. But a real package also passes signals to the outside, receives power, and releases heat. The key point here is that it is not "done once it is in a box."

Typically, a wafer test is done first to check the chips electrically while they are still on the wafer. Next comes dicing, which cuts the wafer into individual dies, and the dies are mounted on a substrate or base. This prepares the small dies to be handled safely and connected to the outside.

The die is connected to the outside with thin wires or small connection points called bumps, protected with resin or similar material as needed, and tested again after assembly. The order and methods vary by product.

A package is not just a container. It also affects whether signals can travel quickly, whether power can be supplied steadily, and whether the heat produced can escape. Besides holding a single die, some approaches combine several dies in one package. Later, we will look at an example where a calculating die and a memory die are connected close together.

If you want to learn more about cutting, connecting, and sealing: Read about the flow of the back-end process. You can also see where the wire bonding in the photo fits in the overall process.

One step further on terms: In general, the "front-end process" is where devices and wiring are made on the wafer. Within it, device formation is called FEOL and wiring formation is called BEOL. The "back end" in BEOL does not mean the same thing as the "back-end process" of assembly and packaging.

LEVEL 3 Gateway to expert topics / Precision and yield

06

Why do defects appear even when chips are made the same way?

Through scaling and yield, we look at why misalignment and variation become problems.

After reading: You can explain how scaling, yield, process conditions, and quality are related.

From here, we use "controlling electricity," "processing thin layers," and "measuring along the way" from the first half to think about the reasons behind the technology. Rather than memorizing new processes, focus on how a deviation in one process affects the next process and the finished product.

Why does a small deviation become a problem when things get smaller?

Making circuit dimensions smaller to fit more transistors into the same area is called scaling (miniaturization). But as wiring gets thinner, even a tiny chip-off can break it, and as the spacing between wires shrinks, the risk of them shorting together because of a processing deviation also rises. Besides the ability to transfer fine patterns, the ability to process those shapes correctly is needed.

Misalignment between one layer and the next leads to holes not lining up with the wiring below. This layer-to-layer misalignment is managed as overlay, and key line widths and hole sizes are managed as CD (critical dimension). The purpose of measuring these numbers is to check that the electrical paths in the design actually exist in the real chip.

As shapes get smaller, the current that flows when a transistor is in its "hard to flow" state also becomes a problem. Current that flows unintentionally is called leakage current, and it affects power use and heat. So shapes and materials are also improved, for example by making the part that controls current three-dimensional. Scaling is not just shrinking the same drawing.

If you make 100, can you use 100? — Yield

Yield, explained in the previous section, is the share of items in a given inspection or process that turn out good. As an example, if 90 out of 100 dies inspected meet the standard, the yield is 90%. Unless you match which process and what base number you use, the numbers cannot be compared directly.

90 good ÷ 100 inspected = 90%Even when you make the same 100, the result depends on how many are usable.

Suppose that, at the same manufacturing cost, good chips drop from 90 to 80. Then fewer good chips share that cost. Simply put, the manufacturing cost assigned to each good chip becomes 90 ÷ 80 = 1.125 times higher. This is an example of the idea. Actual costs also include design, inspection, assembly, and more.

If particles and process variation are at the same level, larger dies tend to have more chances of including a problem spot. However, the type of defect, the circuit design, and how much spare circuitry can make up for it also matter. It does not mean "large chips are always defective."

Increasing the number made is not the same as increasing the number usable. With yield in mind, you can also see why fabs care so much about "small abnormalities."

How do you find the conditions that produce good chips?

If deposition time or temperature changes, film thickness changes. If etching conditions change, hole depth and sidewall shape change. With CMP, dishing from over-polishing and leftover film also affect later processes. This kind of relationship between conditions and results is tracked using equipment logs and inspection and metrology data.

  1. Set the targetRequired film thickness and shape
  2. Make and measureVariation by location
  3. Narrow down the causeMaterials, conditions, equipment state
  4. Verify and feed backEffects and side effects of changes

For example, if you find that "the film is thicker only at the edge of the wafer," the clues to investigate are different from when just the average is off. You measure multiple locations and multiple wafers to see whether it is a random difference or a trend caused by the equipment's condition. It is important not to conclude something is the cause just because the data changed together, but to control the conditions and confirm.

The conditions that produce good chips have a range. This acceptable range is called the process window. If you only aim at one point in a narrow range, small changes in materials or equipment can easily push you outside it. The range in which you can make chips as intended and the measurements that catch when things start to drift are set up together.

It is similar to finding "the combination of temperature and time that browns food without burning it" in cooking. In semiconductors, though, many conditions are involved besides temperature, such as pressure and materials, and pass/fail is checked by dimensions and electrical characteristics rather than appearance.

If you want to know concretely how planarization conditions relate to defects: Read about the causes of over-polishing, leftover film, and variation in CMP. You can check everything from the purpose of polishing to how pads, slurry, and metrology are related.

Making precision and measuring precision go together
Looking at yield tells you not just how many were made but how many are usable. Even after you control shapes, materials, and conditions to get good chips, there is still something that holds back performance. That is the next topic: "waiting for data."

LEVEL 3 Gateway to expert topics / Calculation and data transfer

07

If calculation is fast, why does AI have to wait?

We go from how data is moved to how HBM, connections, and heat dissipation are related.

After reading: You can think about memory capacity separately from how data is moved.

With photo editing, we saw the flow of reading data from storage, placing it in a working area, and calculating. In AI too, huge amounts of numbers that the calculations are based on are delivered to the GPU. Even with calculating power to spare, the GPU cannot work fully if it is waiting for data.

If you make the warehouse bigger, does the wait for data go away?

How much data can be stored is capacity, how much can be moved per second is bandwidth, and the wait from request to arrival is latency. Think of a warehouse's storage space, the amount of goods it can ship out in a set time, and the time until an ordered package arrives. Thinking of these three separately helps you grasp the differences.

A bigger warehouse does not necessarily ship out more goods. But real data transfer is not decided only by the width of the loading dock either. The number of connections, transfer speed, and more are involved, and actual processing speed also depends on the amount of calculation, data reuse, and the amount transferred. No single number for a GPU or memory decides how fast AI is overall.

What changes when DRAM is stacked and connected close by? — HBM

HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) stacks DRAM, the working memory explained in the first half, to exchange large amounts of data at once. To connect the stacked dies vertically, it uses TSVs (through-silicon vias), electrical paths that pass through the silicon, among other things.

Picture placing a multi-level shelf near the worker and providing many hand-off windows. The similarity is the combination of stacking, placing close by, and handing over a lot at once. Stacking alone does not make it faster. In HBM, the connections within the stack and the many signal lines to the GPU and other chips are what matter.

In a typical setup, the GPU and HBM are placed on a relay layer with fine wiring called an interposer and connected within the same package. Compared with running long wires to distant parts, this makes it easier to fit many connections into a short distance. Speed is not decided by distance alone. The amount of data that can be moved at once and the design of the connections matter.

On the other hand, connecting stacked dies accurately, checking connection quality, and releasing heat all become harder. A defect after expensive parts have been combined also has a big impact, so checks on each part and after assembly are essential. This is where the packaging topic from the first half and the yield topic just before connect.

The GPU and HBM combination is a typical example for AI, but not every AI device uses HBM. The setup is chosen based on the performance, power, area, and cost needed. In tech news too, if you break the phrase "fast memory" down into capacity, bandwidth, connections, and heat dissipation, you can understand what was actually improved.

Who solves these hard problems?

Whose work goes into a smartphone chip?

The processing and quality control we have seen require different kinds of expertise. The semiconductor industry includes companies that design chips, companies that manufacture them under contract, companies that handle assembly and testing, and companies that supply materials and equipment.

A company that focuses on design and has no fabs of its own is called fabless. A company that manufactures chips from other companies' designs is called a foundry. A company that does both design and manufacturing in-house is called an IDM. In reality, some companies combine these with outsourcing. Even under the same term "semiconductor maker," it is important to look at what work each one actually does.

Without supplies of wafers, chemicals, photoresist, deposition equipment, lithography equipment, inspection equipment, and more, chipmakers cannot manufacture on their own. When reading company news, check not only "what chip it is" but also which process the company is in and what it supplies. This makes it easier to understand how the industry is connected.

If you want to find out which process a company you know handles: See the semiconductor company map by process. You can link company names to roles such as materials, equipment, and manufacturing.

The hole depth changed. What do you check first?

For example, suppose the fab finds that "the hole depth changed even though we used the same procedure as yesterday." The metrology staff pin down where the change happened, the process staff check conditions such as temperature and time, and the maintenance staff check the equipment's condition. Chasing one problem from different clues is what division of labor means. The "make, measure, feed back" cycle from earlier becomes the link between jobs.

Fabs have cleanrooms that keep particles from landing on the tiny circuits. Not only air but also water, chemicals, temperature, and more must be controlled. Even with automated transport and processing, people are needed to judge the condition of equipment, materials, and processes and to respond to problems.

Manufacturing operator
Checks the equipment's operating status and processing progress following set procedures, keeps records, and reports problems. This is the on-site role that keeps production running steadily.
Process engineer
Studies how process conditions such as temperature, time, and gas flow relate to film thickness, shape, and yield. Sets up conditions that consistently deliver "the intended result."
Equipment engineer, maintenance
Supports stable equipment operation through parts replacement, inspections, and investigating the causes of breakdowns. Even if process conditions are correct, results change when the equipment's condition changes, so they work with the process staff.
Inspection and metrology, quality and yield staff
Narrow down where problems are and what causes them from measurement data and defect distributions, and use that to improve manufacturing conditions and control methods. They not only find defects but also design ways to keep them from happening again.
Facilities and utilities staff
Support the fab's supply of power, air conditioning, ultrapure water (UPW), gases, and more. They maintain the environment the manufacturing equipment needs to run and help keep the whole fab operating steadily.
Field service engineer (FSE)
Usually works for the equipment maker and visits customers' fabs to install, adjust, and repair equipment. They work alongside the equipment staff employed at the fab, but their employer and how much they travel may differ.

Job titles and scope vary by company. Even when looking for an entry point with no semiconductor experience, do not decide by title alone. Check the training, the equipment you would handle, shift work and business trips, and the experience required, along with the job duties.

What materials should you choose to learn more?

Some people learn better by studying repeatedly in an organized order rather than memorizing terms one by one. Before buying materials, check (1) what you want to learn, (2) the background knowledge assumed, and (3) the learning format.

  • If your goal is the big picture: Choose an introductory book whose contents include "types, manufacturing processes, and division of labor among companies," and whose first few pages you can read comfortably.
  • If your goal is going deeper into technology: Narrow down whether you want to learn circuits, materials, or manufacturing. Use a free preview to check how much knowledge of math and electrical circuits it assumes.
  • If you are considering a course: Check the syllabus, whether exercises and Q&A support are included, study time, total cost, and viewing deadline. Also compare whether you just watch videos or solve problems yourself.

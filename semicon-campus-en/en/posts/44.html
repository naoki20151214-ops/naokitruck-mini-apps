Good question. If you jump straight into lithography or CMP, you'll get lost. First, it's important to have a big map: from sand and quartz to a wafer, to a chip, to a package, and into a product.

In this article, we look at the big picture of semiconductor manufacturing as "one continuous flow." Rather than memorizing detailed process names, the goal is to first grasp what each step is for and how it connects to the steps before and after it.

The big picture first: Semiconductors are not made all at once, but by stacking hundreds of steps

N
Naoki

I pictured semiconductors being made by pouring material into a mold and letting it harden. Is that wrong?

O
Professor Owl

Quite different. A semiconductor chip is made on a thin disk called a wafer. You make films, transfer patterns, etch, clean, change electrical properties, flatten, and stack wiring. You repeat this many times.

The big flow of semiconductor manufacturing
Raw materials → High-purity silicon → Single-crystal ingot → Wafer → Front-end process → Inspection → Back-end process → Package → Final product

Once you know this flow, you will be able to tell "where we are right now" later when you learn individual steps such as lithography, etching, deposition, cleaning, CMP, inspection, and packaging.

1. Raw materials: Everything starts with silicon

N
Naoki

So it really starts with sand?

O
Professor Owl

Roughly speaking, silicon is related to silicon dioxide, the main component of quartz and sand. But you can't use it as is. To use it in semiconductors, you need to raise its purity to an unbelievable level.

The most common semiconductor material is silicon. Silicon is abundant on Earth, but the silicon used in semiconductor manufacturing is completely different from everyday sand. Even a tiny amount of metal impurities can affect chip performance and yield, so extremely high purity is required.

2. Single-crystal ingot: Building a base where electrons can move cleanly

N
Naoki

Once it's high purity, does it become a chip?

O
Professor Owl

Not yet. First, the silicon is made into a single crystal. If the crystal arrangement is disordered, the movement of electrons tends to be disordered too. Semiconductors are a technology for controlling electrons, so the base crystal matters.

High-purity silicon is grown into a large cylindrical crystal called a single-crystal ingot. What matters here is that the silicon atoms are lined up in a clean, regular pattern. Because semiconductor manufacturing creates structures too small to see, the quality of the base crystal is extremely important.

3. Wafer: A blank canvas for making chips

N
Naoki

A wafer is that thing that looks like a round mirror, right?

O
Professor Owl

That's right. The ingot is sliced thin, the surface is polished, and it becomes the base for making chips. A wafer is like the canvas of semiconductor manufacturing.

The single-crystal ingot is cut into thin slices, and the surface is polished flat to make wafers. Transistors and wiring are built onto these wafers. If a wafer is not clean and flat, the fine processing that follows will not be stable.

4. Front-end process: Building the foundation for transistors and wiring on the wafer

N
Naoki

"Front-end process" sounds plain, but is this the main event?

O
Professor Owl

It really is the main event. In the front-end process, you build structures for transistors and wiring on the wafer. It has the most steps in semiconductor manufacturing and is also the most technically difficult part.

In the front-end process, fine structures are built into the wafer surface. Typical steps here include oxidation, deposition, lithography, etching, ion implantation, annealing, cleaning, and CMP.

  • Deposition: Makes thin films on the wafer
  • Lithography: Uses light to transfer circuit patterns
  • Etching: Removes unneeded parts to create shapes
  • Ion implantation: Adds impurities to change electrical properties
  • Cleaning: Removes particles and contamination
  • CMP: Flattens the surface
N
Naoki

The process names look scattered, but basically you're repeating "make a film," "transfer a pattern," "etch," "clean," and "flatten" over and over.

O
Professor Owl

That understanding is very important. Each step goes deep, but once you grasp the rhythm of repetition, you can see the whole picture.

5. Inspection and metrology: Measuring an invisible world

N
Naoki

So it's not over once it's made? They inspect along the way?

O
Professor Owl

Of course. Semiconductors are a nanoscale world, invisible to the human eye. So you have to keep measuring dimensions, film thickness, defects, misalignment, and more with equipment.

In semiconductor manufacturing, inspection and metrology are done many times during the process. Engineers check pattern line widths, film thickness, whether there are defects, and overlay misalignment, and then adjust manufacturing conditions. This is an important area that directly affects yield.

6. Back-end process: Turning chips on the wafer into usable parts

N
Naoki

Once the front-end process is done, can it be used as a GPU or CPU?

O
Professor Owl

Not yet. There are many chips lined up on the wafer. You need to cut them apart, connect them electrically, protect them, and put them in a form that can be built into products. That's the back-end process.

The back-end process includes dicing, which cuts the wafer into individual chips; bonding, which connects chips to a substrate; and packaging, which puts them in a form that can connect to the outside. In recent years, as AI chip performance has improved, advanced packaging has rapidly become more important.

7. In the AI era, "how you connect things after making them" matters too

N
Naoki

I thought only the front-end process was impressive for AI chips.

O
Professor Owl

The front-end process is certainly important. But in the AI era, how to connect GPUs, HBM, and chiplets closely and at high speed also matters. That's why the back-end process and advanced packaging are moving closer to the center of cutting-edge technology.

AI chips not only need to do huge amounts of computation at high speed, but also need to exchange large amounts of data with memory. That is why technologies such as HBM, 2.5D/3D packaging, and chiplets are important. To understand semiconductor manufacturing, you have to look all the way through to the back-end process, not just the front-end process.

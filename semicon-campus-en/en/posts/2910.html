After cleaning, you cannot tell by eye whether the wafer "really got clean." That is because the contamination is too small to see.

So fabs use special equipment to count or measure the contamination. This article explains how that equipment works, using everyday comparisons. Difficult terms are rephrased simply as they come up.

Yellow highlights = key points that appear on this course's tests

What you will learn in this article

  • Particles are counted by shining light on them
  • Metals are measured with X-rays or with a dissolved liquid sample
  • Organics are checked by analyzing the gas they give off

1. Particle measurement (surface defect inspection): how particles are counted with laser light

The most common tool for checking particles on a wafer is the optical particle counter. An article by an analysis company in the cleanroom trade journal "Cleanroom Technology" (May 2026) also calls it "the standard method."

How it works, with an everyday comparison

In a dark room, if you shine a flashlight from the side, you can see dust in the air sparkle, right? It is the same idea. A laser beam is shone on the wafer, and a sensor catches and counts the light scattered by particles. This tells you how many particles there are and where they are. (This is a general explanation of the principle.)

Why identify what a particle actually is?

Once a particle is found, its identity is checked with SEM-EDX (a tool that magnifies it with an electron microscope and, at the same time, finds out which elements it is made of). Knowing what it is lets you pinpoint the cause (equipment, chemicals, people, etc.) and eliminate it. Counting alone only tells you "many" or "few."

Evidence

Cleanroom Technology, "Cleanroom compatibility of wafers: Practical guidance on selecting the right analysis methods" (Kalle Niiranen, May 7, 2026; an article by a technical expert at the analysis company Measurlabs; only partially viewable).

Quick quiz: What is the standard tool for counting particles on a wafer?

Answer: The optical particle counter.

2. Evaluating metal contamination (TXRF and ICP-MS): shining X-rays and checking the light given off

Metal contamination is a problem even in tiny amounts, so highly sensitive methods are needed. The two main ones are as follows.

1. TXRF

Its full name is "total reflection X-ray fluorescence." When X-rays hit a material, each element gives off light of its own specific color (fluorescent X-rays). This is like the element's "fingerprint". By examining that light, you can tell which metals are present and how much. Because the X-rays hit the wafer at an angle that just skims the surface, only the surface contamination is seen clearly. Another strength is that it can measure without damaging the wafer (non-destructive).

2. VPD-ICP-MS

The wafer surface is thinly dissolved with chemical vapor and collected into a single droplet, and that droplet is analyzed by an instrument. Because it checks the contamination of the whole wafer at once, it is well suited to routine inspection. (This is a general explanation of the principle.)

Let's think in numbers

Step 1: What is "10 to the 10th power"?
It is 1 followed by ten zeros.
10 to the 10th power = 10,000,000,000 = 10 billion.

Step 2: What is being counted?
According to EAG's document, the smallest amount TXRF can find (the detection limit) is 10 to the 9th to 10 to the 10th atoms per square centimeter (1 billion to 10 billion atoms) for many metals. One square centimeter is about the size of a thumbnail.

Step 3: How small is that?
The surface of silicon has about 7 × 10 to the 14th atoms (about 700 trillion atoms) per square centimeter. (This is a rough figure calculated from the silicon crystal lattice constant of about 0.543 nanometers.)

If there are 10 billion contaminating metal atoms,
10,000,000,000 ÷ 700,000,000,000,000 ≈ 1 ÷ 70,000.
In other words, it is a tiny amount: about 1 metal atom for every 70,000 silicon atoms.

TXRF is a tool that can find even contamination this slight.

Evidence

EAG Laboratories, "The Total Solution For Surface Contamination Measurements." It states that TXRF has a detection limit of 10 to the 9th to 10 to the 10th atoms/cm², an analysis spot of about 10 millimeters, is non-destructive, and can measure entire 100–300 millimeter wafers. The Cleanroom Technology article describes VPD-ICP-MS as suited to routine inspection, with detection limits of 10 to the 6th to 10 to the 10th atoms/cm² depending on the element.

Quick quiz: What does TXRF measure?

Answer: The types and amounts of metal contamination on the wafer surface.

3. Evaluating organic contamination (thermal desorption gas analysis and GC-MS): heating and checking the gas given off

Organics are checked with a method called ATD-GC-MS, in which the wafer is heated and the gas given off is collected and analyzed (Cleanroom Technology article). It is a two-step method: the gas is separated into its components (GC), and then they are identified by mass (MS).

Why use different methods?

Because each type of contamination has different properties. For particles, you look at "number and size"; for metals, "type and amount of element"; for organics, "gas components." No single method can measure every kind of contamination. So methods are combined to fit the purpose.

Let's think in numbers

Light elements that TXRF struggles with (hydrogen to sulfur) are measured with a different method called SIMS (secondary ion mass spectrometry). According to EAG's document, its detection limit for many metals is 10 to the 8th to 10 to the 9th atoms (100 million to 1 billion atoms) per square centimeter.

Compared with TXRF's limit (1 billion to 10 billion atoms), SIMS's limit (100 million to 1 billion atoms) means it can find amounts one order of magnitude (about 10 times) smaller.

Key points of this lesson

Cleaning results are measured separately for each type of contamination. Particles: optical counter (counts bright spots) and SEM-EDX (identifies them). Metals: TXRF (X-ray fingerprint) and VPD-ICP-MS (dissolve, collect, and analyze). Organics: ATD-GC-MS (checks the gas given off). TXRF's detection limit for many metals is 10 to the 9th to 10 to the 10th atoms per square centimeter.

Quick quiz: After a particle is found, what method is used to identify it?

Answer: SEM-EDX.

Key terms review

  • Detection limit: The smallest amount a method can find.
  • Non-destructive: Not damaging the wafer during measurement.
  • TXRF: A method that shines X-rays and checks surface metals using the light given off.
  • VPD-ICP-MS: A method that thinly dissolves the surface, collects it, and analyzes the liquid.
  • SEM-EDX: A method that views a sample with an electron microscope and also identifies its elements.
  • ATD-GC-MS: A method that separates the gas given off by heating into its components and analyzes them.

Common misconceptions

Q. If the wafer looks clean, was the cleaning successful?

No. The contamination that matters is too small to see, so it is measured with special equipment.

Q. Can metals be measured the same way as particles?

No. Particles are "counted with light," while metals are checked with methods that identify elements, such as X-rays or dissolving and analyzing.

The September 15 announcement: 19 nodes running within a 16-node power budget

On September 15, 2026, NVIDIA said that Lambda had presented validation results for DSX MaxLPS using Blackwell-generation systems at the AI Infra Summit that same day. According to the company announcement, Lambda ran 19 nodes within the power budget normally allocated to 16 nodes, increasing total token throughput by 24%. It also reported a 23% improvement in performance per watt. This is a validation report by companies, and reproducibility by an independent third party has not been confirmed. The specific dates and duration of the experiment are also unknown. NVIDIA's September 15 announcement

A node here is a server unit that makes up a group of computers. A group of computers in which multiple nodes work together is called a cluster. Picture this comparison as running 19 machines within a power cap equal to 16 machines. The result comes from combining 3 extra nodes with power control, so it does not mean the computing speed of a single GPU rose by 24%. Lambda's official validation case study

In the official case study's inference comparison, using HGX B200, 16 nodes without control processed about 4.04 million tokens per second, and 19 nodes with power control processed about 5 million tokens per second. Inference is the process in which a trained AI produces answers or other output in response to input. These figures show the throughput of the whole cluster for a specific model and configuration. Validation equipment and comparison conditions

Why does the way power is "distributed" change throughput?

The background is the gap between the maximum power a facility can supply and the power GPUs actually need. According to NVIDIA's technical documents, GPUs do not always use maximum power during inference. With a fixed allocation per GPU, unused headroom may remain across the facility. Making use of that headroom is the goal of dynamic power allocation for AI servers. Technical overview of DSX MaxLPS

It helps to imagine distributing a shared budget, power, across multiple computers. Instead of reserving a fixed maximum for each computer, dynamic allocation adjusts the distribution based on actual usage. NVIDIA's Dynamic Power Software (DPS) changes the allocation to GPUs based on power measurements and other data, while keeping within the facility's power boundary and the equipment's limits. It is not a technology that increases generating capacity or incoming power capacity itself. How DPS power management works

Also, distributing power is different from distributing work. Deciding which computer gets which task is handled by a work-assignment function called a scheduler. DPS does not replace it. The significance of this report is that it shows concretely that when adding computers, not only chip performance but also how the existing power budget is used affects total throughput. The role and scope of power control

The 24% throughput gain and the 23% gain in performance per watt are different metrics

A token is a small unit that generative AI uses to handle text. "Tokens processed per second" here shows how much the group of computers processed in a given time. In road terms, it is closer to the number of cars passing along the whole road per second than to the speed of a single car. Even if total throughput rises, the wait time to answer a single question does not necessarily get shorter by the same percentage. Token throughput comparison

Performance per watt is an efficiency metric that looks at throughput relative to the power actually used. The distinction to make here is that a power budget is "the maximum that can be used," not actual average power consumption. Even if throughput rises within the same cap, performance per watt falls if actual power consumption rises by a larger percentage. So you cannot conclude that efficiency improved from just two facts: the same power budget and a 24% throughput gain. Official case study showing the power budget and performance

The 23% improvement needs to be treated as a figure NVIDIA reported separately from the 24% throughput increase. The unrounded values of both, the interval used to calculate average power, and the scope of what was measured are not fully shown. Also, a reported 23% gain in efficiency cannot be restated as a 23% cut in electricity bills or total energy use. From single-GPU settings to facility-wide design

As technical background, NVIDIA's overview document distinguishes MaxP, MaxQ, and MaxLPS. MaxP is the GPU's maximum power setting, and MaxQ is a static setting aimed at an application's performance per watt. Static means using a fixed setting, as opposed to a method that redistributes as conditions change. MaxLPS, in contrast, targets the throughput of the whole facility. Where MaxP, MaxQ, and MaxLPS fit

The scope of MaxLPS includes not only DPS but also facility design, including buildings and cooling, and efficiency improvements to the workloads being run. So you cannot conclude that installing the software will produce the same result at another facility. This case suggests that turning semiconductor performance into real service capacity requires thinking about power, cooling, and control software as well. Overview of MaxLPS including facility design

What has been demonstrated, and what we want to check next

What was announced this time is validation results in a Blackwell environment. It is not an announcement that deployment across all facilities or availability to general users has been completed. The official documents checked as of September 17, 2026, describe DPS as a Developer Preview and say it is not intended for production use outside authorized collaborations. The results of the demonstration and the product's maturity for broad production use need to be judged separately. DPS availability status

The comparison conditions also have limits. The official case study includes a separate test that mixes inference and training, but those numbers cannot be added to the 19-node inference comparison. There is also no confirmed basis for treating these power control results as official MLPerf rankings or as performance differences between GPU generations. The 24% figure cannot be considered an improvement rate that applies to every model or facility. Official case study describing the conditions of each test

Going forward, we want to see whether the effect holds with different combinations of models and workloads, whether response latency can be kept down, and whether power caps can be respected as load changes. If facility-wide power including cooling and the measurement intervals are stated clearly, and third-party comparisons are added, it will be easier to judge whether this helps at other facilities. The technical documents also emphasize evaluating throughput, latency, and compliance with the power budget together. Evaluation criteria for performance and power constraints

What is the front-end process?

The front-end process is the step where transistors, wiring, and more are built into the surface of a silicon wafer. The stage where finished chips are cut out and put into packages is called the back-end process. In contrast, the stage where the circuits themselves are built on the wafer is called the front-end process.

In a word, the front-end process "turns a wafer with no circuits into a city of electronic circuits." The starting wafer is just a round, polished base. On it, thin films are made, patterns are copied like a photo, unneeded parts are removed, the surface is washed, flattened, and measured, and then the process moves on to the next layer.

If you are from a non-technical background or new to semiconductors, this is enough to start.

The front-end process is "the process that builds circuits on a wafer." You can learn detailed equipment names and English abbreviations once you see the big picture.

The front-end process turns wafers into chips

Semiconductors start from silicon found in sand and quartz. From there, it goes to high-purity silicon, a single-crystal ingot, and then a silicon wafer. But when it becomes a wafer, there are still no circuits. Only in the front-end process does the foundation for transistors and wiring begin to be built on the wafer.

The basic loop of the front-end process

To understand the front-end process, you don't need to memorize every step name from the start. First, look at it as a rhythm: "wash, make films, copy patterns, remove material, flatten, and measure." In a semiconductor fab, this flow is repeated many times while changing the materials and purpose.

Key points of the diagram
  • Cleaning: reduce contamination
  • Deposition: make thin films
  • Lithography: copy patterns
  • Etching: remove unneeded film
  • CMP: flatten the surface
  • Inspection / metrology: check for misalignment and defects

This repetition is needed because semiconductor chips have three-dimensional structures. Making a pattern on a wafer just once is not enough to make complex chips like CPUs, GPUs, and AI chips. By stacking films, keeping only the needed areas, and stacking another layer again, circuits are built up little by little.

The main front-end steps in a word

CleaningReduces particles, metal contamination, and leftover chemicals on the wafer surface. It is the foundation for protecting yield.
DepositionMakes thin films with the needed properties, such as insulating films, metal films, and protective films.
LithographyUses light to copy circuit patterns onto the wafer. This is the step ASML is famous for.
EtchingRemoves unneeded film along the pattern made by lithography.
CMPSmooths out steps on the wafer surface to make a flat surface that helps the next steps go well.
Inspection / metrologyMeasures film thickness, line width, misalignment, and defects to check whether the process is on target.

How should you think about FEOL, MOL, and BEOL?

When you study semiconductors in a little more detail, you will see the terms FEOL, MOL, and BEOL. Beginners often get stuck here, but you don't need to memorize all of them from the start. First, understand the front-end process as "the whole process of building circuits on a wafer."

Then, looking more closely, the part that makes transistors is called FEOL, the connections between transistors and wiring are called MOL, and the wiring layers stacked on top are called BEOL. The way these terms are used can differ a little between articles and company materials, so it is important to understand them from context.

A common beginner mistake: BEOL is not the "back-end process."

BEOL stands for Back-End of Line, so from the name alone it looks the same as the "back-end process." But in semiconductor manufacturing, BEOL is the step that builds metal wiring on the wafer, and it is treated as the latter part of the front-end process.

  • BEOL: The step that builds wiring layers on the wafer. Part of the front-end side.
  • Back-end process: The steps that cut out chips from the wafer, put them into packages, and move on to final testing.

If you see terms like "BEOL process," "interconnect process," "Cu wiring," or "low-k film" in a job posting, think of it as work close to wiring technology on the wafer, not assembly work. This will help you avoid misreading it.

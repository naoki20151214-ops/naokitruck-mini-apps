Say you bake 100 cookies, and 92 of them come out unburned and good enough to sell in a shop. Those 92 cookies show the idea of "yield." It is the share of usable items out of everything you made.

Semiconductor fabs use the same kind of number. It is the share of good chips among all the chips made on a wafer (the round disk that semiconductors are made on). The higher this number, the more good chips you get from the same materials and effort. To give you the answer first: yield goes up by repeating improvements that "find the cause of defects and fix it."

The cookie example only fits one point: "the share of good items." Cookies and semiconductors fail for completely different reasons.

There are two types of yield loss

According to lecture notes from the University of California, Berkeley, yield loss in a wafer fab is divided into two types: "line yield" and "die yield." A die is each individual chip cut from the wafer.

Line yield loss comes from careless wafer handling or process mistakes. Examples include skipping a step, mixing up procedure documents, or equipment going out of control. Die yield, on the other hand, is the share of individual chips that work. The notes call the probability that a chip works the die yield.

The main thing that breaks chips: tiny "defects"

Much of the chip-level loss is caused by very small defects. According to the notes, a defect means any physical abnormality that causes a circuit to fail. Specific examples include particles (dust), scratches, shorts where wires connect when they should not, and opens where wires are broken.

A tool used here is the "wafer map." It marks the chips that failed an electrical test on a diagram of the wafer. If the defect locations are clustered, that gives a clue to the cause.

Key points of the figure

  • Chips that did not work are marked on a diagram
  • Clusters of marks give clues to the cause
  • You can see where defects are at a glance

However, not all loss comes from defects. The notes give an example: at the edge of a wafer, film thickness is hard to control, so chips at the edge can all fail.

The larger the chip, the lower the yield

Even in the same fab with the same number of defects, yield changes with chip size. That is because the larger the chip, the more likely it is to contain at least one defect. The notes also explain that with the same process and the same defect density, a wafer of large chips has lower yield than a wafer of small chips.

So products with larger chips need fabs with fewer defects.

This also shows one more important point. Comparing the yields of products with different chip sizes directly is not a fair comparison. So the notes introduce a way to work backward from the yield figure to the fab's "defect density," that is, how many defects there are per square centimeter. The idea is that a fab with lower defect density can produce at higher yield.

Key points of the figure

  • The number of defects is the same
  • The larger the chip, the more likely it hits a defect
  • So the larger the chip, the lower the yield

Defects can be thought of as adding up step by step

A wafer goes through many process steps before it becomes chips. The notes introduce a way to think of defect density as a sum across process steps and layers. With this approach, the overall yield is the product of the yields of each step.

For example, say three steps have yields of 98%, 95%, and 99%. Multiplying them gives an overall yield of about 92%. That is the same number as in the cookie example. You can see that even slightly improving the lowest step, at 95%, has a big effect on the whole. (This is a calculation for explanation.)

This is useful because it clearly separates which steps lower the yield and by how much. You can also calculate how much the overall yield would rise if you reduced defects in a given step. You can decide the order of fixes by the numbers.

Key points of the figure

  • Compare losses step by step
  • Find the step with the largest loss
  • Fixing the largest one first has the biggest effect

The core of raising yield: "yield learning"

So how do fabs actually raise yield? The industry's technology roadmap (ITRS) presents a concept called yield learning. It is the effort to raise yield by gathering knowledge about the process and the wafers, using it well, and finding and solving manufacturing problems.

The roadmap describes yield improvement work as raising the baseline yield of a technology generation from its research-and-development level to a "mature yield." The yield here is the result of chip-level testing (wafer sort).

Find, solve, and find again. This repetition is what yield learning is all about. The roadmap lists challenges such as detecting ever-smaller defects and quickly pinpointing root causes as data volumes grow.

Two types of causes; the clear-cut ones get solved first

Causes that lower yield fall into two broad groups: "systematic" causes with a clear mechanism, and "random" causes that are closer to chance. According to the inspection equipment maker KLA, most systematic causes are solved as yield ramps up.

So what is left at the end? Mainly random defects caused by process equipment and the environment. In other words, you first fix the big causes, and at the end you deal with small variations.

Key points of the figure

  • First, clear-cut (systematic) causes are solved
  • Yield rises over time
  • Random defects are what remain at the end

"Tool monitoring": getting a clear verdict for each tool

It is surprisingly hard to tell where a defect was created. KLA notes that defects are sometimes found many steps away from where they came from. That is where tool monitoring comes in.

The method is very simple. First, inspect a bare wafer. Next, run that wafer through the tool you want to check. Finally, inspect it again. Any defects added between the two inspections were added by that tool. There is no doubt, and the cause becomes clear.

KLA also says that when this is combined with proper control limits and an out-of-control action plan (OCAP), the sources of random yield loss can be identified and addressed tool by tool. Finding a problem and taking action go together as a set.

Key points of the figure

  • Inspect to record the current state
  • Run it through the tool and inspect again
  • Added defects came from that tool

Key terms review

  • Yield: The share of good items among everything made.
  • Line yield: The share not lost to wafer handling or process mistakes.
  • Die yield: The share of individual chips that work.
  • Defect: A small physical abnormality that breaks a circuit.
  • Wafer map: A diagram of the wafer with the chips that did not work marked on it.
  • Yield learning: A repeated learning cycle of finding causes and solving them.
  • Systematic and random causes: Causes with a clear mechanism, and causes closer to chance.
  • Tool monitoring: A method of finding defects added by a tool by inspecting, running through the tool, and inspecting again.

Common misconceptions

Q. Is low yield only caused by having many defects?

No. Defects are a major cause, but not the only one. Chips can also fail for reasons other than defects, as with losses at the wafer edge.

Q. Is 100% yield the goal?

The goal depends on the application. According to KLA, consumer-product fabs treat the point where further improvement is no longer worth the effort and cost as "mature yield." For applications with high reliability requirements, such as car parts, they aim even higher.

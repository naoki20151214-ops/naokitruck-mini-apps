When you get interested in the semiconductor industry and decide to sign up with a career service or recruiting agent, many people get stuck on "What should I talk about?" In this article, we lay out what people new to semiconductors should prepare before an interview, in a format you can read on your phone and take notes from as you go.

Bring your experience closer to the words used in semiconductor job postingsConnect manufacturing, quality, maintenance, chemistry, data, and more to equipment, materials, inspection, and process improvement.

The bottom line: prepare just 5 things before an interview

An interview with a recruiting agent is not a test. You don't need to memorize everything about semiconductors before you go. To start, short notes on the following 5 things are enough.

1. Your experience so far

Write down experience such as manufacturing, inspection, maintenance, quality, chemistry, mechanical, electrical, or data analysis.

You don't need to polish it like a resume. Bullet points are fine to start.
2. Entry points you're interested in

Choose one or two directions that interest you, such as equipment, materials, inspection, manufacturing, process improvement, or FSE.

If you're unsure, choosing while looking at the job keywords article makes it easier.
3. Work style conditions

Sort out your preferences for work location, shift work, night shift, business travel, relocation, and annual salary.

Work styles vary a lot in semiconductor jobs, so sharing these early helps reduce mismatches.
4. Your concerns

Note things like whether it's possible with no experience, whether there's training, or that you don't understand the jargon.

Turning concerns into questions is more useful in an interview than hiding them.
5. Questions to ask in the interview

Be ready to ask about job content, training, assignment, work schedule, and future career.

Use it as a place to gather information for decisions, not just to sign up.

Translate your experience into semiconductor job terms

Job postings in the semiconductor industry use words that look hard to beginners. But just rephrasing your experience a little can reveal where it connects with a job.

You worked in a factoryIn semiconductors, this may be close to manufacturing operator, equipment operation, cleanroom work, or inspection.
You looked after equipment or machinesThis may be close to equipment maintenance, maintenance, tool installation, or field service engineer.
You worked in quality or inspectionThis may be close to quality assurance, reliability evaluation, measurement, analysis, or defect inspection.
You're interested in chemistry or materialsThis may be close to chemicals, gases, resist, CMP slurry, or materials evaluation.
You like improving things with dataThis may be close to yield improvement, process improvement, or quality data analysis.

Questions to ask in the interview

In an interview, the more appealing a job you're introduced to seems, the more carefully you should check what the work actually involves. If you judge only by company name or salary, the work style after joining may differ from what you imagined.

Job contentIs it equipment operation, maintenance, inspection, or quality? Check what the daily work is.
Assigned processAsk which process the job is close to: front-end, back-end, cleaning, CMP, inspection, and so on.
TrainingCheck for training for people with no experience, OJT, certification support, and support after assignment.
Work styleCheck for shift work, night shift, business travel, relocation, and cleanroom work.
Experience that leads somewhereAsk whether there's a chance to expand from manufacturing into maintenance, quality, equipment, or process improvement.

Split the interview into before, during, and after

In a job search, you don't need to decide everything in one interview. If you split what to do into before the consultation, during the interview, and after the interview, you can move forward much more calmly.

How not to approach an interview

  • Saying "anything is fine as long as it's semiconductors"
  • Looking only at "no experience needed" without checking the job content
  • Moving forward with the work schedule and location still vague
  • Deciding on the spot without looking closely at the jobs you're introduced to
  • Not keeping comparison notes after the interview

Note
This article does not recommend signing up for or applying through any specific career service. Job availability, selection criteria, pay and benefits, and referral availability vary by company, region, timing, and experience. Always check each service's or company's official information, the job posting, and what was discussed in the interview before applying.

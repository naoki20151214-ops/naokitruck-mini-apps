If you make your phone's background black, will the battery last longer?
The answer depends on whether the screen is OLED or LCD, and on how much of the screen is dark.
On OLED, it can save power under the right conditions. On LCD, the effect is often small. Screen brightness and app content also matter a lot.

The short answer
On OLED, each pixel makes its own light, so dark pixels can use less light-emitting current. A typical LCD lights the screen with a backlight behind it and makes black by blocking that light with liquid crystal. So switching to a black background does little to cut the backlight's power.
However, a phone also uses power for the SoC, wireless communication, touch circuits, and more. So the difference in the panel does not translate directly into longer battery life.

Dark mode is a feature that darkens the screen's color scheme

Dark mode switches a screen that mainly uses a light background and dark text to a color scheme that mainly uses a dark background and light text. You can use it when your phone's settings, apps, or websites support it. However, a "dark" design does not always use pure black (RGB 0,0,0) for the whole background. Some designs use dark gray.

The key point here is that turning dark mode ON does not by itself decide how much light the screen gives off. Even if the text background turns dark, the area of photos, videos, ads, and icons may stay the same. If an app does not support the OS theme, part of the screen may stay bright.

OLED pixels make their own light, so dark content changes power use

OLED (organic light-emitting diode) is a self-emissive display. Instead of lighting the whole screen from behind like an LCD, light-emitting elements in the red, green, and blue subpixels each give off their own light. A subpixel is a small unit that splits a pixel by color.

In the organic emitting layer of an OLED, electrons and holes coming in from the electrodes meet, and this produces light. The pixel circuit controls the current sent to each subpixel, and the current is closely tied to how bright the light is. So when showing black or dark colors, the emitting current can be stopped or reduced. The larger the dark area, the easier it is to cut the dynamic panel power used for light emission.

Even with OLED black, the whole display does not drop to zero power

"Black pixels stop emitting light" and "the display module uses no power" are two different things. Each pixel has TFTs and capacitors that hold display data and control current. Scanning circuits that refresh the screen, row and column driver ICs, power conversion circuits, and touch detection circuits also keep running.

So while pure black makes it easier to cut the power of the light-emitting elements, it does not remove the power of the surrounding circuits. Looking at the whole phone, the SoC (the chip that does the computing) and wireless communication are also running. What dark mode mainly reduces is the part of the display power that fits these conditions.

LCD lights the screen with a backlight and makes black with liquid crystal

In a typical LCD, the liquid crystal itself does not give off light. An LED backlight behind the screen makes the light, and the liquid crystal and polarizers adjust how much of that light gets through. Bright areas let light through, and black areas let less light through. That is how the screen creates light and dark.

So if you keep the backlight brightness the same and change a white background to a black one, the main power used by the backlight basically does not change much. Even behind areas that look black, the light source itself often stays on. This is the core reason dark mode works differently on OLED and LCD.

Some LCDs are an exception and dim the backlight based on content

We also cannot say "there is no difference at all on LCD." Some products have content-adaptive backlight control, which lowers the backlight based on what is on screen and compensates on the liquid crystal side. Other designs use local dimming, which splits the backlight into several zones instead of one for the whole screen and dims the zones behind dark areas.

However, local dimming is usually controlled per backlight zone, not per pixel. Even when a dark UI is shown, the result depends on the zone size and the control method. In other words, LCD power savings do not come from "black liquid crystal pixels turning themselves off." They depend on whether backlight control actually kicks in.

The first factor in power savings is screen brightness

On OLED, even with the same dark mode, the gap with light mode tends to grow as screen brightness goes up. When a bright background is emitting strongly, there is more emitting current to cut when you switch to a dark color scheme. On the other hand, if brightness is already low, light mode's emitting power is also small, so switching tends to make less difference.

As one example, a 2021 study from Purdue University tested 4 phone models and 6 apps. It reported that dark mode cut device power by an average of 3–9% at 30–50% brightness, and by an average of 39–47% at 100% brightness. However, these results come from specific devices, specific apps, and specific measurement conditions. They are not numbers that apply directly to everyday use of every smartphone.

A 2026 study using a Galaxy S23 Ultra found that the dark theme saved an average of only 1.4% across 4 apps, while changing the brightness setting from 0% to 100% increased average energy use by 86.5%. This too is a software-based estimate on a single model, not a universal savings rate. The two studies look different because the devices, apps, brightness, and measurement methods differ.

Why "a black screen always saves a lot of power" is not true

First, the dark part of dark mode is not always the whole screen. If a social media feed or web page has many photos, the photos themselves may stay bright. In video apps, only the UI may turn dark, while the processing and display of the video itself stay the same. In the 2026 study, the effect also differed between text-focused apps and video/media-focused apps.

Second, the phone's total power includes items other than the screen. Even if you cut part of the display power, the power for communication and SoC processing stays the same if that work stays the same. It is risky to treat the display's power reduction rate and the improvement in battery life as the same number.

Third, pure black and dark gray are different. On OLED, dark gray can often cut emitting current compared with white, but it may not suppress emission as much as pure black. Dark gray is sometimes chosen for readability, appearance, or app design.

Key points so far
If you want to try dark mode, first check whether your phone is OLED or LCD, then adjust brightness to a comfortable level. Look not only at the theme but also at the backgrounds of the apps you use most and how much photo and video they show. That makes the results easier to judge.

Refresh rate and SoC load are separate power factors

Refresh rate is how many times per second the screen is redrawn. Dark mode mainly changes display colors and pixel brightness. It does not automatically lower the refresh rate. Thinking "the background is black, so the screen refreshes less often" mixes up two different mechanisms.

Showing animations or scrolling at a high refresh rate tends to increase the load on the display circuits and the SoC. Variable control, which raises the refresh rate only when needed, is a power-saving technology separate from dark mode. In apps heavy on video, games, or network traffic, much of the device's work remains even if you change the theme.

To check the effect, match the screen type and conditions

When you compare light mode and dark mode on your own device, it is important not to draw conclusions from a short-term change in battery percentage alone. If auto-brightness is on, the screen conditions change partway through to match the surrounding light. Even at the same slider position, the actual screen brightness may differ on a different device or in a different environment.

To compare, keep the same device, the same app, the same content, the same measured brightness, the same refresh rate, and the same network conditions as much as possible. Also decide in advance whether to include photos and videos or only text screens. If you compare OLED and LCD under different conditions, you cannot tell whether the result comes from the theme or from differences in screen type or brightness.

Even with matched conditions, study results and your own impressions are "results for that device and that usage." They cannot be generalized directly to other models, other OSes, or other apps. Rather than searching for a universal savings rate, it is more practical to check how dark the screens you look at most actually get.

Five common misconceptions, sorted out

Q1. Does dark mode save power on every smartphone?

A. No. On OLED, dark pixels can use less emitting current, but on a typical LCD the effect is often small. There are also exceptions with backlight control.

Q2. Do black OLED pixels use exactly zero power?

A. Even if the current to the light-emitting element is stopped or reduced, the TFTs, scanning circuits, driver ICs, touch, and power conversion keep running. It does not mean the whole panel or the whole phone drops to zero.

Q3. Will OLED always make the battery last much longer?

A. No. It depends on screen brightness, dark area, photos and video, apps, refresh rate, and SoC and network load. Studies include conditions with an average of just 1.4%, as well as conditions that vary greatly with brightness.

Q4. On LCD, is the power difference from display colors exactly zero?

A. No. With a fixed backlight the difference tends to be small, but content-adaptive backlight control or local dimming may control the backlight based on what is on screen.

Q5. Does dark mode also lower the refresh rate?

A. No. Dark mode mainly changes color and pixel brightness, while refresh rate mainly changes how often the screen is redrawn. They are separate controls.

When you use a smartphone, it sometimes slows down, gets hot, or drains its battery fast. There are many causes, and one of them lies between the wires inside the semiconductor chip.

Inside a chip, thin metal wires are packed closely side by side. Between the wires is a material that does not conduct electricity, called an "insulating film" (dielectric). In fact, the chip's speed and energy savings are supported by a trick: making this insulating film deliberately "airy." This article explains why, step by step.

There are many other reasons a smartphone slows down or gets hot. This article covers only the part related to the wiring inside the chip.

The real problem: slow signals traveling along wires (RC delay)

Inside a chip, transistors are connected to each other by thin metal wires. Transistors do the calculations, and the results are sent through the wires. Lecture notes from the University of California, Santa Barbara explain that a major factor in chip performance has become the time it takes for signals to travel along the wires. Whether a chip can run fast depends heavily not only on the transistors but also on the wiring.

What's more, as chips get smaller and faster, the wires get thinner and closer together. An EE Times Japan article also says that the further miniaturization goes, the more important low-dielectric-constant materials between the wires become.

Quick quiz: Is chip speed determined only by the transistors?

Answer: No. It also depends heavily on how fast signals travel along the wires.

"Wiring capacitance": charge that builds up between wires

When wires get close to each other, a little charge builds up between them. A component that stores charge is called a "capacitor," and two nearby wires act like a small capacitor with an insulating film in between. The amount of stored charge is called "wiring capacitance." According to the lecture notes, a capacitor's capacitance gets larger when the facing area is larger, when the distance is shorter, and when the material between them stores charge more easily.

When wiring capacitance increases, signals take longer to travel. Also, according to the EE Times Japan article, power consumption goes up. High wiring capacitance means signals take longer to travel and extra power is used. That leads to problems like battery drain and heat.

Key points of the figure

  • Wires get thinner and closer
  • Wiring capacitance increases
  • Signals slow down and extra power is used
Quick quiz: When wires get closer, what increases?

Answer: The amount of charge stored between the wires, in other words, the wiring capacitance.

Insulating films and the "dielectric constant"

To reduce wiring capacitance, you could space the wires farther apart, but that is not possible if you want to make chips smaller. Instead, the "insulating film" between the wires is changed to a material that does not store charge easily. In the capacitor formula in the lecture notes, capacitance is proportional to the "dielectric constant" of the material in between. The dielectric constant is a number that shows how easily a material stores charge. The smaller it is, the less charge the material stores.

Another approach is to change the wire metal from aluminum to copper. According to the lecture notes, the resistivity of aluminum is 2.65 and that of copper is 1.67 (in μΩ·cm), so resistance drops by less than half. That is why the notes say it is important to reduce both resistance and capacitance.

Key points of the figure

  • Starting from glass, the number goes down step by step
  • Fluorine, carbon, and pores are added one after another
  • The smaller the number, the less charge is stored
Quick quiz: Does a material with a low dielectric constant store charge easily or not?

Answer: It does not store charge easily. It can reduce wiring capacitance.

From glass to low-k materials

According to an IBM paper, the insulating film was originally silicon dioxide (a type of glass). It is an easy material to work with: resistant to heat and chemicals, hard, a good barrier to moisture, and easy to form clean films with. Its drawback was a fairly high dielectric constant.

So "low-k materials" with lower dielectric constants came into use. First, glass mixed with fluorine, which lowered the dielectric constant to about 3.5, was used at the 130-nanometer generation. Next came carbon-doped material (called SiCOH), with a dielectric constant of about 2.7 to 3.0 without pores. Adding carbon lowers the material's density, which also lowers the dielectric constant.

Quick quiz: The first low-k approach was to mix what into glass?

Answer: Fluorine. The dielectric constant dropped to about 3.5.

Lowering the dielectric constant with airy "porous low-k"

To go even lower, tiny pores are made in the material, turning it "porous" and full of gaps. The IBM paper says that adding pores brings the dielectric constant to 2.2 or below. Porous materials are used at the 45-nanometer generation and beyond.

EE Times Japan describes one way to make the pores. ULVAC's material is a solution of the film's base ingredients mixed with a surfactant. When it is baked, the surfactant acts as a template that leaves pores, producing a glass full of gaps. The more pores there are, the less charge the material stores. The required dielectric constant gets lower with each generation. According to the EE Times Japan article, 2.2–2.4 is needed at the 32-nanometer generation, 2.0–2.2 at the 22-nanometer generation, and 2.0 or below beyond that.

Key points of the figure

  • Many tiny holes are open
  • The holes reduce how much charge is stored
  • In exchange, it tends to become fragile
Quick quiz: Why do porous materials have a low dielectric constant?

Answer: Because the more tiny pores (gaps) there are, the less charge the material stores.

Fragility and moisture: weaknesses and fixes

However, a film full of gaps has weaknesses. According to the IBM paper, its elastic modulus (a measure of stiffness) is much lower than glass and it is fragile, so steps are needed to keep the film from peeling during copper polishing. Examples include placing dummy metal, reducing polishing pressure, and improving adhesion between films. Only the last one or two layers use glass, for strength.

The other weakness is moisture. Moisture spreads quickly through porous films, so a cap film is formed on top of the copper, and a wall is built around the edge of the chip to keep moisture out. Poor heat dissipation is another weakness. According to EE Times Japan, there was also a problem where the plasma processing used to form the wiring raised the dielectric constant by about 0.5, so materials that resist this damage were developed.

Key points of the figure

  • Fragile: polish gently
  • Weak against moisture: a cap and a wall around the edge
  • Each weakness has a fix
Quick quiz: What are the weaknesses of a film full of gaps?

Answer: It is fragile, and moisture spreads through it easily.

"Air gaps" that use real air

A more advanced method is the "air gap," which creates real gaps of air between wires. According to the IBM paper, if air gaps are limited to only the critical areas, the strength and heat dissipation of other areas can be kept. An example is shown in which the dielectric constant was lowered to 2.0 in a 65-nanometer processor. However, it adds process steps to create the gaps, and it costs more.

Key points of the figure

  • Gaps of air between the wires
  • Air does not store charge easily
  • Process steps and costs go up
Quick quiz: What does the air gap method create between wires?

Answer: Real gaps of air.

Key terms review

  • Wiring capacitance: The amount of charge stored between nearby wires.
  • Insulating film: A material that does not conduct electricity, placed between wires.
  • Dielectric constant: A number that shows how easily a material stores charge. The smaller it is, the less charge the material stores.
  • Low-k material: An insulating film material with a low dielectric constant.
  • Porous: A state full of gaps, with many tiny holes.
  • Air gap: A real gap of air created between wires.

Common misconceptions

Q. Is SiCOH the same as silicon carbide (SiC)?

No. SiCOH is an insulating film made by mixing carbon and other elements into glass (silicon dioxide). It is different from silicon carbide.

Q. Is a higher dielectric constant better?

For the insulating film between wires, lower is better. It stores less charge and can reduce wiring capacitance.

Summary

As wires get thinner and closer, the wiring capacitance (charge stored between wires) increases, signals take longer to travel, and extra power is used.
To fix this, the insulating film has been changed to low-k materials with lower dielectric constants, and then made porous and full of gaps.
Fragility and moisture need countermeasures, and a more advanced method is the air gap, which uses real air.

In semiconductor manufacturing, finished chips are not checked just once at the very end. While films are formed on the wafer, patterns are created, and steps such as etching and cleaning are repeated, the wafer's condition is checked many times along the way. That is because the later a problem is noticed, the more likely it is that the next wafers or lots will already have been processed under the same conditions.

This is where the terms Inspection and Metrology come in. Both are technologies for protecting quality, but their goals are not the same. Inspection looks for "where is something wrong?", and metrology turns things like dimensions and film thickness into numbers. In a semiconductor fab, these two are combined with defect review and process control.

First, defects are not just "dirt"

When you hear "defect" in semiconductors, you may picture particles stuck to a wafer. Particles and contamination are indeed important targets. But that is not all. Scratches on the surface, residue, missing pattern parts, bridges where lines connect to each other, changes in the shape of holes or lines, and film abnormalities can also affect processes and product performance.

In other words, defects are not limited to "things that look dirty." They also include shapes that do not match the design, positions shifted from the target, and films outside the standard. On top of that, normal circuit patterns themselves create complex reflection and scattering, so simply looking for bright spots is not enough.

Inspection looks for "where" problems are

The core job of inspection is to scan a large area of the wafer and narrow down places that produce unusual signals as defect candidates. The output includes candidate coordinates, signals, possible defect types, and their distribution on the wafer. Targets are not only surface particles and scratches. On patterned wafers, the tool may detect places that break from the normal repetition by comparing with neighboring dies, identical cells, or design data.

A person does not look at the whole wafer one pixel at a time under a microscope. Instead, the tool scans a wide area quickly and outputs candidates. This is the basic idea of inspection in mass production. Optical inspection shines light on the wafer and detects changes in reflection and scattering, making it suited to checking large areas at high throughput. E-beam (electron beam) inspection is suited to looking at finer areas with high spatial resolution.

It is also not accurate to think "anything small can never be seen with light." The limit for resolving two structures as separate in an image is not the same as the limit for detecting that a small abnormality changed the scattering signal. That said, not everything is found automatically. Defect size and shape, material differences, background patterns, noise, algorithms, and more all affect the results.

Why candidates are not immediately judged as defects

Even when inspection flags candidates, not all of them are fatal defects for the product. They may include normal pattern variation, tiny surface changes, or signals the tool picked up that have nothing to do with yield. So the candidate coordinates are passed to a separate high-resolution tool for a closer look.

This step is Defect Review. Tools such as a Review SEM move to the coordinates from inspection and capture magnified images of those spots. Is it a particle, residue, a scratch, or a pattern bridge? The tool classifies what each candidate really is and, if needed, looks for clues to the cause. Rather than having people check thousands or tens of thousands of candidates at the same magnification, automatic review and sampling are used to narrow down the places worth looking at.

It is important not to confuse inspection and review here. Inspection is the step that "finds suspicious places," and review is the step that "examines those places closely and decides what they are." Both are technologies for looking at defects, but they answer different questions.

Metrology turns conditions into "numbers"

Metrology measures conditions on the wafer and converts them into numbers that can be used for process decisions. A typical example is CD, meaning the width of lines or the size of holes. Other measurements include Overlay (the misalignment between one layer and the next), thin-film thickness, shape, angle, and material condition.

A CD-SEM is a dedicated system for measuring the dimensions of fine patterns. While a Review SEM looks closely at what a defect candidate is, a CD-SEM answers questions like "How many nanometers wide is this line?" and "How far is this hole's size from the target?" In practice, the same e-beam tool sometimes handles both defect detection and dimensional measurement. So rather than splitting tools strictly by name, it is more accurate to think in terms of their main purpose and output.

Overlay metrology measures how far patterns on different layers are misaligned. Film thickness metrology checks whether a deposited film has the target thickness. These measurements are not just for finding one problem spot. They are data for tracking distribution within a wafer, trends between lots, differences between tools, and changes over time, so you can judge whether the process is stable.

The difference between inspection and metrology in a nutshell

Inspection answers "Where is something wrong?" Metrology answers "How far off is it?" Defect Review answers "What is the thing we found?" And Root Cause Analysis investigates "Why did it happen?"

For example, suppose optical inspection finds a candidate abnormality on a wafer and outputs its coordinates. A Review SEM might look at that spot at high magnification and classify it as pattern deformation rather than a particle. Next, CD metrology turns the dimensions into numbers, and these are compared with overlay and film thickness results and with the history of the processing tool, chamber, recipe, and lot. In this way, engineers narrow down whether the abnormality is linked to specific process conditions.

Measuring is not the end: feed results back to the process

The value of inspection and metrology is not just collecting data. By linking results to SPC, run-to-run control, APC, recipe adjustments, and equipment maintenance, fabs prevent the same problem from increasing on the next wafers processed.

Of course, more inspection and metrology is not automatically better. Inspection costs time, equipment money, data processing, and review effort. With e-beam, measurement speed, charging, and effects from the beam also need to be considered. Measured values have uncertainty too, and they cannot directly guarantee every location that was not sampled.

The difficulty in mass production is not competing for the record in finding the smallest defect. It is about not missing the defects that matter, not picking up too many irrelevant signals, checking enough area at enough speed, and returning meaningful numbers to the process. To do this, optical inspection, e-beam, Review SEM, CD-SEM, overlay, film thickness metrology, sampling, calibration, and process control are combined into one system.

You cannot understand this approach by looking only at the inspection room. You look on the same timeline at which processes the wafer went through, which tools processed it, which lot it belongs to, and when abnormal signals increased. For example, even if a defect is concentrated at the wafer edge, that alone is not enough to pin the cause on one tool. Other factors, such as fluid conditions, wafer transfer, film formation, or carryover from earlier steps, can create the same distribution. That is why inspection images, review results, dimensions, film thickness, overlay, and tool history are combined to narrow down possible causes step by step.

Also, even the same number may not be directly comparable if it was measured differently. Engineers prepare numbers usable in the process while checking calibration, reference wafers, tool-to-tool matching, and measurement uncertainty. Having many digits is not the same as being close to the true value. Metrology is not about producing precise-looking readouts; it is about understanding how reliable a value is and then using it for manufacturing decisions. This is why the candidate search of inspection and the numbers of metrology are considered together in process control.

Summary: inspection is the eye, metrology is the ruler

Finding semiconductor defects is not a one-time check of finished products at the end. Wafers in the middle of manufacturing are checked broadly, closely, and repeatedly.

  • Inspection searches for the locations of candidate abnormalities and outputs coordinates and defect maps.
  • Defect Review looks at candidates at high magnification and classifies them as particles, scratches, pattern defects, and so on.
  • Metrology turns CD, overlay, film thickness, shape, and more into numbers.
  • The results are fed back into process conditions, tool adjustment, maintenance, and yield improvement.

In short, inspection is "the eye that looks for abnormalities," and metrology is "the ruler that turns deviation into numbers." However, one tool may handle multiple functions, so you need to look not just at tool names but at what question is being asked and what output is obtained.

Inspection and metrology in a semiconductor fab are not just a system for sorting out bad products at the end of manufacturing. They are the eyes and rulers of the production line, finding wafer abnormalities as early as possible, narrowing down causes, and feeding the results back into the next process conditions.

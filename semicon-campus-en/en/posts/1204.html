The Role of the Mass Flow Controller: Keeping Gas at the Same Amount Every Time

In semiconductor deposition and etching, the invisible flow of gas shapes the results on the wafer.
Reading only the displayed flow rate will not tell you what happened partway through a short process step.
The answer is the mass flow controller, or MFC, which runs a gas-measuring sensor and a control valve in a closed loop.

The main subject of this article is themass flow controller (MFC), which is installed partway along a gas line. An MFC is not a circuit inside a finished chip. It is a measurement and control device on the process equipment that performs deposition and etching on wafers. Rather than seeing it as a faucet that "just lets gas out," we will look at it as a device that adjusts a valve while comparing the setpoint with the measured value, to reproduce the conditions going into the process.

Why gas flow rate is a process condition

In deposition, several precursor gases are sent into the chamber at set ratios and react on the surface to form a film. In etching, the combination of gases matters: reactive species that remove material, species that protect the sidewalls, and gases involved in dilution or physical bombardment can affect the etched shape and etch rate. Gas flow rate is less a number on the recipe screen and more the entry point for the reactions on the wafer surface.

An MFC does more than protect the displayed value. The gas ratios created by several MFCs, the total flow, the chamber pressure, the concentration of reactive species, and the conditions for removing byproducts all act on the wafer at the same time. Changes in flow can affect the results, but there is no universal formula that converts MFC error into a change in film thickness or dimensions. That is because the equipment, materials, pressure, power, step time, and chamber condition together decide the result.

Keeping this limit in mind helps you avoid the mistake of thinking, "If the MFC display matches, the process is the same." The MFC is an important part that supports process conditions, but it does not decide deposition or etching results on its own.

What happens inside an MFC

In a typical thermal MFC, part of the flowing gas is split into a thin sensor path, and the rest passes through a bypass path. The sensor path has a heater and temperature sensors, and it reads changes in the heat carried by the flow as a signal. That signal is checked against the gas properties, the gas setting, and calibration data, and treated as a flow rate.

Do not take the word "mass" to mean that it weighs the gas directly like a scale. This method measures heat transfer and estimates flow through gas properties such as heat capacity and a calibration model. Temperature and pressure change the state of the gas, and a different gas type transfers heat differently. The same sensor output cannot always be read as the same flow rate for every gas.

Besides thermal MFCs, there are also pressure-based MFCs that use pressure sensors and known flow path characteristics. It is not that thermal types are always better and pressure types always worse. The method is chosen to fit the equipment's conditions, such as the target gas, pressure range, required flow range, response characteristics, and calibration method.

Bringing the flow back to the setpoint with a closed loop

Looking at the MFC as a control flow makes its role clear. The equipment inputs a flow setpoint, the sensor measures the actual flow, and the difference from the setpoint is sent back to the control circuit. If the flow is too low, the valve is adjusted to open; if it is too high, it is adjusted to close. A system like this, which feeds the measurement back into the next action, is calledclosed-loop control.

A closed loop is a way to stay close to the setpoint even when upstream pressure fluctuates or piping conditions change. But the flow does not reach the ideal value the moment the valve moves. Sensor delay, valve response, piping volume, inlet pressure, and the state of the downstream chamber and exhaust all add up. You need to look at the surrounding equipment on the same timeline, not just the MFC screen.

Look at the waveform along the way, not just the final value

Say that after a recipe change, the flow finally settles at 100 sccm. Even so, the total amount of gas that entered the chamber during a short step may differ depending on whether the flow started at 50, jumped up sharply once, and came back, or approached smoothly. Even if the final displayed value is the same, the time waveform the process experienced is not always the same.

So when evaluating an MFC, steady-state accuracy and transient characteristics are separated. Dead time is the time from changing the input until the response starts. Step response is the response to an input change. Settling time is the time to settle within a set range. Overshoot is a temporary move past the target. Repeatability and zero offset also matter when reading results under repeated identical conditions. SEMI E17 and NIST documents cover transient characteristics because a single number cannot describe a waveform.

Also check inlet pressure fluctuation, ambient temperature, warm-up, contamination or condensation, and the sequence of the upstream and downstream shutoff valves. On some equipment, the timing of opening and closing those valves can affect overshoot. This does not mean a guaranteed value shared across manufacturers; it is an area where you check the waveform under actual equipment conditions.

How this connects to deposition and etching

In deposition, the supply ratio of precursors relates to the film's growth rate, composition, thickness, stress, and surface condition. In etching, the gas ratio changes the balance between reactive species and sidewall protection, which can affect the shape and etch rate. Even when research reports that the SF6-to-O2 ratio, or the total flow under other etching conditions, affected the results, you must read that together with conditions such as the material, chamber, pressure, and power.

So you cannot say, "The MFC display drifted a little, so the film thickness will drift by the same ratio." An MFC is a device that prepares the input for reproducing gas conditions. The impact on results is judged using process evaluation data, chamber condition, the ratio with other gases, and step length. Not rushing to reduce the numbers to a single cause-and-effect line matters for both beginners and equipment staff.

How far to isolate the cause in equipment maintenance

When the flow does not match the setpoint, replacing the MFC right away can lead you to the wrong cause. First, check whether the gas type specified by the equipment, the gas correction, the zero point, and the calibration conditions are correct. Next, check the inlet and outlet pressures, regulators, the shared supply system, upstream and downstream valves, piping, and connections. Pressure-based and thermal types rely on different assumptions, so do not compare them without checking the method.

Even when the steady-state value matches, the rise may become slower or the overshoot may grow. In short steps, recording only the final value can miss a problem. Overlay the flow trend, pressure, temperature, valve operation, alarms, and recipe switch times to see when the waveform started to change.

Downstream of the MFC are the mixing and distribution system, the process chamber, pressure control, and the exhaust system. If the chamber pressure or exhaust condition changes, looking at the MFC alone will not give you the full picture. Treat a Flow NG not as the name of an MFC failure, but as a problem occurring in the control system from the gas supply through the chamber to the exhaust. Specific replacement steps and safe operation fall under the equipment's official procedures.

Common misunderstandings

Q. Does an MFC weigh the mass of gas directly, like a scale?

A. A typical thermal MFC measures changes in the heat carried by the gas flow and estimates the flow rate through gas properties and calibration. Thermal is not the only method, and you cannot explain every MFC with one principle.

Q. If the displayed flow setpoint matches, will the process results always be the same?

A. No. Transient response, pressure, gas ratio, chamber, exhaust, materials, and more all play a part. Even if the final value is the same, a different waveform during a short step may change the gas conditions that went in.

Q. If there is a Flow NG, should you just replace the MFC?

A. First, isolate the cause on the same timeline across the gas setting, calibration, inlet pressure, upstream and downstream valves, piping, chamber, and exhaust. To narrow the cause down to the MFC itself, you need information about the whole system.

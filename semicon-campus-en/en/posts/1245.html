On September 24, 2026, SEALSQ announced that it will expand commercial deployment of its secure element "VaultIC408," building on its FIPS 140-3 Level 3 validation from NIST (the US National Institute of Standards and Technology). The important point is that this does not mean a new certification was obtained on September 24. According to NIST's official certificate, VaultIC408 1.2.4 was first validated on August 5, 2026, as Certificate No. 5463, and it is now listed as Active, Overall Level 3, Hardware, SingleChip. We need to read SEALSQ's announcement and NIST's certificate separately.

What happened: commercial deployment after validation

VaultIC408 is a "secure element," a semiconductor chip with built-in functions for cryptographic keys and device authentication. For example, when a factory sensor or communication device connects to the cloud, it handles the secret keys used to prove that the device is genuine and to encrypt communications. The basic idea is to protect secret keys with dedicated hardware instead of keeping them only in ordinary software areas.

NIST's Certificate No. 5463 covers a specific cryptographic module: version 1.2.4 of VaultIC408. The validation date is August 5, 2026, and the Sunset Date listed on the certificate page is August 4, 2031. So SEALSQ's September 24 announcement can be seen as a plan to use an existing validation result for sales and design wins in government, defense, critical infrastructure, industrial IoT, and other areas. No customer contracts, order values, shipment volumes, or completed deployments were announced. NIST Certificate No. 5463

How to understand FIPS 140-3 and Level 3

FIPS 140-3 is a US Federal Information Processing Standard for evaluating the security of "cryptographic modules" that perform cryptographic processing. It does not just check whether the cryptographic calculations are correct. It also evaluates the module's specification, its external interfaces, user authentication, physical protection, management of secret parameters, self-tests, and product life cycle. It defines four levels according to how strict the security is. FIPS 140-3 replaced the earlier FIPS 140-2 standard. NIST's FIPS 140-3 overview

"Level 3" here is not a grade saying that a whole product or a whole factory is secure. It means the cryptographic module was validated as meeting Level 3 requirements under defined design, configuration, and operating conditions. In validation, an accredited testing lab runs the tests, and the CMVP (Cryptographic Module Validation Program) reviews the report and issues the certificate. In other words, it goes beyond the maker's own claims and passes through defined third-party testing and program review. NIST CMVP description

What cryptographic functions have been confirmed?

NIST's certificate lists approved algorithms for VaultIC408 1.2.4, including AES, RSA, ECDSA, SHA-2, HMAC, key agreement using elliptic curve cryptography, key derivation functions, and Counter DRBG.

  • AES: A symmetric-key cipher that turns data into an unreadable form. It is used, for example, when a device and a server protect their communication with the same secret key.
  • RSA and ECDSA: Typical examples of public-key cryptography. RSA is used for encryption and signatures, and ECDSA for digital signatures. They help confirm that a device holds the legitimate secret key.
  • Hash functions and HMAC: Used to detect data tampering and for message authentication with a secret key. They let you check whether data from the other party was changed along the way.
  • DRBG: A mechanism that generates random numbers for cryptographic use. Hard-to-predict random numbers are important for key generation.

This list shows that VaultIC408 can use conventional cryptographic functions in its approved configuration. However, a list of algorithm names alone does not guarantee the security of the system it is built into or of the whole network.

Why does this matter for critical infrastructure and industrial IoT?

In critical infrastructure and industrial IoT, many devices are installed in the field, and device spoofing, counterfeit parts, eavesdropping on communications, and firmware tampering become problems. A secure element protects device-specific secret keys and credentials and can serve as the foundation for a system where only genuine devices can connect to a service. Especially in government procurement and regulated industries, certification of cryptographic modules may be used as a procurement requirement or evaluation factor. NIST CMVP description

So an Active FIPS 140-3 Level 3 certificate gives customers and system integrators a factor for narrowing down candidate parts. SEALSQ also describes expansion into government, defense, critical infrastructure, industrial IoT, secure manufacturing, and identity for connected devices. However, this is the company's sales policy and business plan. Actual customer adoption, orders, revenue growth, or deployment in critical infrastructure cannot be confirmed from this announcement or the certificate alone.

The link to post-quantum cryptography, and cautions

The announcement also describes the link to hardware security in the post-quantum era and to the company's future product portfolio. Post-quantum cryptography is cryptographic technology meant to prepare for the possibility that future powerful quantum computers could break the public-key cryptography widely used today.

However, this FIPS 140-3 validation of VaultIC408 does not mean post-quantum algorithms were certified. What the NIST certificate shows are approved conventional cryptographic functions such as AES, RSA, ECDSA, and SHA-2. SEALSQ describing future post-quantum products and development strategy is a separate matter from VaultIC408 itself being certified for post-quantum cryptography. The limits of certification, and what to watch next

The NIST certificate assumes operation in approved mode, following the version, configuration, and Security Policy listed on the certificate. The certificate also notes that no minimum security is assured for secret information loaded from outside. In other words, adopting a certified chip does not automatically make a system secure. Initialization, key loading, firmware, access rights, update procedures, and the network it is built into must all be designed properly. NIST Certificate No. 5463

Going forward, the first thing to check is whether actual customer names, orders, shipments, or adoption in mass production are announced. Second, it is important whether products using the chip keep to the certified version and approved configuration. Third, for successor products that include post-quantum algorithms, we need to see which algorithms are validated and under what certification scope. What we can confirm now is that a certified single-chip cryptographic module has gained a foundation for commercial deployment. This news does not show market expansion or completed post-quantum support.

Have you ever taken photos with a tiny speck of dust on your camera lens? The same black spot shows up in every photo you take.

Something similar can happen in a semiconductor fab. If even one speck of dust lands on a "photomask," the master pattern for a circuit, the same defect can be copied onto the chips made from it. A thin, transparent film called a "pellicle" prevents this. In this article, we explain the roles of the photomask and the pellicle, step by step.

The lens dust example only fits in one way: "dirt on the master shows up in every copy." Cameras and semiconductor fabs work in completely different ways.

One speck of dust can ruin a chip

A Semiconductor Engineering article (2016) explains that if dust or other particles land on a photomask, the lithography tool copies that defect onto the wafer. An Applied Materials engineer is quoted as saying that for logic chips, a single defect can make the whole chip unusable. Dust on the master leads directly to chip defects.

Check quiz: What goes wrong if dust lands on a photomask?

Answer: The defect can be copied onto the wafer, and chips can become unusable.

The circuit master: the "photomask"

A photomask is the master plate on which a semiconductor circuit pattern is drawn. TU Wien's explanation of masks describes it as a transparent plate called a "blank," with a pattern drawn in a light-blocking film. The plate is made of glass such as quartz. Quartz stays transparent even to short-wavelength light and expands very little with heat. The light-blocking film is very thin chrome, about 100 nanometers thick, with an anti-reflective layer on the surface. High-quality photomasks must meet strict requirements, such as flatness, accurate pattern placement, minimum line width, line width control, and very few defects.

Check quiz: What is used for a photomask's light-blocking film?

Answer: Very thin chrome. It is about 100 nanometers thick.

Photomask defects are copied onto the wafer

A MONOist article reporting on an announcement by Mitsui Chemicals explains that the process of shining light on a semiconductor wafer to draw circuit patterns is the photolithography process. In that process, the photomask is the "template" for copying the same circuit onto many wafers. So if the mask has a defect, that defect is copied as well.

TU Wien's explanation gives examples of mask defects: missing chrome, small pinholes in the chrome, and areas of chrome that were not resolved. Defects larger than about 0.1 micrometers are found by automated inspection and repaired with lasers or ion beams. It also cites an observation that defects were found on up to 75% of masks made with electron beams.

The dust shield: the "pellicle"

A pellicle is a thin, transparent dust-shield film placed over a photomask. A Semiconductor Engineering article explains that in the manufacturing flow, lithography tools tend to produce particles and contamination, and a pellicle keeps them from falling onto the mask. At the same time, it must be transparent enough to let light pass from the tool to the mask. The article also explains that memory chips have spare circuits, so their situation differs from that of logic chips.

According to the MONOist article, a pellicle is a dust cover whose film material is chosen for light resistance at each wavelength, with a film thickness designed to transmit light well. It keeps the photomask clean and improves semiconductor productivity. Mitsui Chemicals launched its pellicle in 1984. A pellicle is a cover for the mask that lets light through but keeps dust out.

Key points of the figure

  • A transparent film placed over the mask
  • Lets light through, keeps dust out
  • Keeps the mask clean
Check quiz: What is a pellicle for?

Answer: To keep dust and other particles from falling onto the photomask.

Standard masks and EUV masks

According to the Semiconductor Engineering article, pellicles have been used for decades on standard masks that let light through. They are made of thin resin. With cutting-edge "EUV" lithography, however, things change. The MONOist article describes EUV as extreme ultraviolet light with a wavelength of 13.5 nanometers. An EUV mask is not a plate that lets light through. It is a mirror made of 40 to 50 alternating layers of silicon and molybdenum.

Key points of the figure

  • A standard mask is a plate that lets light through
  • An EUV mask is a mirror that reflects light
  • A special structure of stacked layers

For EUV, there was once a view that pellicles would not be needed if the inside of the tool could be kept perfectly clean. But chipmakers changed their minds, saying they could not keep the inside of the tool 100% clean. An Applied Materials engineer says that having a pellicle also makes handling and storing masks much easier.

An EUV pellicle must transmit light well while withstanding intense light, which makes it a difficult part to develop. The 2016 article listed the very high temperature the film reaches as a challenge. In 2021, Mitsui Chemicals began commercial production of EUV pellicles under license from ASML of the Netherlands.

Check quiz: Is an EUV mask a plate that lets light through, or a mirror?

Answer: A mirror. It is made of 40 to 50 layers of silicon and molybdenum.

Next-generation pellicles: carbon nanotubes

According to the MONOist article, next-generation EUV lithography tools require a high numerical aperture and high power of 600 watts or more, which calls for pellicles made of new materials that can withstand harsh exposure. EUV lithography is being adopted more widely to make ultra-fine semiconductors with circuit line widths of 7 nanometers or less. In 2024, Mitsui Chemicals announced that it would install production equipment for carbon nanotube (thin tubes made of carbon) films that transmit 92% or more of EUV light and withstand exposure power above 1 kilowatt. The equipment can produce 5,000 units per year and was scheduled for completion in December 2025. Current EUV pellicle films use silicon-based materials, and carbon nanotube products are a next-generation addition to the lineup.

Key points of the figure

  • Transmits 92% or more of light
  • Withstands intense light
  • Material: carbon nanotubes

Review of key terms

  • Photomask: The master plate on which a semiconductor circuit pattern is drawn.
  • Blank: The transparent plate part of a photomask.
  • Pellicle: A thin, transparent dust-shield film placed over a photomask.
  • EUV: Extreme ultraviolet light with a wavelength of 13.5 nanometers.
  • Carbon nanotube: A thin, tube-shaped material made of carbon.

Common misunderstandings

Q. Is the pellicle the light-blocking film on a photomask?

No. The light-blocking film is made of chrome or similar material and forms the circuit pattern. The pellicle is a transparent dust-shield film placed over the mask.

Q. Is an EUV mask also a plate that lets light through?

No. An EUV mask is a mirror that reflects light, made of stacked layers of silicon and molybdenum.

Summary

A photomask is the master for a circuit. If it has dust or scratches, those defects are copied onto the wafer.
A pellicle is a thin, transparent dust-shield film placed over a photomask. It lets light through but keeps dust out.
For cutting-edge EUV, development is moving forward on carbon nanotube films that transmit light well and withstand intense light.

What was announced?

On September 16, 2026, Infineon Technologies and Winbond Electronics announced an agreement for Winbond to acquire Infineon's NOR flash and F-RAM business. Infineon's Japanese page is dated September 17, but the original announcement date shown in the text is September 16. The key point is that what was announced is the signing of a sale agreement, not the completion of the sale. The business mainly serves the automotive, industrial, and infrastructure markets. You can check this in Infineon's original announcement and the Japanese version.

The announced deal value is US$1.12 billion, all in cash. Winbond says it signed a stock purchase agreement with Infineon Technologies LLC and will acquire 100% of the business. However, US$1.12 billion is the base purchase price, subject to adjustments set out in the agreement, so the final payment has not been fixed. Winbond's board of directors has approved the deal. Winbond's announcement sets out the deal terms and plans.

The two companies are targeting completion in the second half of 2027. However, closing conditions such as required regulatory approvals must be met. After completion, Winbond plans to name the acquired company Spansion and run it as an independent business headquartered in the United States. These are future plans, and they do not mean the name change or business transfer has been completed.

What is NOR flash?

Non-volatile memory is memory that keeps its stored data even when the power is turned off. NOR flash is one type, and it is well suited to random access, meaning it can read any needed address fairly freely. It is used to store programs that start up devices and code that controls equipment, with typical uses in automotive ECUs, industrial controllers, and communications infrastructure. Infineon's NOR flash technology page explains its properties and uses.

Another key term is XIP (Execute in Place). Instead of copying a stored program entirely into separate working memory before running it, XIP runs the code directly from NOR flash in a supported configuration. It is easy to understand if you think of it as where an automotive electronic control unit stores the control program it reads at startup. NOR flash is not just long-term storage; it is a part that supports functions close to device startup and control.

How does F-RAM differ from NOR?

F-RAM (ferroelectric RAM) is also non-volatile memory, but it records 0s and 1s using the polarization state of a ferroelectric material. Infineon describes its strengths as fast writes and suitability for repeated recording. That makes it useful for storing data updated many times during operation, such as operating logs in industrial equipment, event data in automotive equipment, and status records in medical devices. You can check how it works and its uses in Infineon's F-RAM technology page.

Put simply for beginners, NOR flash's typical role is "storing the code that runs a device," and F-RAM's is "recording while the device is running." However, this is an example to help grasp the difference, not a strict definition of their uses. Speed, capacity, data retention conditions, and write endurance differ by product, so real designs require checking each spec.

Why does this deal matter?

This deal matters as a plan to move a lineup of embedded non-volatile memory products used in automotive, industrial, and infrastructure equipment, along with development resources and customer base, to Winbond. Infineon says the sale will let it focus capital allocation on key growth areas. Winbond, meanwhile, expects to expand its R&D resources, supply capacity, and customer service. These are goals and outlooks presented by the two companies, and the benefits of integration have not yet been proven. The announcements from Infineon and Winbond explain the aims of the seller and the buyer, respectively.

Also, Infineon is not leaving the memory business entirely. The company says it will keep SRAM, HYPERRAM, nvSRAM, and SONOS-based radiation-hardened memory. So this deal should be seen not as "Infineon giving up all its memory products," but as the sale of a specific business centered on NOR flash and F-RAM.

It can be called a move to consolidate an embedded non-volatile memory business into one company, but this deal alone is not grounds to conclude that the overall memory market has become more concentrated, that prices will rise, or that supply shortages will be resolved. The announcement includes no independent evidence showing market share, supply volume by product, or quantitative effects on prices or lead times.

What is still unknown and points to watch

For now, it is not clear what the transfer covers product by product, how fabs, equipment, and intellectual property will be handled in detail, what happens to existing long-term supply agreements, or whether customers will need to requalify parts. The stronger supply capacity Winbond expects also does not mean that fab expansion or higher output has been decided. How the actual supply system changes will depend on the operating plan after completion and further announcements.

Going forward, it is important to check the following points separately:

  • Whether the required regulatory approvals can be obtained in each country and region
  • Whether the deal closes on the target of the second half of 2027
  • What the final deal value will be after price adjustments
  • How product supply, development plans, and customer support will be handed over
  • Whether the plan for the Spansion name and running it as an independent business is carried out

The solid fact we can confirm is that a sale agreement was announced on September 16, 2026. Completion of the production transfer, increased supply capacity, and effects on market prices are things to verify as results going forward. Tracking announced plans separately from events that have actually been completed is the basic way to read large deals by semiconductor companies.

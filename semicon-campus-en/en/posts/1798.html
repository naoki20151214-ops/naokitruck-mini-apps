On October 1, 2026, an international research group including The University of Tokyo announced that it had grown "BCN (boron carbon nitride)," a single-atomic-layer material made of boron, carbon, and nitrogen, at the 2-inch wafer scale, and demonstrated transistors that work as a p-type 2D semiconductor. The research paper was published online in Nature on September 30, 2026. "Demonstrated" here means the performance was confirmed in research devices, not that mass production or commercialization has been completed.The University of Tokyo announcement, Nature paper

What was achieved

BCN is a material made by incorporating carbon into the single-atomic-layer lattice of hexagonal boron nitride (hBN), which is an insulator. The research group used monomethylamine borane (MMAB) and ammonia borane (AB) as source materials, and grew single-layer BCN epitaxially by low-pressure CVD on copper (111), a crystal face of copper.

Epitaxial growth is a method that uses the crystal structure of the underlying layer as a guide to line up the atoms of the material on top in a regular pattern as it grows. Based on atomic-resolution observations and other data, the group reports that carbon atoms and carbon pairs are taken into the BN lattice, mainly replacing nitrogen sites. The key technical point is that the group tuned the decomposition and surface reaction pathways of MMAB and AB, encouraging carbon to enter the BN lattice while preventing it from clumping too much on the copper surface.Nature paper

The band gap of BCN was measured at about 1.90 eV by scanning tunneling spectroscopy, consistent with about 1.92 eV from first-principles calculations. The band gap is the energy difference electrons need to move into a state where they can carry electricity. It is generally important for understanding how well current can be controlled, but actual transistor performance also depends on crystal quality, defects, contact with electrodes, and more.Nature paper

How to read the transistor performance

The research team made FETs by combining BCN with a 20 nm thick HfO2 gate insulator and nickel-gold electrodes. A FET is a basic transistor that controls the current in a semiconductor by applying voltage to a gate electrode. These devices showed p-type operation at room temperature, with a field-effect hole mobility of about 100 cm²/Vs, an on-current of about 0.9 mA/μm, an on/off current ratio of about 10 to the 8th power, and a threshold voltage of about −0.45 V.The University of Tokyo announcement, Nature paper

Hole mobility is a measure of how easily "holes," which behave like positive charges, move through a material. Higher values tend to make it easier for current to flow under the same conditions. The on/off ratio shows the difference between the state where current flows and the state where it is stopped, and about 10 to the 8th power indicates strong current control as a switch. However, because device size, electrodes, insulator, and measurement conditions differ, these numbers alone cannot determine whether it is better or worse than existing materials or commercial transistors.

In addition, the team evaluated 224 devices from three growth batches at different positions on a 2-inch wafer. Within the range evaluated, the results support consistent characteristics between devices and between batches. This is an important step toward larger areas, but it does not guarantee results at every position on every wafer, yield on mass production lines, or long-term stability.The University of Tokyo announcement

Why p-type 2D semiconductors matter

2D semiconductors are expected to make current easier to control with the gate field because they are nearly as thin as an atomic layer, and to work well with stacking technology that layers different materials. In particular, complementary circuits called CMOS need not only n-type transistors, which mainly carry electrons, but also p-type transistors, which mainly carry holes.

However, with 2D materials, the challenges have been making stable, high-performance p-type materials over large areas, reducing contact resistance with electrodes, and controlling the interface between the material and the insulator. This BCN widens the range of candidate materials because it showed p-type operation by introducing carbon at the atomic level while using the regular lattice of hBN as a base.

hBN is a layered material made of boron and nitrogen, and it is a chemically and thermally stable electrical insulator. It is also important as a substrate and protective layer for forming 2D materials such as graphene. This BCN can be understood as a material design that uses hBN's atomic arrangement while changing its electrical properties through carbon substitution.Explainer by the National Institute for Materials Science (NIMS)

Challenges toward mass production and 3D integration

These results do not show that mass production or commercialization of BCN has been completed. The main growth was done on copper (111), and the BCN film was transferred onto HfO2 when making the FETs. Whether the process, including growth, transfer, and electrode formation, can be run stably as a large-area process in a semiconductor fab needs to be verified separately.

Also, the evaluation of 224 devices shows uniformity across the three selected growth batches and evaluation positions. It is not data that guarantees yield or defect rates across the entire area. The relationships among the amount of carbon substitution, hole concentration, band structure, contact resistance, and operating lifetime also need further study.

The research group points to the possibility of extending BCN to complementary circuits combined with n-type 2D semiconductors, deposition on 3D substrates, and 3D integration. However, this is a future outlook, and it does not mean practical complementary circuits, mass production, or use in products have been demonstrated. Going forward, attention will focus on how reproducibly carbon placement can be controlled, whether the process including transfer can be applied to mass production, and whether long-term reliability and circuit operation can be confirmed.Nature paper

Summary

The essence of this news is not only that single-atomic-layer BCN was made, but that it was operated as a p-type 2D semiconductor and its uniformity was evaluated across 224 devices on a 2-inch wafer. It is a strong research result for the challenge of making p-type materials over large areas, but mass production and 3D circuits are still ahead. It is important to read the published performance figures separately from the future plans the research group describes.

For readers who are starting to understand yield, defect inspection, SPC, film thickness measurement, and overlay metrology. Instead of formulas, this article explains how a fab uses data to find out "why the good-chip rate went down."

Yield analysis is the job of narrowing down possible causes of defects when the good-chip rate drops in a semiconductor fab. It does this by overlaying defect maps, measurements, process history, equipment data, and more. The goal is not just to see "how many defects there were," but to track "where, since when, and under what conditions they increased," and then feed that back into process improvement.

What is yield analysis?

Yield is the percentage of chips made that are good enough to use. When yield drops, you get fewer sellable chips even with the same wafers, equipment, and materials. In other words, yield is a number tied directly to a fab's profits.

However, when yield drops, just saying "defects went up" doesn't help the fab improve anything. What matters is finding out why they went up.

In yield analysis, you compare where defects appear, when they occur, which tools the wafers passed through, changes in measurements, material lots, operating conditions, and more. Instead of naming a culprit right away, you overlay multiple pieces of evidence and gradually cut down the list of suspicious causes.

If you're reading on a phone, this part is enough to start. Yield analysis is "the job of finding out why the good-chip rate dropped by overlaying data." It is where inspection, quality, process, and data analysis meet.

A defect map shows how defects are spread out

One common view in yield analysis is the defect map, or wafer map. It shows, like a map, where on the wafer defects are concentrated.

Even with the same defect rate, the causes you suspect change depending on whether defects are scattered randomly, concentrated at the edge, clustered in the center, or appear in streaks.

More at the edgeSuspect drying at the wafer edge, chemicals wrapping around the edge, wafer transfer, or process conditions near the edge.
More in the centerYou may look at uneven process conditions, such as film thickness, temperature, plasma distribution, or rotation conditions.
Appearing in streaksSuspect causes with a direction, such as scratches during transfer, flow inside the tool, or the effect of a specific part.

Yield analysis is about overlaying data

A defect map alone doesn't settle the cause. You can see a trend in location, but you need other data to know which process step caused it, which tool it increased on, and when it started.

So yield analysis overlays multiple types of data. It connects defect inspection results, CD-SEM line widths, film thickness measurements, overlay metrology, SPC control charts, equipment logs, material lots, process history, and more.

For example, say defects increased in one lot. The defect map shows more at the edge. The film thickness data shows the film is slightly thinner only at the edge. The equipment history shows that this lot went through a specific deposition tool. When these line up, you can form a hypothesis that the deposition conditions or the tool's condition may be the problem.

Two major approaches to yield analysis

In real semiconductor fabs, engineers use even more detailed data to track causes. Two things beginners should know first are bitmap correlation and FDC.

Bitmap correlationOverlays the locations that failed electrical testing with physical positions on the wafer. The idea is to compare memory failure patterns and test results with the coordinates from defect inspection.
FDCShort for Fault Detection and Classification. It looks at sensor data from the tool, such as temperature, pressure, gas flow, and processing time, to find unusual equipment conditions.

For example, electrical testing shows that only certain chips failed. Defect inspection finds a pattern abnormality near them. And FDC shows that the tool's pressure was slightly off only when that lot was processed. When this information lines up, it's easier to treat the issue not as a coincidence but as a process or equipment abnormality.

The important thing here is not memorizing difficult system names. In yield analysis, what matters is the mindset of connecting electrical results, physical defects, and equipment condition.

In simple terms
Yield analysis is a bit like finding the culprit in a mystery. But it doesn't run on gut feeling. You overlay evidence such as defect locations, measurements, equipment history, and material lots to find "the most suspicious condition."

Sort possible causes into process, equipment, and materials

The key in yield analysis is not to jump to a conclusion about the cause. In a semiconductor fab, the same defect could have many causes: process, equipment, materials, environment, human work, and more.

So first, you sort the possible causes. "Did it increase after lithography?" "Did it increase after cleaning?" "Is it higher only on Tool A?" "Is there a difference between material lots?" "Did it change after maintenance?" You compare data from angles like these.

AngleWhat to checkRelated jobs
ProcessAfter which process step defects increasedProcess engineer, quality, yield improvement
EquipmentDifferences by specific tool, chamber, or after maintenanceEquipment engineer, FSE, maintenance
MaterialsLot differences in chemicals, gases, wafers, resist, or slurryMaterials, quality assurance, process
DataTrends in SPC, measurements, defect classification, and test resultsData analysis, yield analysis, quality data analysis

How it differs from yield improvement, failure analysis, and SPC

Yield analysis often appears alongside similar terms. Beginners easily get confused here, so let's sort them out roughly.

TermMain meaningRelation to yield analysis
Yield improvementAll activities to raise the good-chip rateYield analysis comes before improvement and looks for possible causes
Failure analysisDigging into the cause of individual defects through observation and analysisSuspicious defects found in yield analysis may be passed on to failure analysis
SPCFinding process abnormalities early from changes in measurementsShows early signs of yield loss and when changes started
Process data analysisComparing tools, conditions, lots, and measurementsThe core way of looking at data in yield analysis

In other words, yield analysis is "the job of narrowing down possible causes with data." You notice a change with SPC, see the location with defect inspection, dig deeper with failure analysis, and feed it back into process improvement. Yield analysis sits within this flow.

Connecting yield analysis, equipment logs, and FDC to real work and relearning

To link equipment logs with defect data, the starting point is lining up when, on which tool, and in which lot a change occurred. Experience matching maintenance records against defect maps and measurements, and separating equipment causes from candidates that can't be confirmed yet, connects you to process improvement. You can compare two paths: learning the basics of quality control and statistics, or moving into a technical job where you can use your manufacturing, maintenance, or quality experience.

The market value of people who understand yield analysis

Yield analysis requires both knowledge of the manufacturing floor and the ability to read data. If you only look at data without understanding what the processes and tools mean, it's hard to narrow down causes. On the other hand, floor experience alone has limits when it comes to broadly comparing differences between lots and tools.

That's why people with experience in manufacturing, inspection, quality, equipment maintenance, or data analysis can connect their experience to yield analysis more easily. Experience summarizing defect rates in Excel, tracking down the causes of quality problems, responding to equipment abnormalities, or adjusting conditions based on measurements all make good material to put into words for semiconductor jobs.

Manufacturing experienceMakes it easier to understand defects, operating conditions, lots, and tool differences from a floor perspective.
Quality experienceExperience with defect rates, preventing recurrence, root cause investigation, and writing reports is useful.
Data experienceExperience with Excel, Python, statistics, charting, and checking outliers is useful.

QC and quality improvement experience from other industries is a strong asset

Even if you have no semiconductor experience, quality control or quality assurance experience in automotive, electronic components, precision equipment, chemicals, food, pharmaceuticals, and similar industries fits well with yield analysis.

The reason is that the basics of quality improvement are shared across industries. Look at defect rates, separate where defects occur, check lot differences, confirm changes in equipment or conditions, narrow down causes with "5 Whys" analysis, and connect it to preventing recurrence. This flow is the same in semiconductors.

Of course, you need to learn semiconductor-specific process and tool names. But people with experience in QC stories, FMEA, control charts, design of experiments, process improvement, and troubleshooting can more easily translate job posting terms like "yield improvement," "quality data analysis," "process data analysis," and "failure analysis" into their own experience.

Summary: Yield analysis is data work that protects a fab's profits

Yield analysis is the job of tracking why the good-chip rate dropped, using defect maps, measurements, process history, equipment data, and more. It's not just counting defects. It looks at where, since when, and under what conditions they increased, and narrows down possible causes.

Data from defect inspection, CD-SEM, film thickness measurement, overlay metrology, and SPC become the material for yield analysis. Measuring is not the end. What matters is feeding the results back into process, equipment, materials, and environment to make improvements.

When you see terms like yield analysis, process data analysis, quality data analysis, SPC, failure analysis, or mass-production ramp-up in a job posting, think of it as work connected to what this article covers.

Yield is the percentage of chips made in semiconductor manufacturing that are actually good and usable. When yield is low, you get fewer chips you can sell, even with the same wafers, the same equipment, and the same materials. This article explains, for beginners, what yield means, why defects increase, and the jobs involved in yield improvement.

What is yield?

Yield is the percentage of products that could have been made that are actually usable as good products. In semiconductors, many chip areas are made on a single wafer. Only those that meet standards for electrical characteristics, appearance, reliability, and so on are used as good products.

Semiconductor industry materials describe yield as "the number of sellable products divided by the number of products that could potentially have been made." It may look difficult, but beginners only need to remember it as "the percentage of usable chips."

In practice, yield is seen as the product of "three yields"

Going one step deeper, semiconductor yield is not just one number. In practice, it is broken down into stages: from wafer start to the end of the front-end process, until the chips on the wafer pass electrical testing, and then until they are packaged in the back-end process and pass final testing.

Type of yieldWhat it measuresExamples of main problems
Wafer yield
Line yield		The percentage of started wafers that make it through the front-end process without breaking or being scrapped.Wafer breakage, transfer errors, lots scrapped mid-process, etc.
Chip yield
Die yield		The percentage of chip areas on the wafer that pass electrical testing and inspection.Lithography misalignment, etch residue, particle contamination, film thickness non-uniformity, wiring defects, etc.
Package yield
Assembly yield		The percentage of diced chips that are assembled and pass final testing after packaging.Connection failures, thermal stress, encapsulation defects, scratches, characteristic failures after mounting, etc.

Overall yield = wafer yield × chip yield × package yield

For beginners, "the percentage of good chips" is enough. But if you move to the intermediate level, remembering this multiplication changes how you see things. Even if only the front-end process is good, the number of final products will not increase if chips fail in the back-end process. Semiconductor manufacturing is the technology of reducing losses at every stage.

Why is yield important?

Yield is important because semiconductor manufacturing costs are extremely high. Wafers, chemicals, gases, electricity, cleanrooms, manufacturing equipment, and labor are all expensive. Even at the same cost, if there are fewer good products, the cost per chip goes up.

In the AI chip era, package yield is also a battleground

For GPUs and AI chips, advanced packaging, which places multiple chips and HBM side by side or stacks them, has become important. Here, not only the yield of individual chips but the overall yield after combining them becomes a major challenge.

For example, if you combine four chips that each have a 90% chance of being good, the chance that all of them are good drops to 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 = about 65.6%. This is because if even one defective chip is mixed in, the whole assembled product becomes unusable.

That is why inspection technology to select dies confirmed to be good before assembly, called KGD (Known Good Die), and yield improvement in the packaging process are very important. For semiconductors in the AI era, quality control not only in the front-end process but also in the back-end process and advanced packaging becomes a competitive advantage.

When bringing a new process or new product into volume production, yield is not always high from the start. So engineers look at inspection data and process data, find where defects are increasing, and improve things step by step. This is yield improvement.

Where do defects increase?

There is not always just one cause of lower yield. Causes can lie in many places, such as materials, processes, equipment, environment, design, and inspection conditions.

MaterialsWafer defects, impurities, metal contamination, chemical quality, and more have an effect.
ProcessesDeposition non-uniformity, lithography misalignment, etch residue, insufficient cleaning, CMP over-polishing, etc.
EquipmentCondition drift, worn parts, chamber contamination, sensor abnormalities, and more lead to defects.
EnvironmentParticles, temperature and humidity, chemical residue, and control inside the cleanroom are involved.
DesignThe less margin a design has due to miniaturization, the more easily slight variations lead to defects.
InspectionMeasurement variation or shifts in judgment criteria can make pass/fail decisions unstable.

What does yield improvement work involve?

Yield improvement is not simply about "making things more carefully." It is work that narrows down causes by looking at the location, type, and timing of defects, process conditions, equipment status, material lots, and inspection data.

1. Find defects

Use visual inspection, defect inspection, electrical testing, film thickness measurement, line width measurement, and more to see where abnormalities are.

2. Look at the defect distribution

Check whether defects are concentrated in the wafer center or at the edge, and whether they appear only in specific lots or on specific tools.

3. Narrow down candidate causes

Compare process conditions, equipment maintenance history, material lots, work timing, and measurement conditions.

4. Change conditions and verify

Change deposition, etching, cleaning, CMP, and other conditions to see whether defects decrease.

5. Standardize

Reflect the improved conditions in procedures and control limits so the same defect does not recur.

KLA is a company that handles process control and yield management using inspection, metrology, and computational analysis. In explanations of manufacturing data management such as Applied SmartFactory, yield management is also treated as a system that collects data from across the entire fab to speed up yield learning and volume production ramp-up. In other words, yield improvement is not just about on-site intuition. It is also work involving measurement and data.

Yield improvement work differs depending on whether it involves adjusting process conditions, looking at inspection data, or compiling quality documents. In job postings, check the assigned process, the tools used, the data handled, and how far you can be involved in improvements.

Yield improvement, defect analysis, and process data analysis directly affect a fab's profits and production stability, so they are areas where experienced-hire jobs tend to offer better pay. Yield terms beginners should know

Defect rate

The percentage of what you made that turned out defective. It looks at yield from the opposite side.

DefectYield
Defect density

A way of looking at how many defects there are on a wafer or chip. The more miniaturized things become, the bigger a problem small defects become.

DefectInspection
SPC

Short for Statistical Process Control. It manages variation in measured values statistically to check whether the process is running as usual.

StatisticsProcess control
Yield ramp

Raising yield step by step to bring a new product or new process closer to volume production.

Volume production ramp-upImprovement

Summary: Yield is the report card of semiconductor manufacturing

Yield is the percentage of chips made that are actually good and usable. The higher the yield, the more usable chips you get from the same wafers, the same equipment, and the same materials.

The causes of lower yield lie in many places, such as materials, processes, equipment, environment, design, and inspection. That is why in semiconductor manufacturing, inspection is not the end. It is important to look at data, isolate causes, change conditions, and prevent recurrence.

Once you understand yield, the jobs of process engineering, inspection and quality evaluation, quality assurance, and data analysis all connect. Don't just read technical articles. Also pay attention to words in job postings such as "yield improvement," "defect analysis," "SPC," and "process improvement."

What is lithography?

Lithography is the process of transferring a circuit pattern onto a wafer.

To put it more simply, it is the process of making marks on a film on the wafer that say "keep this," "remove this," or "process this in the next step."

In semiconductor manufacturing, various films such as oxide films, insulating films, and metal films are formed on the wafer. But if a film is left covering the whole surface, it is just a layer. To make a circuit, it has to be processed into the required shapes.

So a light-sensitive material called photoresist is coated on top of the film, and light is shone through a mask. The photoresist's properties change differently where light hits and where it does not, and after development, a resist pattern appears.

This resist pattern becomes the guide that decides the "areas to protect" and the "areas to remove" in the next step, etching.

Why is lithography needed?

Semiconductor chips contain extremely fine circuits. CPUs, GPUs, memory, and AI chips all depend on building huge numbers of tiny structures on a wafer.

Simply stacking materials does not make a circuit. After the materials are stacked, they must be processed into the required shapes. Lithography is what decides those "shapes."

The basic rhythm of the front-end process
DepositionForm a film
LithographyTransfer the pattern
EtchingRemove unwanted parts

Think of a building: just bringing in materials does not make a house. You need a blueprint that shows where to put the pillars, where to build the walls, and where to leave open space.

Semiconductors work the same way. Deposition provides the material, lithography specifies the shape, and etching does the processing. By repeating this cycle, complex semiconductor chips are built on the wafer.

The basic flow of lithography

Roughly speaking, lithography goes through the following steps.

  1. Clean the wafer
  2. Coat the photoresist
  3. Shine light through a mask
  4. Develop
  5. Form the resist pattern
  6. Move on to the next step, etching

1. Clean the wafer

Before lithography, the wafer surface is cleaned. In semiconductor manufacturing, tiny dust and contamination are a big problem. Even particles invisible to the human eye can be huge obstacles on a wafer.

2. Coat the photoresist

Next, photoresist is coated on the wafer surface. Photoresist is a material that reacts to light. Think of it as a thin film whose properties change with light.

The wafer is spun at high speed while liquid photoresist spreads over it, forming a very thin, even coat. Coating "evenly" is not easy, either. Uneven thickness affects the results of exposure and development.

3. Shine light through a mask

After the photoresist is coated, light is shone through a mask. A mask is like a master plate with the circuit pattern drawn on it.

The equipment used here is the lithography (exposure) tool. Companies such as ASML, Nikon, and Canon work in this field. ASML is especially dominant in cutting-edge EUV lithography tools.

However, not every step uses EUV. Not every layer in a chip is equally difficult, so EUV may be used for the truly fine, difficult layers, while other layers use other lithography technologies such as DUV.

4. Develop to form the resist pattern

After exposure comes development. Development is a process that removes the parts of the photoresist whose properties were changed by light.

After development, a photoresist pattern remains on the wafer. This becomes the guide for the next step.

What is photoresist?

Photoresist is a photosensitive material that reacts to light. In semiconductor manufacturing, it is used to create circuit patterns on wafers.

When people talk about lithography, attention tends to go to the lithography tools. Of course, those tools are very important. But without the material called photoresist, you could not transfer patterns with light.

This shows why materials makers such as Tokyo Ohka Kogyo, JSR, and Shin-Etsu Chemical are important. Equipment and materials push the limits together, as a set.

Why lithography is difficult

There is more than one reason lithography is difficult. Typical challenges include the following.

Patterns are extremely smallAs miniaturization advances, the patterns copied with light get smaller too.
Alignment is difficultMany layers of patterns must be stacked in exactly the right positions.
Material performance is neededResist sensitivity, resolution, and etch resistance are important.
Defects must be reducedTiny dust or defects can lead to major defects in later steps.
Stability in mass productionIt is not enough to succeed with one wafer in the lab. It must be made stably in the fab.
Paired with metrologyConditions are tuned while measuring dimensions, overlay, and defects.

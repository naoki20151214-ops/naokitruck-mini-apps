On September 23, 2026, Super Micro Computer (Supermicro) announced that it is "now shipping" NVIDIA's Vera Rubin NVL72 racks. What we can confirm is that the company has announced the start of product shipments. Shipment quantities, customer names, delivery sites, and the dates the racks start running in data centers have not been disclosed. So we cannot conclude that large-scale customer operation has already begun, or that a certain volume of production deliveries has been completed.Supermicro announcement

What is being shipped?

According to Supermicro, Vera Rubin NVL72 puts 72 Rubin GPUs, 36 Vera CPUs, 18 1U compute trays, and 9 sixth-generation NVLink switch trays into one rack. Total HBM4 capacity is given as 20.7 TB. In other words, it is not an ordinary server with just GPUs, but a rack-scale AI system designed as one unit for compute, memory, GPU-to-GPU communication, power, and cooling. These configuration figures are based on Supermicro's announcement.Rack configuration

HBM is high-speed memory placed close to the GPU. In AI, what matters is not only model computation but how fast weights and input data can be delivered to the compute units. HBM4 provides large capacity and fast data transfer, but capacity alone does not decide real processing performance. The model used, software, GPU-to-GPU communication, power limits, and data placement all affect the results.

Another key term is the NVLink switch. This is a dedicated switch that connects multiple GPUs at high speed so they can exchange data. In NVL72, instead of splitting 72 GPUs into separate servers, they work together within the rack like one large accelerator. NVIDIA describes Vera Rubin as a platform co-designed across CPU, GPU, networking, power, and cooling.NVIDIA technical blog

Why liquid cooling is central to deployment

As AI GPUs become more powerful, the power and heat concentrated in a rack grow. Fans circulating air alone have a hard time moving chip heat out fast enough, so liquid cooling becomes important for high-density racks.

Direct liquid cooling is a method in which a cold plate is mounted on top of a hot chip and liquid flows through it to carry the heat away. NVIDIA's technical blog describes Vera Rubin NVL72 as using single-phase warm-water direct liquid cooling with a 45°C supply. The collected heat is moved to the facility through manifolds, a CDU, and heat exchange equipment. A CDU (coolant distribution unit) connects the liquid loop inside the servers with the data center's cooling loop and manages liquid flow and heat transfer.Description of the cooling method

Supermicro says it integrates its DCBBS and DLC-2 direct liquid cooling stack with NVL72. The setup includes in-row CDUs rated at 1.8 MW each with N+1 redundancy, and Supermicro supplies liquid cooling components from cold plates and manifolds to CDUs, heat exchangers, and cooling towers. This shows that you need to prepare not only by putting AI chips into servers but also the facility's power and cooling equipment and installation work. However, these materials do not provide measured figures showing that liquid cooling always reduces electricity costs or water use.Supermicro liquid cooling configuration

Timeline from production plans to the start of shipments

On May 31, 2026, NVIDIA announced the production ramp of Vera Rubin and said that system makers including Supermicro would take part in production. At that time, the start of volume shipments was given as fall 2026. Supermicro's September 23 announcement can be seen as a system maker's announcement after that planned period began.NVIDIA production announcement

Here it is important to tell similar terms apart. A production ramp means setting up manufacturing and moving to a stage where products can be made continuously. The start of shipments means the maker has announced it is shipping the product. Customer delivery, completed installation, and live operation are later stages. This announcement alone does not tell us at which customer's facility, or when, operation began.

Supermicro also shows a design example of a Scalable Unit that places 1,152 Rubin GPUs and 331 TB of HBM4 in 16 compute racks and scales from 5 MW to gigawatt scale. This is the company's design and supply plan; it does not show completed deployments or order volumes across all customer projects. When judging scale, planned figures need to be kept separate from actual shipment and operation data.Scalable Unit description

From liquid-cooled AI infrastructure to power and cooling equipment jobs

After reading news about rack shipments, separate the roles of installation, power distribution, piping, cooling, and monitoring and recovery. Home PCs and data center equipment differ in scale and maintenance conditions. Translate your experience inspecting and measuring power and cooling equipment into the scope of work in job postings, and fill gaps in mechanical and electrical basics. For certifications, start with those you have confirmed are required by employers you are applying to, and compare exam requirements and study materials.

Think about benchmarks and real-world operation separately

On September 16, 2026, MLCommons published MLPerf Inference v6.1 results and included Vera Rubin NVL72 in the preview category. The start of evaluation in standardized tests shows it has moved to the stage of being verified as a real system. However, benchmark results do not prove shipment quantities, customer deployments, or long-term stability.MLCommons announcement

In its explanation the same day, NVIDIA presented comparison results under specific models, software, and measurement conditions. Because these figures depend on submission conditions, they do not mean every AI model gets the same performance, or that real performance per watt and operating costs improve by the same ratio. Judging performance requires third-party data with matched model, batch size, software, power, and cooling conditions, or customers' operating results.NVIDIA's MLPerf explanation

What this news means and what to check next

What matters here is not the launch of the Rubin GPU as a single chip, but the move to supplying whole systems that include GPUs, Vera CPUs, HBM, high-speed interconnect, liquid cooling, power, rack assembly, validation, and on-site installation. In AI data centers, deployment speed depends not only on a chip's theoretical performance but on whether the needed power can be secured, whether liquid cooling equipment can be installed, and whether networking and storage can run reliably.

Going forward, we want to check actual shipment quantities, customers and delivery regions, installation and start-of-operation dates, power consumption per rack, facility cooling conditions, and serviceability. Supermicro's large-scale design examples and NVIDIA's performance evaluations are important clues, but company plans and explanations are different from independent deployment results. The key to reading this news correctly is not to equate "start of shipments" with "completed volume deployment" or "live operation at customer facilities."

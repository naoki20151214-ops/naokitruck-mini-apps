Have you ever seen people in semiconductor fabs wearing cleanroom suits that cover their whole body?

Those suits are not just for looking clean.

A cleanroom is a place that controls the manufacturing environment to protect yield, before particles or environmental changes can turn into circuit defects.

A cleanroom in a semiconductor fab is a space that controls airborne particle concentration, as well as airflow, contamination from people, equipment, and materials, and temperature and humidity. The goal is not to "get dust down to zero." It is to protect the tiny patterns, films, and wiring on the wafer from contamination, so the number of good dies is less likely to drop.

Sources for the basic definition: ISO 14644-1:2015, ISO 14644-5:2025.

A cleanroom is not just "a room that has been cleaned"

In an ordinary factory, you can sometimes keep production going by cleaning the floor and equipment and removing visible dirt. But a semiconductor fab doesn't make parts you can see with your eyes. On a silicon disk called a wafer, it stacks thin films, makes patterns with light, removes material, cleans, and then builds the next layer.

The wafer is the base on which semiconductor devices are made. A die is a section that will be cut out as a single chip when finished. During manufacturing, the patterns, films, interfaces, and wiring needed in this small section are built up over many process steps. So the problem of foreign particles is not about dirt on the floor. It is about changing the shape or material condition partway through the process.

Naoki: Isn't it enough for a cleanroom to be cleaned well and sealed?

Professor Owl: Every time people walk, tools run, wafers are moved, or maintenance is done, new sources of particles and molecular contamination appear. So it's not enough to clean once at the start. You have to keep flowing, filtering, measuring, and managing what gets generated.

The main cleanliness classification for a cleanroom is the number concentration of airborne particles. This means how many particles at or above a given size are in one cubic meter of air. The number in an ISO Class is not the particle count itself.

For example, ISO Class 1 does not mean "only one speck of dust." It is a classification value of 10 or fewer particles of 0.1 micrometers or larger per cubic meter. For Class 5, it is 3,520 particles per cubic meter at 0.5 micrometers or larger. For Class 7, it is 352,000 particles per cubic meter. You always look at particle size and units together.

Source for cleanliness definitions and classification values: ISO 14644-1:2015. The numbers use the classification values listed in the Research Pack.

Particles don't stab circuits directly; they become starting points for defects

Particles on a wafer are a problem not only because large debris lands on a finished circuit and physically breaks it. Particles and residue can act as a mask in later steps, a local obstacle, a source of film peeling, or a foreign object that blocks contact. As a result, part of the intended shape may be missing, or extra material may be left behind.

In photolithography, a foreign particle near the photoresist on the wafer or near the photomask can disturb pattern transfer. In deposition, it can get trapped in the film or later become a source of peeling. In etching, particles or residue can act like a local mask and change how material is removed.

In wiring, this can lead to abnormalities such as a short, where neighboring conductors are unintentionally connected; an open, where wiring or a via that should be connected is broken; or higher contact resistance. A via here is a path that connects different conductive layers. In other words, a particle is not just surface dirt. It can turn into a defect in structure or electrical properties.

On defects and their relation to yield: Lam Research: Defect, KLA: Wafer Inspection.

The finer the structure, the more the same particle can affect it

As features get smaller, there are more cases where a particle of the same size becomes relatively large compared with the patterns and process margins. A particle that would have no effect on structures with wide spacing could disturb the shape if it sits between narrow lines or on an important contact.

However, there is no simple rule that if circuit dimensions are cut in half, tolerance to particles is always cut in half too. How serious a defect is depends not only on particle size but also on location, material, process step, 3D structure, electrical role, and whether it will be removed in a later step. You also can't read a technology node name as the physical line width of all wiring.

NIST notes that in advanced semiconductor manufacturing, controlling particle concentration and particle size distribution is related to yield and device performance, and that smaller particles will need to be measured. A safe way to put it is: "Scaling tends to narrow the margin for defects, so even small particles that used to be no problem can affect yield."

Source on particle measurement and advanced manufacturing: NIST: Nano-electrotechnologies.

Why people themselves are a major source of contamination

A big reason for wearing cleanroom suits is to protect products from people. Human skin, hair, clothing fibers, dust picked up outside, breathing and talking, and particles stirred up again by movement can all be sources of contamination. Especially in spaces where people are present, actions like walking, moving arms, and handling materials continue nonstop, so the amount of particles shed changes with clothing and activity.

That's why skin and regular clothing are covered with coveralls, hoods, masks, gloves, boots, and so on, and gowning procedures and movements are also controlled. Gloves not only limit contamination from hands but also serve as a barrier so people don't touch products directly. Practices like moving slowly, following set transfer methods, and not letting clean-side garments touch the floor or dirty surfaces are also important.

Cleanroom suits are not the same as safety PPE.
Cleanroom garments are mainly clothing to limit contamination moving from people to products and the environment. Their purpose is different from safety personal protective equipment, which protects workers from chemicals, hazardous gases, and mechanical hazards. Separate equipment may be needed depending on the hazards of the process.

Also, it is not accurate to say flatly that "people are always the biggest source of contamination." Equipment, transfer mechanisms, the processes themselves, materials, chemicals, and process gases can also be sources. People are one of the main sources, but which source dominates in which process depends on the facilities and working conditions.

Sources on human-derived contamination and cleanroom operation: ISO 14644-5:2025, KLA: Wafer Inspection.

Filters and air handling don't just bring air in; they circulate it and bring it back

Cleanroom air handling is not like a home, where you let clean air in once and you're done. Even under control, people, equipment, materials, and processes keep generating contaminants. So filtered air is sent near the products, carried to the return side without letting particles linger, and then filtered again and recirculated.

HEPA and ULPA are high-performance filters that remove particles from recirculated air. It is not correct to explain them as "removing 100% of particles 0.3 micrometers or larger" or "letting all smaller particles through." Filter collection efficiency is evaluated according to the applicable standard and conditions, such as around the particle size that passes through most easily.

There are also different types of airflow. Highly clean areas may use unidirectional flow, but that doesn't mean there is no turbulence anywhere in the room. The design takes into account heat and particles from equipment, layout, required cleanliness, energy, and more, and may include methods that use mixing and dilution.

In zones that must stay clean, the pressure may be kept slightly higher than the surroundings, so untreated air is less likely to come in through gaps around doors. This does not mean "a cleanroom is a vacuum." Rooms where people work are usually close to atmospheric pressure, which is a separate concept from vacuum processes inside equipment. In local equipment that contains hazardous materials, positive pressure for cleanliness and local exhaust for safety are combined as separate requirements.

Sources on air circulation, filters, zoning, and differential pressure: IEST: Cleanroom Standards, imec: Cleanroom.

It's not just particles that are controlled

ISO Classes represent airborne particle concentration, but the semiconductor manufacturing environment has other types of contamination and variation too. AMC (Airborne Molecular Contamination) is molecular, gas-phase chemical contamination rather than particles. Even if a particle counter shows few particles in the air, that does not automatically prove the air is chemically clean enough.

What is controlledRelation to manufacturing
ParticlesStick to wafers, photomasks, and equipment, and become starting points for pattern and film defects.
AMC and outgassing from materialsCan become chemical contamination that affects processes, products, and equipment.
Temperature and humidityRelated to process repeatability, surface condition, static electricity, and more.
Pressure and airflowLimit air coming in from less clean areas and particles lingering.
Static electricity and vibrationRelated to device damage, attracting particles, and precise alignment and measurement.

In other words, a cleanroom is not a room that only reduces airborne dust. It is a total manufacturing environment that controls outgassing from equipment and materials, trace moisture in process gases, metal, ionic, and organic contamination, temperature and humidity, static electricity, vibration, and more, based on the product and process.

Sources on chemical contamination: ISO 14644-8:2022, ISO 14644-15:2026. Coverage of temperature, humidity, static electricity, and vibration is also limited to the scope of the Episode 066 Research Pack.

By now, you can see that a cleanroom is not "a room with no dust," but "an operating system that keeps foreign particles and environmental changes from turning into defects." Next, let's look at how this control connects to the number of good chips.

Protecting yield means making it harder to lose good dies

Yield is the percentage of manufactured items that are good enough to use. A wafer has many dies on it, and those that remain as good dies after inspection move on to assembly and the final product. A cleanroom does not directly "create" yield, but as basic infrastructure that keeps defect density and process variation low, it works to protect good products.

The impact is not uniform. If a local particle is in an unimportant location, it may not cause an electrical failure. On the other hand, with a foreign particle on an important contact or photomask, or with repeated equipment or process abnormalities, the same defect can spread to many dies or wafers. That's why it is important to use inspection, metrology, and monitoring to separate one-off defects from systematic abnormalities.

The more advanced the semiconductor, the more contamination control matters

Advanced semiconductors stack not only fine patterns but also thin films, complex interfaces, 3D structures, and fine wiring and contacts over many process steps. As the acceptable margin narrows, there tend to be more cases where particles or chemical contamination that used to have no effect on products start to affect process results.

In EUV lithography, tiny particles on a photomask can be printed onto the wafer as pattern defects and lead to chip failures, so the photomask is protected with things like a pellicle. In advanced packaging too, particles or residue on the bonding surfaces that connect dies can lead to local voids or connection failures. Contamination control is not just a front-end process issue.

However, being more advanced doesn't mean a particular particle size will always cause a defect, or that ISO Classes for each process step are the same worldwide. How important a defect is depends on particle size, location, material, process, equipment, and product design. Detailed limits at each company also can't be stated as universal numbers based only on public information.

Common questions

Q. Is there no dust at all in a cleanroom?

No. It is a space where particle concentration by size is kept very low. An ISO Class is not a statement of zero particles. It classifies cleanliness by particle size and concentration per unit volume.

Q. Are cleanroom suits only for protecting workers?

One of their main roles is to make it harder for particles from people's skin and clothing to transfer to products. Their purpose is different from safety PPE, which protects workers from chemicals and mechanical hazards.

Q. If one particle lands on a wafer, does the whole wafer become defective?

Not necessarily. Depending on location and process, the effect may stay local. But if photomask or equipment abnormalities repeat, the problem can spread to many dies and wafers.

Q. Is a cleanroom a vacuum?

Usually, the cleanroom itself is close to atmospheric pressure, with small pressure differences from its surroundings being controlled. Vacuum is a separate process environment used inside equipment for etching, deposition, and so on.

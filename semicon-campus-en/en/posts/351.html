What is SPC?

SPC is a way of looking at variation in measured values statistically to check whether a process is stable. It is widely used in manufacturing and quality control, not just in semiconductors. The NIST/SEMATECH statistics handbook also describes process control as a method for monitoring processes and products and signaling when corrective action is needed.

In semiconductor manufacturing, even when you think you are making things under the same conditions, measured values change a little every time. Line width gets a little wider, film thickness gets a little thicker, the defect count rises a little. SPC is how you tell whether these small changes are just natural variation or a sign that something is wrong with the tool or process.

Put simply, SPC is a "health checkup for measured values."

Instead of judging from a single measurement, you look at changes over time, by lot, and by tool to spot unusual movement early.

SPC connects measured values to process control

CD-SEM, film thickness measurement, and defect inspection produce a lot of measured values. But just storing the data does not raise yield. What matters is looking at the data and judging "Is the process stable right now?" and "Has some change started?"

  • Record them together with lot and tool conditions
  • Check on a control chart whether things are as usual

For example, suppose the line width measured with CD-SEM is getting slightly narrower over time. It is still within spec, so the product may pass. But if it keeps creeping in the same direction, there may be a change in lithography conditions, etching conditions, or tool condition. SPC is used to find this "not out of spec yet, but maybe different from usual."

What is a control chart?

A tool often used in SPC is the control chart. A control chart is a graph that lines up measured values in time order, with a center line and control limits. The center line shows the average state, and the control limits are a guide for "the usual range of variation."

The important point is that control limits and product specifications are not the same. Specifications are lines for judging "whether the product passes." Control limits are lines for judging "whether the process is running as usual." Even within spec, a control chart may show that the process has shifted away from the center.

Key points of the diagram
  • Center line: the average state
  • Control limits: the usual range of variation
  • Outlier: a signal to investigate
  • Continuing drift: a possible change in conditions

Connecting SPC and trend monitoring to real work and reskilling

Where SPC meets maintenance is in matching changes on control charts against the history of tool inspections, part replacements, and condition changes on the same timeline. Keep control limits and product specifications separate, and organize which changes you reported as signals to investigate. If you want to build the ability to explain variation and possible causes, not just the steps to make a graph, learning the basics of quality control and statistics is the next step.

Why people who understand SPC are valuable in the job market

People who understand SPC are valuable not simply because they can read graphs. It is because they can look at changes in measured values and think, "Is this natural variation?", "Did tool conditions change?", "Is the material lot different?", or "Should process conditions be set back?"

A system can display data on its own. But people who can form hypotheses from control chart abnormalities about the tools, processes, materials, and maintenance history on the shop floor are not easy to replace. In a semiconductor fab, even a small drop in yield has a big impact, so people who can connect SPC to process improvement tend to be valued in quality evaluation, process engineer, failure analysis, and data analysis jobs.

What data is monitored with SPC in semiconductors?

In a semiconductor fab, every process produces measured values. With SPC, these values are viewed by process, by tool, and by lot to check for unusual movement.

  • Film thickness: watch for variation in deposition and CMP
  • Defect count: watch for increases in particles and scratches from cleaning and wafer transfer
  • Electrical characteristics: watch for performance changes in testing and process checks

Why SPC helps improve yield

SPC helps improve yield because it lets you notice changes before defects increase sharply. If you start looking for the cause after yield has dropped a lot, many lots may already be affected. If SPC helps you spot early signs of change, you can check changes in tool conditions, material lots, work conditions, and the environment at an early stage.

However, SPC is not "a tool that automatically tells you the cause when you look at a graph." A control chart is only a signal. When a signal appears, you narrow down possible causes by combining it with tool history, process conditions, materials, cleaning status, maintenance history, and other information from the shop floor.

The key with SPC is not to overreact.

Measured values have natural variation. If you change conditions right away based on a single point, you can actually make the process less stable. That is why you need control charts to separate "the usual variation" from "changes worth investigating."

Summary

SPC is a way of thinking that looks at variation in measured values to check whether a process is running as usual. In semiconductors, data such as CD, film thickness, defect counts, and electrical characteristics are used to spot signs of abnormality on control charts.

For beginners, it is enough to first remember that "SPC is a health checkup for the process." Don't just collect data; notice changes that are different from usual, and trace possible causes back to the process, tool, or materials. Once you understand this flow, quality evaluation, process engineer, failure analysis, and yield improvement jobs become easier to see.

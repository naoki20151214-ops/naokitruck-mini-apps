Bottom line of this article: Some problems cannot be seen from the "number" of defects alone, but recording their "location" and "distribution" shows which direction to investigate. However, a distribution is not proof of a cause. Only by combining inspection conditions, equipment and process history, and observation of the actual wafer can you narrow down the cause.

For example, suppose one wafer has 1,000 semiconductor chips, and 100 of them fail the electrical test. 900 pass. Counting only the tested chips, the good-chip rate is 90%.

For engineers on the floor, however, this is where the real problem begins.

Are the 100 failed chips lined up all around the wafer's outer edge? Are they connected like a single line? Or are they scattered here and there?

In every case, the number is the same: "100 defects." But the place to start investigating is not necessarily the same. That is because the suspected phenomenon differs: process variation that tends to occur at the edge, the possibility that something touched the wafer, particles spread over a wide area, and so on.

Image: Illustrative data with the same number of red squares in different arrangements. These are not actual manufacturing results. A specific cause cannot be determined from the distribution alone.

A wafer map is what makes this "where did it happen" visible.

Why is the location of defects a clue for finding the cause?

First, imagine a school test. If the class average simply drops, you cannot tell what happened. But if many students got the same question wrong, you have a reason to look at how that topic was taught or how the question was written.

Semiconductor manufacturing is similar. Information about where defects are concentrated is an entry point for checking whether something affected different locations differently during manufacturing.

However, this analogy has limits. In semiconductors, the place where a problem is measured and the process where it occurred can differ, and you cannot work backward from the pattern to a single cause. What matters is not memorizing "this shape means this cause," but listing phenomena that could produce that shape and confirming them with other information.

A problem that occurs only at the edge is a reason to check whether processing conditions differ between the edge and the center. If you see a line, it might be related to the path of something that made contact or to wafer handling. If defects keep appearing at the same position, you start to wonder about a link to repeated patterns or positioning.

At this point, the engineer changes from someone looking at numbers to someone who forms hypotheses and investigates on the floor.

In two kinds of wafer maps, the same "red" means different things

The term wafer map covers different kinds of data. The first two that beginners should tell apart are these.

Image: An educational illustration comparing pass/fail results per square with defects detected as points. The meaning of colors and bin numbers varies by product and test setup.

A Wafer Bin Map takes the test result category for each chip (die), mainly from electrical testing, and places it back at its position on the wafer. Each colored square may represent one die. Which bins mean pass, and what kind of failure each one means, depends on the product and test program.

A Defect Map, on the other hand, shows the positions of defect candidates found by inspection equipment. One die may have several points.

So 10 red dots is not the same as 10 failed dies. You cannot say that every detected defect causes an electrical failure, or that a die that passed the electrical test has no abnormality outside what was inspected. When overlaying maps, first confirm what the data actually measured.

Electrical testing on the wafer is explained in detail in How testing works, from wafer test to final test. For the difference between inspection (finding defects) and metrology (measuring dimensions and film thickness), see our article on inspection and metrology.

What do the seven common distributions prompt you to suspect?

Next, let's look at the patterns on wafer maps. The seven below are a way of organizing things for understanding. They are not a classification in which each shape maps one-to-one to a failure cause in every fab.

Image: An illustrative image showing edge, center, line, local cluster, scattered, radial, and repeating patterns. These are not measured wafer maps.

When defects gather in a ring around the edge, consider whether processing conditions differed between the edge and the center. For example, Lam Research explains that electrical and chemical conditions at the wafer edge affect uniformity in the etching process. However, a ring does not prove that a specific edge part has worn out.

If defects concentrate in the center, you can consider whether film thickness, temperature, amount of processing, and so on differ between the center and the edge. But there is no fixed rule such as "center = CMP problem."

When defects line up in a line, it is worth checking for mechanical contact, the direction of wafer handling, and scratches left on the surface. However, a line on a bin map is not a photo of a scratch; it is just a row of failed dies.

If defects gather in one corner, list candidates for a local problem at that location. If they are scattered, the lack of an obvious shape does not mean you can ignore them. Defect density, type, and time of occurrence matter.

For radial streaks, processes involving rotation are sometimes candidates. A published Tokyo Electron (TEL) patent describes radial film abnormalities around the edge in spin coating. However, you should not extend that to explain every radial pattern in electrical testing.

And then there are patterns that repeat at the same relative position. Here, you focus not on the "absolute position" on one wafer, but on the "corresponding position" within the same circuit or exposure field. KLA has introduced a technique that converts wafer inspection data into exposure-field coordinates and checks repeating defects against other inspection data.

Whatever the pattern, the first question is the same.

"What phenomena could produce this pattern? And where is other evidence that supports it?"

A real investigation: following a line of scratches led to a tiny fiber

What follows is a case that was actually made public. However, it is a report by Foamtec itself, not the result of independent third-party verification.

When you find a scratch-like distribution, it is not appropriate to immediately replace the transfer robot on the floor. You check the hypothesis step by step by examining the direction of the scratches, where the wafer is touched, and how it moves during transfer.

Foamtec's published article describes a case in which a polyester fiber was observed moving on the robot blade of a transfer system, and similar behavior occurred during wafer transfer. The company reports that it identified the fiber as the cause of the scratches and removed it.

Image: A conceptual illustration to help understand the Foamtec case. It is not a photographic reproduction of the equipment, fiber, or scratches in the report. Source: Foamtec official case study.

What this case teaches is not the shortcut "it's a line, so the robot is at fault." It is the process: forming a contact hypothesis from the pattern, looking at the actual equipment, and confirming a specific foreign object.

In the published material, we could not confirm the yield improvement after the fiber was removed or the long-term recurrence rate. We must not add convenient numbers to turn it into a success story.

The hard part is "overlaying at the correct position"

Real root-cause investigations face another big hurdle.

What if inspection tool A uses the lower-left of the wafer as the origin, while test equipment B counts from a different position? Even if both data sets are correct, overlaying them as is creates a misleading image.

Image: A hypothetical example showing differences in data orientation. It does not reproduce the actual coordinate conventions of any specific equipment.

SEMI's official explanation also notes that the coordinate system used by a given combination of prober and test recipe is not always the same as the coordinate system of the substrate mapping standard E142.

What is needed is to align the wafer ID, origin, X and Y directions, units, notch direction, die layout, and inspection time.

Finish the unglamorous data cleanup before drawing nice-looking graphs. In data analysis at semiconductor fabs, that is a very important job.

Once a distribution is visible, what do engineers check, and in what order?

Now suppose you find that defects are concentrated on wafers that went through a certain tool. Is that tool the cause?

You cannot decide yet. If that tool only processed a particular material lot, you cannot separate the tool's effect from the material's effect using observed data alone.

The checks and approvals needed in an actual fab follow each fab's quality control and safety procedures. The following organizes the thinking behind an investigation. It is not an instruction to stop or restart equipment or to perform maintenance work.

  1. Confirm what the data means. Is it a map of failed dies or of detected defects? Are the inspection sensitivity and test conditions the same? Are untested areas being counted as defects?
  2. Compare good and bad groups. Stratify by product, tool, chamber, recipe, and material lot. Not only "conditions shared by the defects," but also "wafers that were fine under the same conditions" are important.
  3. Link location to process history. When and through which processes did the wafer pass, and what changed before and after cleaning or parts replacement? The process that found the defect and the process that created it may be different.
  4. Observe and verify by comparison. As needed, examine the location with a review SEM or similar tool, and check which of several explanations fits. After the fix, confirm that the problem does not recur and that nothing else gets worse.

A 2025 study published by researchers affiliated with IBM analyzed the process history and defect density of 787 wafers and identified long wait times at a specific tool as a candidate cause. However, what the published study confirms is only the identification of a candidate. It must not be retold as a story in which changing the wait time proved the cause.

This whole way of thinking also connects to techniques for narrowing down the cause of equipment trouble and why process quality is checked even after recovery.

What can AI find in wafer maps?

Comparing large numbers of defect distribution patterns is a heavy load for humans alone. So image processing that turns shapes into numbers, machine learning, and deep learning have been researched and used.

Image: An educational image that distinguishes AI shape classification from verifying the cause on the floor.

A 2025 paper by Tsai and Wang reports a classification accuracy of **98.39%** under the proposed conditions, for nine classes using a research dataset called WM-811K. This comes with training data augmentation and specific evaluation conditions.

Here is an important point. This number is the accuracy of classifying maps into shape classes. It is not a 98.39% rate of identifying failure causes, nor a 98.39% improvement in yield.

On the floor, several patterns can overlap on one wafer. Even if AI classifies a pattern as "line," you cannot know what phenomenon created that line without checking the original coordinates, inspection conditions, and equipment, material, and process history.

AI's role is not to make unsupported conclusions in place of engineers, but to bring out patterns that are easy to miss and set priorities for investigation.

The "compare, record, confirm" skills built in manufacturing pay off

This work is not made up only of advanced AI knowledge.

In equipment maintenance, it is the ability to explain which parts were replaced when, and how the tool behaved afterward. In production engineering, it is the ability to stratify and compare the relationship between condition changes and quality. In inspection and quality control, it is the ability not to miss differences in measurement conditions and judgment criteria.

This kind of experience connects in terms of thinking even after moving to the semiconductor industry. Of course, you will still need to learn processes, equipment, and safety management specific to semiconductors.

Why does this work matter? If the cause cannot be narrowed down quickly, more wafers and tools need investigating, and decisions about the scope of impact and production plans become harder. On the other hand, misreading the data and suspecting healthy tools or good products can also cause losses.

In other words, reading wafer maps is not just looking at round diagrams. It is work that reduces wasted investigation and wrong decisions, so semiconductors can keep being made at the same quality.

Summary: A wafer map tells you "what to check next"

One wafer had 100 defects. All that tells you is how many defects there were.

Are they at the edge, in a line, or repeating at the same position? Adding location gives you hypotheses for investigating the cause.

But the shape is only a starting point. What did the map measure? Do the coordinates line up? Which tools and processes did the wafer pass through? Engineers connect separate pieces of evidence and remove assumptions one by one.

Finding defects and proving their cause are different things. That is exactly why wafer maps are valuable.

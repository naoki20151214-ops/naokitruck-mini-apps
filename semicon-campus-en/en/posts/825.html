To light a room about as brightly, an LED bulb needs less power than an incandescent bulb.
The difference is not the shape of the bulb, but "the path it takes to turn electricity into light."
The reason is that an incandescent bulb heats a metal wire until it gives off light across a wide range of wavelengths, while an LED uses a semiconductor to make light that is close to what our eyes can see.

The short answer
An incandescent bulb heats a thin metal wire called a filament to a very high temperature and uses the visible part of the thermal radiation it gives off for lighting. An LED makes photons when electrons and holes recombine inside a compound semiconductor. LEDs also have losses and produce heat, but it is easier for them to reach the same luminous flux with less input power.

An incandescent bulb glows by heating a filament

Inside an incandescent bulb is a thin filament made of tungsten. Tungsten has a high melting point and holds up well at high temperatures. When current flows through it, electrical resistance turns electrical energy into internal energy, and the filament heats up. This is called Joule heating.

The key point is that in an incandescent bulb, getting hot is not a side effect. It is the very way the bulb makes light. Any object with a temperature gives off electromagnetic radiation. When the filament gets very hot, part of that thermal radiation falls into wavelengths the human eye can see, and we see it as warm light.

However, thermal radiation does not conveniently gather only in the range people can see. At the temperature of an incandescent bulb, much of it spreads into the infrared range, which has longer wavelengths than visible light. Heat also flows into the supports and lead wires, and moves through the gas inside and the glass bulb. In other words, the input energy is split among things other than the light that is actually useful for lighting a room.

An LED makes photons inside a semiconductor

LED stands for Light Emitting Diode. The light-emitting part uses a compound semiconductor made of several elements. It has an n-type side with relatively many electrons that carry current, and a p-type side with relatively many holes, which are like empty seats where electrons could be.

When a forward voltage is applied, electrons and holes are injected into a thin region that emits light. When an electron moves from a higher-energy state to a lower one and fills a hole, this is called recombination. When the energy is released as light, one unit of that light is called a photon.

This does not mean that "electrons and holes crash into each other." Also, not every recombination produces light. In some processes, the energy goes into crystal defects or lattice vibrations and becomes heat. Even so, LEDs do not work by heating the whole light source until it glows. That is why their strength is being able to selectively make light close to the visible range.

How is white LED light made?

Home lighting needs white light, but most white LEDs do not produce white light directly from the semiconductor alone. In the common method, part of the light from a blue LED shines on a phosphor. A phosphor is a material that absorbs light and gives it off again at longer wavelengths.

When the blue light that was not converted mixes with the light from the phosphor, which includes green, yellow, red, and other colors, it looks white to the human eye. Some products use several phosphors to fine-tune how colors look. There are also methods that combine a near-violet LED with several phosphors, or that mix red, green, and blue LEDs, so "all white LEDs have the same structure" is not necessarily true.

A phosphor is not a magic material that amplifies light. Making white light also involves losses, such as the energy difference when changing wavelength, reabsorption, scattering, and absorption by the encapsulant or cover. Just because LEDs save energy does not mean all the input power becomes lighting.

An LED bulb is more than just an LED chip

Here, let's separate the small LED that emits light from the LED bulb you screw into a socket at home. An LED bulb is a product that combines LED packages, a driver circuit that regulates current, a board that holds the LEDs, a cover that spreads the light, a housing and heat sink that carry heat away, and a base.

Household power is alternating current (AC), but LEDs must run on current of the right direction and size. That is why the driver handles rectification and current control. The driver's components also have losses from resistance, switching, and so on, and they produce heat. Even after light is created inside the LED, some of it reflects at the boundary between the semiconductor and the outside, and some is absorbed by the phosphor, encapsulant, or diffusing cover.

So when thinking about high efficiency, you need to look at the whole bulb, not just the LED chip. A record value for a single chip cannot be taken as the performance of a bulb sold in stores. And once the bulb is put in a light fixture, the fixture's cover, reflector, internal temperature, and light distribution also have an effect.

Why is there a difference in power use?

To sum up the difference as one chain of cause and effect: an incandescent bulb goes "heat it up → wide-wavelength thermal radiation comes out → use part of it as visible light," while an LED bulb goes "control the current → make photons in a semiconductor → turn the light white and send it out."

In an incandescent bulb, a large share of the input energy goes not only into the visible light we want for lighting, but also into infrared radiation. In an LED, the semiconductor materials and structure make it easier to produce light close to the visible range, and it can be converted into the wavelengths needed for white light. Losses along the way remain, but it is easier to keep down the input needed to make the same amount of light.

Here, it is not correct to say flatly that "LEDs turn electricity directly into light, so there is no waste." There are recombinations that do not produce light, resistance in electrodes and wiring, driver losses, phosphor conversion, internal reflection, and absorption by the cover, and each one lowers the efficiency of the whole bulb. Saving energy does not mean zero losses. It is a comparison: compared with an incandescent bulb, an LED can more easily direct power into light that is useful for lighting.

Once you understand this much, you can explain it without the misconception that "LEDs save energy because they give off no heat."

To judge efficiency, read lm and lm/W, not W

Watts (W) show the real power a bulb uses. For incandescent bulbs, wattage was long used as a guide to brightness, so many people remember "more watts means brighter." But when comparing with LEDs, which make light in a different way, watts alone cannot tell you how bright a bulb is.

Lumens (lm) are the total amount of light a source gives off, weighted by how sensitive the human eye is to each wavelength. They are the main measure for comparing the brightness of bulbs. Lux (lx), on the other hand, is the density of light reaching a surface such as a desk or floor. Even with the same lumens, lux changes depending on whether the light spreads wide or is focused narrowly, and on distance and angle.

lm/W shows how many lumens of luminous flux a bulb gives off per watt of input power. For example, for the same luminous flux, the fewer watts needed, the higher the lm/W. Note that lm/W is not a percentage. Because lumens are weighted by human visual sensitivity, you cannot say "100 lm/W means 100% of the electricity became light."

Also, lm/W alone does not decide everything about a bulb's value. How the light spreads, color temperature, how colors of objects look, flicker, compatibility with fixtures, and durability are separate measures. To compare energy savings, it is safest to first pick bulbs with similar lumens, similar light distribution, and similar color conditions, and then look at W and lm/W.

Why LEDs still need thermal design

LEDs do not heat their light-emitting part until it glows, but that does not mean they give off no heat. Energy from recombinations that do not produce light, electrical resistance in the semiconductor and electrodes, the driver circuit, and losses in the phosphor and optical parts mostly become heat.

In an incandescent bulb, infrared radiation from the hot filament goes out in all directions, including forward, and the glass bulb also tends to get hot. In an LED, the design focuses on carrying heat from the light-emitting layer through the board, the heat sink housing, and toward the base, and then out. That is why the base of an LED bulb can get hot even though its light-emitting surface is not as hot as an incandescent bulb. This is not necessarily a sign of a fault. It is partly because the heat path is concentrated at the base.

If heat cannot escape well and the temperature inside the semiconductor rises, luminous flux and efficiency may drop, the color may shift, and the encapsulant, solder, adhesive, and driver parts may wear out faster. Heat tends to build up in enclosed fixtures, so you need to check product compatibility labels such as "suitable for enclosed fixtures."

Clearing up common misconceptions

Q1. Do LEDs give off no heat at all?

No. Energy that does not become light in the semiconductor, plus losses from resistance, the driver, the phosphor, and optical parts, mostly becomes heat. Not heating the light-emitting part until it glows is different from producing zero heat.

Q2. Does an incandescent bulb always waste a fixed share of its power as heat?

It is not accurate to describe every product with one fixed share. It changes with temperature, product conditions, and where you draw the line between light and heat, so it is safest to think of it as "much of the input goes into infrared radiation and heating."

Q3. Does a "60W equivalent" bulb use 60 W?

No. "Equivalent" is a guide to the brightness class it replaces, not its actual power use. On product labels, check lumens (lm) for brightness and watts (W) for power use separately.

Q4. If a bulb has high lm/W, is it better in every way?

Not necessarily. lm/W is luminous flux per unit of input power. Light distribution, color temperature, how colors of objects look, flicker, durability, and fixture compatibility need to be checked separately.

Q5. Can an LED bulb go into any fixture?

No. Check not only the base and size, but also labels for use in enclosed fixtures, with dimmers, in fixtures covered by insulation, outdoors, in bathrooms, and so on. Do not take the bulb apart to check, because mains power and leftover electrical charge are dangerous.

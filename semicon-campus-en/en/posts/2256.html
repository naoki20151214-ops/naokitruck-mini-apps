On the same day, several people got an upset stomach. When asked, it turned out they had all eaten boxed lunches from the same shop. In that case, the shop is the suspect. When searching for a cause, "what things have in common" is a big clue.

Semiconductor fabs think the same way. When defects are found on wafers (the round discs semiconductors are made on), engineers look for the tools and processes that the defective wafers all went through. The "distribution," meaning where on the wafer the defects are concentrated, is also important. In this way, they get closer to the "deepest cause" behind the problem, called the "root cause."

The boxed lunch example only applies in the sense of "looking for what things have in common." A boxed lunch and a semiconductor fab are, of course, different things.

A root cause is the deepest cause

The American Society for Quality (ASQ) glossary describes a root cause as a factor that caused a nonconformance and should be permanently eliminated through process improvement. In other words, it is the topmost cause that started the chain of cause and effect.

The methods and tools for finding this root cause are together called "root cause analysis (RCA)." However, just knowing the cause is not an achievement. It only has meaning once you remove it.

Why focus so much on the root? Because if you fix only the surface cause, the same defect may come back in a different form. Like a game of whack-a-mole, fixing defects one by one as they pop up never ends.

Stages 1 and 2: Detect and contain

In semiconductor manufacturing, a departure from the expected results is called an "excursion." According to a patent description, most of them involve tools. What's more, wafers go through hundreds of processes, and it is normal for one process to have several tools. So finding which tool is the cause is harder than you might think.

So what do you do first when you notice an abnormality? The answer is to "contain" it. According to an explanation by inspection equipment maker KLA, the affected wafers are isolated (quarantined), all of them are inspected with high sensitivity, and a decision is made whether to ship, scrap, or downgrade them. The more often inspections are done, the sooner abnormalities are found, so fewer lots are affected and there are fewer candidate causes.

To compare it with something familiar, it is like a supermarket that learns "products received on this day may have a problem" and first pulls that day's products from the shelves. Investigating the cause comes after that. If you contain the problem first, the damage does not spread while you investigate.

Stage 3: Narrow down the tools from what defective wafers have in common

Now the real search for the cause begins. A typical method is "commonality analysis," which looks for tools that the defective wafers all went through. This analysis is possible because the fab records everything about "which tool processed which wafer, and when." Wafers are carried in containers called "lots," about 25 at a time, so records are also kept by lot.

There are two main points for telling things apart.

  • Do all the defective lots come from a single tool? It is unlikely that several tools drift at the same time, so the more the lots line up on one tool, the more suspicious it becomes.
  • Are the defects concentrated in a short period? This is because the effects of a drifting tool gather around that tool's process.

Just checking these two points can greatly reduce the number of tools to investigate. If you can narrow hundreds of processes and tools down to a few tools, you can then carefully study those tools' records.

Key points of the figure

  • Compare, by tool, the share of defective wafers that passed through
  • Defects are concentrated on tool A
  • The tool with the concentration is suspicious (but don't jump to conclusions)

The figure shows the share of defective wafers that passed through each tool. Almost all the defective wafers went through tool A only. In this case, it would be natural to start investigating with tool A.

However, it is not that simple. There are cases where each tool looks fine on its own, but defects increase only with a combination of two tools, meaning only for wafers that went through both in a row. This example also appears in a patent. That is why commonalities are treated as clues, not answers.

Key points of the figure

  • With tool A alone or tool B alone, there are few defects
  • Going from A to B in a row produces many defects
  • Check combinations too

To look at combinations, the scope of investigation widens from one tool at a time to two tools at a time. It takes more effort, but it lets you reach hard-to-catch causes where "each tool is normal on its own, yet defects appear."

You can also trace the cause from the "shape" of defects

Engineers also pay attention to the shape in which defects gather on the wafer. According to a peer-reviewed paper, when an abnormality occurs, defective chips gather in specific shapes. That makes it possible to trace the cause from the shape. For example, if defects gather in the center, uneven polishing or abnormal liquid pressure is suspected; if they form a ring, a problem with temperature control during annealing is suspected.

This is where the "wafer map" comes in. It refers to a wafer diagram on which chips that did not work in an electrical test (probe test) are marked. It is easier to notice skew by looking at a diagram than by following numbers in a table. The paper notes that if the shape is linear, like a scratch, an abnormality in a process that transports or cuts the wafer is suspected.

Of course, the shape alone does not determine the cause. The same shape can have several possible causes. It is easier to use the shape as a clue for deciding the order in which to investigate.

There is also a method of comparing the distribution of measurement data for each layer with the distribution of final defects. If you find a layer that looks similar, the process just before it becomes suspicious. If layer i does not look similar but the next layer, i+1, does, the problem most likely started between them.

Key points of the figure

  • Compare each layer with the defect distribution
  • The first layer that looks similar is a clue
  • Between a layer that doesn't look similar and one that does is a candidate for where it started

Stage 4: Fix and confirm

Once the cause is visible, you remove it. Detect the abnormality, match the collected data against the failure symptoms, decide on the most likely cause, and fix it. This flow is also shown in a patent. In a PDF Solutions case, combining tool records with electrical test results revealed that an indicator had drifted right after maintenance. The countermeasures were revising the maintenance procedure, adding a break-in run, and confirming that signals were stable before returning the tool to production. It does not end with the fix; confirming the result is part of the same sequence.

As in this case, there is also a method that focuses on "what has changed recently." ASQ's explanation calls it "change analysis," which examines changes in people, equipment, information, and so on when performance changes significantly. Maintenance is a big change for a tool. So comparing records before and after maintenance can reveal when the drift started.

There is another method called "barrier analysis." It examines why the controls meant to prevent or detect a problem did not work. If you dig down to "why couldn't we notice the abnormality," you gain more ways to prevent recurrence.

Searching for causes is a team effort

According to ASQ, many of the tools can be used by one person, but results are generally better when several people work together. It is also recommended to include the people responsible for removing the root cause as core members.

Key terms review

  • Wafer: The round disc that semiconductors are made on.
  • Root cause: The deepest cause that triggered a problem.
  • Excursion: When process results depart from expectations.
  • Lot: A container that carries about 25 wafers together.
  • Isolation (quarantine): Keeping affected lots from moving forward.
  • Commonality analysis: A method of finding tools that defective wafers all went through.
  • Distribution: The spread of where things are and how many there are.

Common misconceptions

Q. If you fix the place where the defect was found, is the problem solved?

No. The place where it was found may be just a result. If the root remains, the same defect will happen again.

Q. If you know which tool the defective wafers went through, is that tool the cause?

Don't jump to conclusions. Even if each tool is normal on its own, defects can increase with a combination of two tools.

Summary

Searching for the cause of defects has four stages: detect, contain, narrow down, and fix and confirm.
When narrowing down, what the tools that defective wafers went through have in common and the shape of the defect distribution are big clues.
Commonalities are clues, not answers, so remove the root cause while confirming as you go.

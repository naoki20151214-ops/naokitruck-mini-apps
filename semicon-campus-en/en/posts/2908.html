So far, we have looked at ways to remove contamination. But in fact, cleaning itself also causes problems.

In this article, we explain, step by step, the challenges of "wet cleaning," which uses liquids, and the idea of "dry cleaning," which uses no liquid.

Yellow highlights = key points that appear on this course's tests

What you will learn in this article

  • Why over-cleaning is a problem (the surface gets rough)
  • 5 challenges of wet cleaning
  • What dry cleaning methods there are

① Over-cleaning makes the surface rough

Lecture materials from KTH (Royal Institute of Technology, Sweden) first list surface roughness (microroughness) as a problem with wet cleaning. The cleaning solution or metal contamination etches the silicon surface and creates very fine bumps. In particular, they explain that a high concentration of the ammonia in SC-1 etches and damages silicon.

Why is a rough surface a problem?

According to KTH's materials, a rough surface lowers how easily the particles that carry electricity move through the transistor's channel (carrier mobility). It also has a large negative effect on the quality of thermally grown gate oxide and on how hard it is to break down (breakdown voltage). In other words, cleaning to remove contamination can end up lowering transistor performance.

Evidence

The "Problems in Wet Cleaning" slide in KTH IH2655, "Lecture 3: Wafer Clean and Wet Processing" (Mikael Östling, 2012).

Quick quiz: In SC-1, a high concentration of what makes the silicon surface easier to damage?

Answer: The concentration of ammonia (NH4OH).

② 3 ways to reduce roughness

KTH's materials give the following 3 countermeasures.

  • Lower the ammonia concentration
  • Lower the cleaning temperature
  • Shorten the cleaning time

IMEC's "ammonia : hydrogen peroxide : pure water = 0.05 : 1 : 5," which we saw in Lesson 3, is an example of the first idea.

Let's think in numbers

When making 10 liters of solution, how much ammonia do you need?

Standard SC-1 (1:1:5) adds up to 7, so 10 × 1 ÷ 7 ≈ 1.43 liters. The IMEC method (0.05:1:5) adds up to 6.05, so 10 × 0.05 ÷ 6.05 ≈ 0.083 liters (83 milliliters).

1.43 ÷ 0.083 ≈ about 17, so that is a difference of about 17 times. (Even though "0.05 vs. 1 is a 20-times difference," the totals differ, so it comes out to about 17 times.)

Quick quiz: Name 3 ways to reduce surface roughness.

Answer: Lower the ammonia concentration, lower the temperature, and shorten the time.

③ 5 challenges of wet cleaning

Besides roughness, KTH's materials list the following 5 challenges of wet cleaning.

  • It can bring in particles (chemicals and pure water contain particles)
  • Drying is difficult
  • It is costly
  • Waste liquid must be treated
  • It has become a poor fit for advanced integration technology
Why would cleaning bring in particles?

Because neither the cleaning solution nor the pure water is guaranteed to be 100% clean. KTH's materials even include a graph showing the number of particles in semiconductor-grade chemicals. If the cleaning tool (the liquid) is dirty, the more you clean, the dirtier things get.

Let's think in numbers

In Lesson 1, about 80 of roughly 400 steps were cleaning. If each cleaning needs 10 liters of chemicals and pure water, that is 80 × 10 = 800 liters (along the route one wafer travels). (10 liters is just an example to show the idea.)

The more times you clean, the more cost and waste liquid increase. The numbers show why it is worth improving how cleaning is done.

Quick quiz: Name one challenge of wet cleaning.

Answer: (Examples) Any of these: it brings in particles, drying is hard, it is costly, waste liquid must be treated, or it is a poor fit for advanced technology.

④ "Dry cleaning," which uses no liquid

That is where "dry cleaning," which removes contamination with gases or energy instead of liquids, comes in. KTH's materials introduce the following 4 methods.

  • HF / water vapor (gas) cleaning: Removes native oxide with HF vapor
  • UV ozone cleaning (UVOC): Breaks down organics with ultraviolet light and ozone
  • Hydrogen / argon plasma cleaning
  • Thermal cleaning
What should you watch out for in dry cleaning?

To drive reactions at low temperatures, you need energy. That energy comes from plasma, ion beams, short-wavelength light, heat, and so on. However, KTH's materials say it is important not to let that energy damage the wafer.

⑤ The "containers" used for cleaning matter too

The containers that hold chemicals and the carriers that move wafers can also be sources of contamination. KTH's materials say that SC-1, SC-2, and similar solutions use quartz or Teflon containers, HF uses Teflon containers, and wafer carriers should be only quartz or Teflon.

Key points of this lesson

Over-cleaning roughens the surface and lowers transistor performance. The countermeasure is to lower concentration, temperature, and time. Wet cleaning has challenges with particles, drying, cost, waste liquid, and fit with advanced technology, so dry cleaning is also used.

Word review

  • Microroughness: Very fine bumps that form on a surface.
  • Wet cleaning: A method that cleans using liquids.
  • Dry cleaning: A method that cleans with gases or energy instead of liquids.
  • Teflon and quartz: Container materials that resist chemicals and give off little contamination.

Common misconceptions

Q. The more you clean, the cleaner it gets, right?

No. Over-cleaning can roughen the surface and lower transistor performance.

Q. If you clean with liquid, particles will never increase, right?

No. Particles in chemicals and pure water can stick to the wafer.

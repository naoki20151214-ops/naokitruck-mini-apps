What is deposition?

Deposition is the process of creating a very thin layer of material on the surface of a wafer. In English, it is called deposition or thin film deposition.

You cannot make a semiconductor just by carving a silicon wafer. You stack many layers, such as insulating films, conductive films, protective films, and films meant to be etched later. Then you keep some parts and remove others to build the structures of transistors and wiring.

This is all you need to know for now.
Deposition is "the process of making a thin layer with the needed properties on top of the wafer." Making a film alone does not create the shape of a circuit. It is combined with the next steps, lithography and etching, so the film stays only where it is needed.

Why do we need to make films?

Deposition is needed because a semiconductor chip needs layers with many different roles. If the whole wafer stayed plain silicon, you could not separate the places where electricity flows from the places where it is blocked.

InsulateSeparates areas where electricity must not flow. Oxide films and nitride films play a part here.
Conduct electricityMetal films that connect to gate electrodes and wiring create paths for signals and power.
Protect and prepareProtects the surface or creates an underlayer to be etched in the next step.

Understand deposition together with the steps before and after it

If you learn deposition on its own, the knowledge is a little hard to use. What matters is what happens before and after deposition.

The basic rhythm of the front-end process
CleaningReduce contamination
DepositionMake a thin film
LithographyTransfer the pattern
EtchingRemove unneeded parts

For example, making an insulating film on the wafer surface does not yet create the shape of a circuit. You coat photoresist on that film, transfer a pattern with lithography, and remove the unneeded parts with etching. Only then do you reach a state where "the film remains only where it is needed."

Types of films used in semiconductors

Semiconductor manufacturing uses more than one kind of film. The materials and the methods change depending on the role.

Oxide filmsA typical example is silicon oxide. It is important for insulation, protection, and as an underlayer.
Nitride filmsThese films have many roles in the process, such as protective films, insulating films, and etch stop layers.
Metal filmsUsed to conduct electricity, for example in electrodes and wiring.
High-k filmsHigh-dielectric-constant materials that became important with scaling. They appear in gate insulators and similar layers.
Low-k filmsLow-dielectric-constant materials used to reduce the capacitance between wires.
Barrier filmsHidden guardians that, for example, keep metal from diffusing into places it should not go.

The main categories of deposition methods

There are many deposition methods, but beginners get a clearer view by first learning CVD, PVD, and ALD. Applied Materials' metal deposition page also explains that metals are deposited using technologies such as ALD, CVD, and PVD.

CVD

Grows a film from reacting gases

Uses chemical reactions to form a film on the wafer surface. It is widely used for insulating films, conductive films, semiconductor films, and more.

PVD

Sends material flying onto the surface

Forms a film by physically sending material onto the surface. It is important for metal films and similar layers.

ALD

Stacks little by little, one atomic layer at a time

A method that makes it easier to control very thin, uniform films. It is becoming more important with 3D structures and scaling.

How are CVD, PVD, and ALD used differently?

The choice depends on the film material, thickness, uniformity, coverage over steps, process temperature, productivity, and more.

CVD uses reactive gases to form films, so it works well with many materials. Lam Research explains that CVD and ALD are used to form metal films, insulating layers, and more.

PVD is like physically sending metal material flying to form a film. It is important for metal films, but it can be hard to coat evenly deep inside narrow trenches or complex steps.

ALD builds films little by little at the atomic-layer level, so it shines with very thin films and complex 3D structures. Lam Research's explanation of ALD also describes it as a type of CVD used to form thin films.

Why deposition is difficult

If you think of deposition as "just making a film," it sounds easy. In reality, it is a very difficult process. That is because the film must have the target thickness, the target composition, and the target quality across the entire wafer.

Thickness uniformityIf the thickness differs between the center and the edge of the wafer, it affects performance and yield.
Step coverageThe film must coat the sidewalls and bottoms of trenches and holes cleanly.
Impurities and defectsEven tiny contamination or particles can cause leakage or broken wires.
Temperature limitsHigh temperatures can make quality easier to raise, but sometimes they would damage the structures underneath.
Mass productionA film you can make in a lab and a film you can make reliably in a fab are two different things.
Scaling and 3DIn structures like GAA and 3D NAND, controlling films gets even harder.

Summary: Deposition stacks functions on the wafer

Deposition is the process of making thin films with the needed properties on top of a wafer. By making insulating films, conductive films, protective films, and films to be etched later, it builds up the structure of semiconductor devices on the wafer.

The key point is that deposition alone does not complete a circuit. Cleaning prepares the surface, deposition makes the film, lithography transfers the pattern, and etching removes the unneeded parts. In this flow, deposition plays the role of "stacking functions."

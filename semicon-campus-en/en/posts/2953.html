In this lesson, we focus on hydrofluoric acid (HF), which needs special care. Based on university safety documents and other sources, we take an introductory look at why it is dangerous and what to do if it gets on your body.

① Why hydrofluoric acid needs special care

The chemicals used in cleaning are all hazardous. Among them, the hydrofluoric acid from Lesson 2 is said to need special care.

According to a University of Washington safety document (a hydrofluoric acid fact sheet), HF is a chemical that needs immediate treatment even if there is no pain. If it gets on your skin, it may not hurt right away, but it can damage deep tissue later.

A clinical guide from an Australian children's hospital (The Royal Children's Hospital) also says that HF absorbed through the skin can cause body-wide hypocalcemia (low calcium in the blood), and that blood tests and ECG monitoring may be needed.

Evidence

The University of Washington document says that concentrated HF covering about 2% or more of the body surface can be life-threatening.

Check quiz: If HF gets on your skin and it does not hurt, why do you still need treatment right away?

Answer: Even if it does not hurt right away, it can damage deep tissue later.

② Basic steps if it gets on your body

University safety documents and other sources describe steps like these.

  • Rinse right away in a safety shower (about 5 minutes). Your clothes have chemical on them, so take them off while showering, being careful not to spread the chemical
  • Apply calcium gluconate gel (about 2.5%). Until help arrives, rub it in again every 10–15 minutes
  • Even if there is no pain, always get checked at a medical facility
Let's think in numbers

If you reapply the gel every 10–15 minutes for 1 hour until help arrives, that is 60 ÷ 15 = 4 times or 60 ÷ 10 = 6 times, so 4–6 times.

About 5 minutes in the safety shower is 5 × 60 = 300 seconds. Even if it feels long, keep rinsing.

Check quiz: What is the first thing to do if HF gets on your body?

Answer: Rinse right away in a safety shower for about 5 minutes.

③ What to prepare

The documents recommend keeping calcium gluconate gel near places where HF is used. The gel also has an expiration date, so keep an eye on it. Whether it can be used right away in an emergency can change the outcome.

Why be careful with "dilute HF" too?

In Lesson 2, we saw diluted HF (about 1:50 with water). The ratio is 1 ÷ 51 ≈ about 2%. Even when dilute, it is still HF. This course does not teach that "a dilute solution is safe." Always handle it according to your workplace rules.

Check quiz: What should you keep near places where HF is used?

Answer: Calcium gluconate gel (also watch its expiration date).

④ Things to watch with other chemicals

It is not just HF. SC-1 and SC-2 in RCA cleaning are both used hot, at 70–80°C (Lesson 3). Hot liquids carry a risk of burns on top of the hazards of the chemical itself. Also, as we saw in Lesson 4, HF goes in Teflon containers, not quartz. Using the right container material is part of safety too.

What is written here is only introductory knowledge. In real work, always follow your workplace rules, the safety data sheet (SDS), and your supervisor's instructions. This article is not medical advice.

Word review

Hydrofluoric acid (HF): A chemical used to remove native oxide. If it gets on your body, it can damage deep tissue even without pain.

Calcium gluconate gel: A gel used for first aid when HF gets on the skin.

Hypocalcemia: Low calcium in the blood. It can happen when HF is absorbed through the skin.

SDS: Safety data sheet. A document that describes a chemical's hazards and first aid.

Common misconceptions

Q. If it does not hurt, do I still need to see a doctor?

Yes. The documents say you should always get checked at a medical facility, even if there is no pain.

Q. Is diluted HF safe?

No. This course does not teach that. Even when dilute, handle it according to your workplace rules.

Why do semiconductor fabs use so much water? The answer is not just "for cooling." The most important reason is to remove invisible particles, metal ions, and leftover chemicals from the wafer surface, so the next process step does not fail.

Learn through a conversation between Naoki and Professor Owl

Naoki

I heard semiconductor fabs use a huge amount of water. Honestly, at first I thought, "Do they really need to wash that much?"

Professor Owl

That feeling is worth keeping. Semiconductor cleaning is not about making things look clean. It is about not carrying anything that could ruin nanometer-scale circuits into the next process step.

Naoki

So it's less "wash it because it's dirty" and more "wash it so the next step doesn't fail."

Professor Owl

Exactly. In semiconductor manufacturing, every time a process step finishes, particles, reaction byproducts, metal contamination, and leftover chemicals may remain on the wafer surface. If we don't reduce them, the next deposition, lithography, etching, or CMP step will go wrong.

Why ordinary water won't work

The water we use at home is clean enough for people. But for semiconductors, it is still water with "too much stuff in it."

Tap water contains minerals, metal ions, organic matter, fine particles, and dissolved gases. Even if people can't see them, they can cause defects in tiny circuits.

Ordinary waterContains minerals and ions
TreatmentFiltration, ion removal, organic removal
Ultrapure waterImpurities reduced to the extreme
Wafer cleaningFewer defects in the next step

Semiconductor fabs use water with these impurities reduced as far as possible. This is "ultrapure water," or UPW for short.

Naoki

Is ultrapure water just a stronger version of clean water?

Professor Owl

That's close, but in the semiconductor world, what matters is "how close to nothing is in it." The purer the water, the more easily it picks up contamination from pipes and air. So it's not enough to make it. It has to be controlled right up until it's used.

Where does cleaning come in?

Cleaning is not a step that appears only once. It shows up again and again throughout semiconductor manufacturing.

Before depositionReduces surface contamination so films can be stacked cleanly.
After etchingReduces reaction byproducts and residue to prevent defects in the next step.
After CMPRemoves slurry and polishing debris, then passes the surface to the next step.

The key point is not to think of cleaning as "rinsing with water." Cleaning is a fairly advanced process that includes chemical treatment, ultrapure water rinsing, drying, and control of the surface condition.

Why even a little contamination is a problem

Semiconductors are built from extremely fine structures on a wafer. If a small particle lands there, the pattern may not transfer correctly in lithography, the area removed in etching may be disturbed, wiring may break, or, on the other hand, a short circuit may occur.

In other words, cleaning is not just a step to "keep things clean." It is a step that protects yield. Yield is the percentage of chips made that are good enough to use. If cleaning is not thorough, defects increase even with the same materials, the same equipment, and the same design.

Key point
In semiconductor manufacturing, invisible contamination leads to visible defects. That's why cleaning and ultrapure water are not just "unglamorous but important." They are core technologies that make manufacturing possible.

Did water play a role in TSMC choosing Kumamoto?

Yes. But this is a point to understand accurately. Water is not the "only" reason TSMC chose Kumamoto.

TSMC's water resource materials explain that JASM in Kumamoto benefits from abundant groundwater resources and is working on groundwater recharge. In addition, the official announcement by TSMC and Sony Semiconductor Solutions shows that JASM was planned to be established in Kumamoto, with Sony joining as a minority shareholder and strong support from the Japanese government.

So when thinking about the Kumamoto location, it makes sense to look at it like this.

WaterThe foundation for cleaning, cooling, and ultrapure water, which are essential to semiconductor manufacturing.
PowerA condition for stable operation of a fab that cannot stop, 24 hours a day.
SonyKumamoto has important sites related to image sensors.
Kyushu clusterThe "Silicon Island," where equipment, materials, and people gather easily.
Government supportPolicies that lower investment risk and strengthen the domestic supply chain.
Land and logisticsConditions that make it easy to build a large fab and bring in nearby suppliers.
Naoki

So saying "TSMC came to Kumamoto because of the water" is a bit too simple.

Professor Owl

Right. Water is an important condition. But choosing a fab location means looking at water, power, customers, government support, the supply chain, and people all together. What makes Kumamoto interesting is that all of these connect in one place.

Summary: Semiconductors are also a "water technology"

Semiconductor fabs don't use large amounts of water just to wash things away. They use it to return the wafer surface to a state the next step can handle, and to reduce the causes that ruin tiny circuits.

That's why you can't skip cleaning and ultrapure water if you want to understand semiconductor manufacturing. And when you look at real news like TSMC Kumamoto through the lens of water, power, customers, and the supply chain, you can see it not just as economic news but as an extension of manufacturing technology.

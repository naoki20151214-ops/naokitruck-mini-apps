What Is Semiconductor Testing? How Good and Defective Chips Are Sorted, from Wafer Test to Final Test

Even if a semiconductor looks perfect, that does not mean it will work correctly inside a smartphone or a car.

Only by sending real electrical signals into a nearly finished chip and checking the response can you judge whether it is good or bad as a circuit.

Semiconductor testing is the process of measuring electrical behavior at several stages, both on the wafer and after packaging, and picking out only the products that meet the specifications.

Bottom line: good or bad is judged by "what happens when you apply electricity"

In semiconductor testing, test equipment applies voltage, current, clocks, digital signals, and so on to the semiconductor, and measures the response that comes back. That response is compared with expected values or spec limits decided in advance to decide Pass or Fail. The main point is not just whether it looks right on the outside, but whether it works as intended as a circuit and whether its electrical characteristics are within spec.

The automated test equipment used for testing is called ATE (Automated Test Equipment). ATE is not a box that automatically understands everything and gives you the answer. It is equipment that creates an environment where you can apply the electrical stimulus a product needs and compare the measurement results. This article does not focus on specific equipment models. Instead, it focuses on how the judgments connect from the wafer to after packaging.

Where inspection ends and testing begins: Defect inspection and metrology during manufacturing look at physical conditions such as particles, patterns, and dimensions. The semiconductor testing covered here mainly uses the response to electrical signals and the electrical characteristics to judge how a product works. The two complement each other to protect quality, but they do not look at the same things.

Semiconductor testing is built on three basic actions

The first is applying an electrical stimulus to the semiconductor you want to test. The second is measuring the signals and electrical characteristics that come back as a result. The third is comparing the measurement results with expected values or spec limits. These three are repeated in the needed order, and the results are recorded and used for the judgment.

The key point here is that "it powers on, so it is good" is not always true. A chip may do one operation correctly but fail to give the expected response in another. On the other hand, even when you cannot see any difference from the outside, differences show up in the response when electricity is applied. Semiconductor testing uses these circuit-level differences to make judgments in mass production.

Wafer testing checks the dies before they are cut apart

A wafer holds many copies of the same circuit design lined up side by side. Each individual circuit area is called a die. In wafer testing, each die is electrically connected at this stage and the needed tests are run.

That connection is made by the probe card. The tiny contacts on the probe card are pressed temporarily against the pads or bumps on the wafer surface, electrically connecting the die to the ATE. It does not "stick needles into the inside of the chip." It touches the circuit's external connection points and creates the inputs and outputs for the signals needed for testing.

Once a die is connected, the ATE sends signals to it and reads the response. The results are recorded for each die and later used to decide which dies to send to the next process when the wafer is cut. By linking each position on the wafer to its test result, you can also see how good and bad dies are distributed.

Why test once while still in wafer form?

One major reason is to find clearly defective dies before they are assembled into packages. The back-end process, where the wafer is cut, the dies are picked out, connected, and assembled into packages, also takes equipment, materials, and time. If dies found to be defective at the wafer stage can be removed first, you avoid spending unnecessary back-end work on them.

This does not mean that wafer testing alone settles all of a product's quality. The role of wafer testing is to choose early which dies move on to the back-end process, and to create judgment data for use in the next step. Finding problems early and checking quality all the way to the end are separate goals, and both are pursued.

Even after passing on the wafer, chips are checked again after packaging

A die that passed wafer testing has not necessarily become a shippable product. After cutting, the die is assembled into a package and electrically connected to external terminals. The packaging process adds connections, heat, mechanical stress, and more, and new failures can occur along the way. That is why a final test is done on the finished package to check operation once again.

Wafer testing and final testing are not just the same work done twice. At the wafer stage, the purpose is to screen out defective dies early. In final testing, the purpose is to confirm that the assembled product works electrically. Even if some test items overlap, the physical state of what is tested and the point at which quality is judged are different.

So the idea that "it passed on the wafer, so no more checking is needed" is not correct. If you think of it as splitting the work between problems that can be found on the wafer and problems that must be checked after packaging, the reason for two stages becomes clear.

DFT: testing huge circuits efficiently

Today's complex semiconductors are not tested by directly touching each internal transistor one by one. Mechanisms for finding faults inside the circuit efficiently, such as Scan, ATPG, and BIST, are built in from the design stage. This approach is called DFT (Design for Test, or Design for Testability).

Scan is a structure that makes internal states easier to test. ATPG is the approach of creating test patterns that detect targeted faults. BIST is the approach of giving the circuit its own self-test mechanism. The important point is that testability itself is built into the design, rather than relying on test equipment alone to solve everything after manufacturing is finished.

It makes the needed faults easier to find, with only a limited number of pins and limited time. DFT shows that semiconductor testing is a joint effort of design, manufacturing, and testing.

Stricter judgments do not always mean higher quality

Test judgments can go wrong in two directions. A test escape is when a defective product that should fail is passed. Overkill is when a good, usable product is failed. Keeping defects from getting out is important, but if you reject too many good products as well, you lose usable products and manufacturing cost.

In production testing, you need enough coverage to find defects while also achieving short test time, low cost, and low overkill at the same time. Adding more test items and making judgments stricter does not automatically give the best result. You need to find the necessary faults while keeping time and cost at a level that can be sustained in mass production.

This balance is hard because you cannot maximize quality alone, speed alone, or cost alone. Longer test time affects how many units can be processed, and changing the judgment limits affects the number of passes and fails. What to check and where to draw the line are considered together as one system.

Binning: "sorting" that goes beyond pass/fail

Test results are not always used only for a simple Pass or Fail. Binning means sorting products by test results into groups such as pass/fail, failure type, and performance grade. Recording which bin each product went into not only helps pick shippable products, but also gives you data for checking later which types of failures are common.

Here too, the sorting is based on the test results for each product. The word "binning" alone does not mean that every product uses the same criteria or the same number of bins. What they share is the role of linking measured results to what happens to the product next.

Test data is not just for shipping decisions

Test results for each die and package are used not only to decide whether to ship, but also to learn about yield and investigate causes. Checking where, what type of failures, and how they increased gives clues that narrow down what the process side should investigate.

Testing itself does not repair defects. But if you record the measurement results and put them in a form where distributions and trends can be seen, they feed into the next manufacturing decisions. By using the data for both shipping decisions and process improvement, testing becomes more than a step that just marks pass or fail at the end of manufacturing.

Semiconductor quality is also not created by a single final test. DFT at design time, screening at the wafer stage, checks after packaging, sorting of results, and feedback to the process all link together to support the required quality and manufacturability.

Common misconceptions (FAQ)

Q. If no defects are found under a microscope, is the chip good?

A. No. Even if nothing looks wrong with the appearance or patterns, the circuit's response or electrical characteristics can be out of spec. Physical inspection and metrology and electrical testing complement each other.

Q. Does a probe card stick needles into the inside of the chip?

A. No. It mainly makes temporary contact with pads or bumps on the wafer surface to electrically connect the die to the ATE.

Q. If a chip passes wafer testing, is the final test unnecessary?

A. No. New failures can occur during packaging, so the chip also needs to be checked after assembly.

Q. If judgments are made stricter, will defects stop getting out?

A. Making them stricter alone is not enough. Overkill, which fails good chips, can also increase, so the needed defect detection, repeatability of judgments, time, and cost are considered together.

Summary: splitting roles between the wafer and after packaging

Semiconductor testing judges good and bad by comparing the response to electrical signals with expected values and specs.

Wafer testing finds defective dies early, and the final test after packaging checks the assembled product.

DFT, binning, and test data are combined to balance quality, time, cost, and overkill.

A chiplet design is an approach where, instead of building everything on one huge chip, several small dies with specific roles are connected inside one package and run as a single system. As an analogy, it is like designing a house by combining rooms that fit each purpose, rather than carving it out of one solid block.

Key points in 30 seconds
  1. A monolithic design puts the main functions on one die
  2. A chiplet design combines several dies inside one package
  3. In some cases, you can choose the best manufacturing technology for each role
  4. On the other hand, die-to-die connections, power, heat, testing, and overall yield become harder

What is a chiplet?

In semiconductor manufacturing, circuits are built on a wafer, and each piece cut out at the end is called a die. A chiplet generally means a die designed on the assumption that it will be combined with other dies to form a system, or the design approach behind it. However, the industry has not fully settled on a single usage of the term, so we do not treat "die" and "chiplet" as always meaning the same thing.

There is no single setup. Some designs combine dies with different roles, such as compute, I/O, and cache, while others line up several identical compute dies. What matters is that multiple dies work together through short connections inside the package.

How it differs from monolithic

AspectMonolithicChiplet
StructureMain functions integrated on one dieSeveral dies connected in one package
CommunicationWiring within the same dieRequires die-to-die interfaces and package wiring
Manufacturing technologyAs a rule, the same across the whole dieA different process can sometimes be chosen for each role
Design challengesDesigning and manufacturing a large dieCo-design covering connections, power, heat, testing, and assembly

Neither is always better. For small products, putting everything on one die can be simpler, and chiplets add package and connection costs. The choice depends on product size, performance, power consumption, supply, development time, and more.

Advantages of chiplets

Manufacturing technology that fits the roleIn some cases, you can choose a leading-edge process for compute circuits and a different, proven process for I/O.
Reusing building blocksVerified dies can be used across multiple products, with designs that change the number or combination of dies.
Options for scaling upInstead of relying only on one huge die, you can increase computing resources by having several dies work together.
Integrating different functionsFunctions with different characteristics, such as CPUs, accelerators, I/O, and cache, can be placed close together.
"Splitting into smaller pieces always raises yield" is not accurate. Smaller dies make it easier to limit the area affected by defects, but the final product also needs every required die to be good, and the assembly and connections to succeed. We evaluate yield and cost for the whole package, not just individual dies.

The challenges gather at the "boundaries"

Die-to-die connectionsSecure enough bandwidth while keeping latency and connection power low.
Power deliveryDeliver stable voltage to multiple dies and manage noise and voltage drop.
Thermal designLook at heat density and temperature differences between dies, and move heat out to the heat spreader.
Testing and yieldYou need to identify good dies before assembly, test connections and functions after assembly, and isolate where failures occur.

How do dies connect? UCIe and packaging

Connecting dies involves not only the electrical signaling method but also the wiring on the package, the spacing between connection points, and mechanisms for testing and management. Package technologies such as interposers, silicon bridges, organic substrates, and 2.5D/3D packaging are used depending on the product. You can review the basics in Advanced packaging that connects GPUs and HBM up close.

UCIe (Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express) is a specification that aims to standardize die-to-die connections inside a package and make them easier to interconnect. It covers the physical layer, protocols, software model, compliance testing, and more. However, UCIe is not the chiplet itself; it is one common specification for connecting dies. It also does not mean that any parts supporting the specification can be combined without conditions. Next time, we will look at UCIe in more detail.

What products use chiplets?

Typical examples include CPUs for servers and PCs, high-performance computing, and AI accelerators. They are used in designs that add more compute dies, separate I/O functions, or add large caches. Their use may expand to automotive, networking, and telecommunications in the future, but cost, reliability, and supply conditions differ for each product.

Frequently asked questions

Q. Is "chiplet" just a name for a small chip?

Size alone does not define it. What matters is a design that assumes it will be combined with other dies to form a system inside a package.

Q. Are chiplets always cheaper?

No. Die reuse and process choice can be advantages in some cases, but package, connection, testing, and design costs also go up.

Q. If a die supports UCIe, can it be freely connected to other companies' dies?

Standardization is an important condition for better interoperability, but performance, power, heat, mechanical dimensions, assembly, verification, and supply agreements also need to match.

Q. Are chiplets and advanced packaging the same thing?

No. Chiplets are a design approach that builds a system from multiple dies, while advanced packaging is a broader concept that includes the assembly technologies for placing and connecting them at high density.

Summary

  1. Chiplets are a system design that connects multiple dies in one package
  2. Role-specific manufacturing technology and reuse can be advantages, but they do not automatically lower costs
  3. The hard parts are die-to-die connections, power, heat, testing, and overall yield
  4. UCIe is a specification that aims to standardize chiplet-to-chiplet connections; it is not the chiplet itself

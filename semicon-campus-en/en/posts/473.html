Wire bonding is a back-end process that electrically connects the electrode pads on a semiconductor chip to terminals on a substrate or lead frame, using fine metal wire made of gold, copper, aluminum, or similar materials. It looks like simply attaching thin wires, but bond strength, loop shape, wire breaks, and contact with neighboring wires all affect quality.

What is wire bonding?

A semiconductor chip is fixed to a substrate or lead frame by die bonding. But just fixing it in place doesn't connect it electrically to the outside world. The circuits inside the chip need to be connected to the terminals outside the package. Wire bonding is the most common way to do this.

In wire bonding, the tiny electrode pads on the chip are connected to connection points on the substrate or lead frame with extremely fine metal wire. ASMPT describes wire bonding as one of the key interconnect technologies in electronic components. K&S also explains that in ball bonding, a small ball is formed at the tip of a fine wire made of gold, copper, or similar metal, and bonded using heat, pressure, and ultrasonic energy.

Put simply for beginners, wire bonding is "the process of creating an electrical path from the chip's circuits to the outside." If a wire breaks here, a bond is weak, or a wire touches its neighbor, it can lead to problems such as no current flow, short circuits, or lower reliability.

If you're reading on a phone, this part is enough to start. Wire bonding is "the process of connecting a chip and substrate with fine metal wire." The key quality points are preventing wire breaks, poor bonds, loop deformation, and shorts.

Where does it fit in the back-end process?

In the back-end process, the wafer is first cut into chips by dicing. Next, die bonding fixes each chip to a substrate or lead frame. After that, wire bonding, flip-chip bonding, or similar methods are used to electrically connect the chip to external terminals.

In other words, wire bonding is the "electrical connection step" that comes after die bonding. Even if a chip is fixed in the right position, it won't work as a product if the wire connections are weak. That's why the back-end flow is easier to follow if you understand the fixing step and the connecting step as a set.

Die bondingThe process of fixing diced chips to a substrate or lead frame. Position, tilt, and adhesion are controlled.
Wire bondingThe process of electrically connecting the fixed chip to external terminals with fine metal wire. It prevents wire breaks and shorts.

Basic flow of wire bonding

There are several methods of wire bonding. Here, we use the common method of ball bonding as an example and organize it into five steps for beginners.

1. Form a ball at the tip of the metal wire

First, a small ball is formed at the tip of the fine metal wire. When gold or copper wire is used, this ball is the first thing bonded to the pad on the chip. K&S explains the flow of forming this ball, bonding it to the chip, and then connecting to the substrate or lead frame.

2. Make the first bond to the pad on the chip

The chip has small electrode pads for connecting to the outside. The equipment aligns the position, presses the tip of the metal wire onto the pad, and bonds it using heat, pressure, ultrasonic energy, and so on. If this bond is weak, the wire may come off later or fail to connect electrically.

3. Form a loop and carry the wire to the substrate side

After the first bond, the equipment lifts the wire and carries it in an arc to the substrate or lead frame. The shape of this arc is called the loop. If the loop is too high, too low, or too close to a neighboring wire, it can affect molding or reliability during use.

4. Make the second bond to the substrate or lead frame

The other end of the wire is bonded to the connection point on the substrate or lead frame. Here too, the bond position, bond strength, and how the wire is cut are important. Each wire is very thin, but a product has many wires lined up, so stable, repeatable precision is required.

5. Check for breaks, height, and shape

After bonding, the wires are checked to make sure they aren't broken, that loop height and shape are stable, and that they don't touch neighboring wires or components. Various checks are done depending on the product and fab, such as visual inspection, equipment data, pull tests, and shear tests.

Differences in wire materials and methods

Wire bonding uses gold wire, copper wire, aluminum wire, and more. Which material is used depends on the product's purpose, cost, bondability, reliability, and current and heat conditions. At the beginner stage, it's enough to understand that "when the wire material changes, the bonding conditions and the quality points to watch also change."

ASMPT explains that flexible process control is becoming important in wire bonding to handle smaller, higher-density packages and new wire materials. In other words, wire bonding is a long-established process, yet it is still an area where equipment control and material support continue to advance.

Quality points to watch

Wire breaks

This is when a wire is broken and current can't flow. Some breaks can be seen visually, while some connection failures are found only through testing. Wire breaks lead directly to product malfunctions.

Poor bonds

This is when the wire is not attached well enough to the pad on the chip or the connection point on the substrate. Bonding conditions, surface condition, wire material, tool condition, and more are involved. If a bond is weak, there is a risk it will come off in later steps or during use.

Loop deformation

This is when the wire's height or shape is not stable. If the loop is too high, it can easily deform during molding. If it's too low, it may touch the chip or components. Care is also needed when it is too close to a neighboring wire.

Shorts

This is when a wire touches a place it should not connect to, causing an electrical short. The spacing between wires, loop shape, flow during molding, and component layout are involved.

Connection to yield
Wire bonding defects may be found as electrical defects in final testing. That's why it's important to look not only at visual inspection but also to connect equipment conditions, materials, pad condition, bond strength, and electrical test results.

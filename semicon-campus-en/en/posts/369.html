Failure analysis is the job of investigating "why it happened" when a defect or abnormality is found in semiconductors, and narrowing down the possible causes. A dot was found in defect inspection, a line width shifted on CD-SEM, or SPC showed unusual behavior. Failure analysis does not stop there. It considers where the cause is likely to be: the process, equipment, materials, design, or working conditions. What is failure analysis?

Failure analysis means investigating the cause of a defect that occurred in a product or process. In semiconductors, the targets include defects, scratches, residue, open circuits, shorts, film thickness shifts, line width shifts, leakage current, and abnormal electrical characteristics.

The important point here is that failure analysis is not "just finding bad chips." Defect inspection finds where defects are on the wafer. SPC checks whether measured values are as usual. Yield improvement tracks causes across the whole picture to raise the rate of good products. Within that, failure analysis looks deeply at the abnormalities found and narrows down the possible causes.

For beginners, failure analysis is "making a map for finding the cause."

Instead of jumping to conclusions from one defect, you overlay location, shape, measured values, and process history to sort out where to start investigating.

Failure analysis is not about finding, but about isolating

In a semiconductor fab, a defect does not always have just one cause. Even when defects look the same, there are many possibilities, such as leftover chemicals, particles, insufficient etching, CMP scratches, scratches during transfer, worn equipment parts, or differences between material lots.

So in failure analysis, you do not suddenly decide "this is the cause." First, you look at which lots it increased in, where on the wafer it appears most, after which process it appeared, and whether it occurs only in lots that went through the same equipment. This is how you reduce the number of possible causes.

Four kinds of evidence collected in failure analysis

In failure analysis, it is important not to judge from a single result. A defect map alone shows location trends but does not settle the cause. An image alone shows the shape of a defect but not when it started increasing. That is why you combine several kinds of evidence.

Key points of the figure
  • Defect map: see where defects are concentrated
  • Measured values: see when they started to shift
  • Image observation: see shapes, scratches, and residue
  • Process history: see equipment, materials, and conditions
Defect mapCheck whether defects are concentrated at the center of the wafer, at the edge, or in a specific pattern.
Measured valuesCheck when line width, film thickness, defect count, or electrical characteristics changed.
Image observationUse SEM and similar tools to see the shape of defects, scratches, residue, and foreign particles.
Process historyCheck the equipment used, material lots, processing conditions, and maintenance history.
Comparison samplesCompare good and defective products, and abnormal and normal lots.
Reproduction checkCheck whether the hypothesis is correct and whether the problem reproduces under the same conditions.

How SEM and FIB are combined on site

You cannot get a real feel for failure analysis just by memorizing tool names. For example, even if electrical analysis or a defect map shows that "this area looks suspicious," to actually see that spot in cross-section you need to decide the observation position, use FIB to cut into the target spot, and make it visible with SEM or TEM.

If the position is off by even a little, the cross-section of the defect you wanted to see may not appear. If you cut too much of the sample, you can lose the evidence itself. That is why failure analysis needs more than "being able to operate the equipment." Knowing where to look to get closer to the cause, and in what order to collect evidence, is what matters.

Why people who understand failure analysis are valued in the job market

People who understand failure analysis are valued not just because they can use the equipment. It is because they can look at a defect symptom and think about which evidence to collect and which process to send the check back to.

In a semiconductor fab, even a small drop in yield can greatly affect mass production and delivery dates. Changing conditions without knowing the cause can create other defects. That is why people who can link defects, measured values, images, and process history to narrow down possible causes tend to be valued in job postings for quality evaluation, process engineers, yield improvement, failure analysis, and data analysis.

What is more, failure analysis skills are not limited to semiconductor makers. Analysis using SEM, FIB, and EDS/EDX also involves equipment makers, contract analysis companies, and evaluation service companies. People who understand the equipment itself, listen to customers' analysis challenges, and suggest the best observation conditions and analysis methods can also move into jobs such as application engineer, FAE, technical support, and technical sales.

Summary

Failure analysis is the job of investigating why a defect or abnormality occurred in semiconductors and narrowing down possible causes. It takes abnormalities found through defect inspection, CD-SEM, SPC, and yield improvement and traces them back to the process, equipment, materials, design, and so on.

For beginners, it is enough to remember that "failure analysis is making a map for finding the cause." Overlay defect maps, measured values, image observation, and process history, and reduce possible causes without jumping to conclusions. Once you understand this way of thinking, jobs in inspection, quality, process improvement, and data analysis become easier to see.

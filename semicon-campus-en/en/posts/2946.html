In Lesson 5, we looked at TXRF as a way to measure metal contamination. We described it as "shining X-rays on the wafer and checking the light that comes out to find the type and amount of metal." But why can it find only the tiny amount of contamination on the wafer surface?

In this extra lesson, we look at how TXRF works and, with numbers, how small an amount it can find.

What you will learn in this article

  • What X-ray fluorescence is
  • Why the X-rays hit at a very shallow angle (total reflection)
  • The detection limit of TXRF (how small an amount it can find)
  • What TXRF is good and bad at (light elements)

① What does a TXRF tool do?

In Lesson 5, we looked at TXRF as a way to measure metal contamination. The full name is total reflection X-ray fluorescence analysis. The name is long, so let's go through how it works step by step.

When you shine X-rays on a material, the elements in it give off light that is specific to each element (fluorescent X-rays). The type of light tells you "which element," and its strength tells you "how much." This is the basic idea of X-ray fluorescence analysis.

Quick quiz: What can fluorescent X-rays tell you?

Answer: The type of element (from the type of light) and the amount (from the strength of the light).

② Looking only at the surface with "total reflection"

Contamination on a wafer sits only on the surface, in tiny amounts. But if you shine X-rays in the normal way, they go deep into the wafer, and a strong signal also comes from the silicon inside. Then the weak signal from the small amount of surface contamination gets drowned out.

So TXRF shines the X-rays on the wafer surface at a very shallow angle. Below a certain angle (the critical angle), almost all of the X-rays are reflected and do not enter the wafer. This is total reflection. Because the X-rays do not go in, little signal comes from inside the wafer, and only the surface contamination shows up clearly.

Evidence

An introduction page from SSRL (Stanford Synchrotron Radiation Lightsource) in the US shows an example of measuring tiny amounts of impurities on a silicon wafer, with the X-rays coming in at 0.085 degrees, a shallow angle that causes total reflection.

Let's think in numbers

0.085 degrees is about 1/12 of a degree (1 ÷ 0.085 ≈ 11.8). Even 1 degree is so shallow that it looks almost parallel to the ground. TXRF shines X-rays at about 1/12 of that, a very shallow angle.

Quick quiz: Why does TXRF shine X-rays at a very shallow angle?

Answer: So the X-rays do not enter the wafer (total reflection), and only the signal from the surface contamination shows up clearly.

③ How small an amount of contamination can it find?

In Lesson 5, we wrote that the detection limit of TXRF is 10^9 to 10^10 atoms per square centimeter. The numbers vary by source. Here are some examples.

  • In a table in one review (a Rigaku journal), standard TXRF is 10^9 atoms and VPD-ICP-MS is 10^7 atoms (both per square centimeter)
  • A page introducing a 1989 paper says a standard tool can go down to 10^11 atoms
  • In the SSRL example using synchrotron radiation (very strong X-rays), nickel can be found down to 8×10^7 atoms (1,000-second measurement)
Let's think in numbers

Comparing TXRF (10^9 atoms) and VPD-ICP-MS (10^7 atoms): 10^9 ÷ 10^7 = 10^2 = 100 times. By this math, VPD-ICP-MS can find amounts 100 times smaller.

In Lesson 5, we saw that there are about 7×10^14 silicon atoms per square centimeter. If there are 10^9 metal atoms, then 7×10^14 ÷ 10^9 = 7×10^5. That means 1 metal atom for about 700,000 silicon atoms.

The international standard ISO 14706:2014 sets out how to measure contamination on silicon wafer surfaces with TXRF. According to the standard, it covers elements with atomic numbers 16 (sulfur) to 92 (uranium), over a range of 10^10 to 10^14 atoms per square centimeter. With a pretreatment called VPD (vapor phase decomposition), even smaller amounts can be measured. (VPD generally means collecting the contamination on the wafer surface into a small droplet to concentrate it before measuring.)

Why is TXRF bad at light elements?

As the standard covers only sulfur (atomic number 16) and heavier, elements lighter than sulfur (atomic number 16) cannot be measured well with TXRF. So, as we saw in Lesson 5, light elements are measured with a different method (SIMS).

Quick quiz: What kind of elements can TXRF not measure well?

Answer: Light elements (the standard starts at sulfur, atomic number 16). Light elements are measured with methods such as SIMS.

Key points of this extra lesson

TXRF shines X-rays at a very shallow angle so they are totally reflected, and checks only the metals on the wafer surface. The fluorescent X-rays from each element tell you the type and amount. A rough guide for the detection limit is around 10^9 atoms per square centimeter (it varies by source). TXRF is bad at light elements, which are measured with other methods.

Word review

Fluorescent X-rays: When X-rays hit a material, each element gives off its own specific light (X-rays). The word means this effect, or the light itself.

Total reflection: When light or X-rays hit at a very shallow angle, almost all of it is reflected and does not go in.

Critical angle: The boundary angle at which total reflection starts.

Detection limit: The smallest amount a measurement can find.

VPD (vapor phase decomposition): A pretreatment that collects and concentrates the contamination on the wafer surface before measuring.

Common misconceptions

Q. Does TXRF destroy the wafer to measure it?

No. As we saw in Lesson 5, it can measure without damaging the wafer.

Q. Is the detection limit of TXRF one fixed number?

No. The number varies with the source, the tool, the element measured, the type of X-rays (synchrotron radiation or not), and whether pretreatment is used. In this article, we listed each source's numbers as they are.

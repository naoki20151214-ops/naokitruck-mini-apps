Semiconductor job postings can be quite hard for beginners to read. They are full of words like "process," "FSE," "tool installation," "yield," "shift work," and "cleanroom." In this article, we gently explain where people new to semiconductors, or people thinking about changing jobs, should look when reading a semiconductor job posting.

Don't read a job posting top to bottom; split it into "5 sections"

  1. Job content: Is it closest to manufacturing, equipment, quality, materials, or process?
  2. Required experience: Separate required and preferred qualifications, and look for words close to your experience
  3. Work style: Check work location, shift work, night shift, business travel, and relocation
  4. Training: If it says "no experience needed," check what the training and OJT actually involve
  5. Growth path: See whether the experience leads to other jobs or a better understanding of the process

After reading a job posting, compare real job postings

Once you know the points covered in this article, try comparing job postings for "manufacturing," "equipment maintenance," "quality," "materials," and more on job sites or with recruiting agents. If some conditions are hard to judge on your own, checking them during a consultation makes mismatches less likely.

If you're looking for manufacturing, inspection, or equipment maintenance jobs

If you're entering the semiconductor industry with no experience, manufacturing operator, inspection, and equipment maintenance jobs are options, not just research or design jobs. It's easier to search if you first look at the differences between entry points and then choose a job service.

1. Look at the job content: which entry point is it?

The first thing to look at is the job content. Even when they all say "semiconductor," semiconductor jobs split into fab manufacturing operator, equipment maintenance, field service, quality evaluation, materials development, process development, and more.

Manufacturing and inspection jobs

Postings that often use words like equipment operation, cleanroom, inspection, records, and shift work.

These are often easier entry points for people with no experience, but check the work schedule and assigned process carefully.

Equipment and maintenance jobs

Postings that often use words like equipment maintenance, maintenance, tool installation, troubleshooting, and FSE.

For people with mechanical, electrical, tool, or maintenance experience, this area connects easily to what they've done.

Postings that often use words like quality assurance, reliability evaluation, measurement, analysis, defect inspection, and yield.

2. Look at required experience: separate required and preferred

The requirements in a job posting include "required" and "preferred" items. A common mistake for beginners is thinking they can't apply unless they meet every preferred item too.

Of course, each posting needs its own judgment, but as a way to read it, look at the required items first. Then look at the preferred items and search for words close to your experience. For example, even without semiconductor experience, manufacturing, maintenance, inspection, quality, chemistry, mechanical, electrical, or data analysis experience may be treated as related.

Reading tip
A posting that says "semiconductor experience required" and one that says "manufacturing experience a plus," "mechanical or electrical knowledge a plus," or "quality control experience a plus" are at different distances for someone new to semiconductors. In a job posting, look for which words your experience is close to, rather than semiconductor experience itself.

3. Look at work style: location, shift work, and travel

In semiconductor jobs, checking the work style is as important as the job content. Especially in manufacturing, equipment, and FSE jobs, your daily rhythm changes a lot depending on the work schedule and whether there's business travel.

Work style is as important as job content.
In a job posting, check not only "what you'll do" but also "where, during what hours, and under what employment type you'll work." If you leave this vague, the job content may fit but the daily rhythm may not.

Work locationCheck whether it's factory-based, at a customer site, on-site resident work, or involves business travel.
Working hoursCheck for day shift, 2-shift, 3-shift, night shift, and holiday work.
AssignmentThe work changes depending on front-end process, back-end process, equipment maker, materials maker, and so on.

4. Look at training: check what "no experience needed" really means

Even when a posting says "no experience needed," the training differs by job. Check whether there's training after joining, whether it's mainly OJT, whether it starts with cleanroom and safety training, and who will teach you after you're assigned.

The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's job tag also describes semiconductor manufacturing as work that makes semiconductors by operating and monitoring facilities and equipment. Because you handle various tools in a cleanroom and affect quality control and yield, following procedures, keeping accurate records, and reporting problems are important.

5. Look at the growth path: will the experience lead somewhere?

When looking at a job posting, look not only at the work right after joining but also at how that experience can expand. For example, a manufacturing operator may move into inspection, equipment maintenance, quality, or process improvement. Some people go from equipment maintenance to FSE or tool installation.

Semiconductor engineering work spans a wide range of areas, including process, design, equipment, and materials. Even if your first entry point is manufacturing, the understanding of processes and equipment you gain there helps you understand the next job type.

Notes to make before applying

After reading a job posting, making notes like the following before you apply makes it easier to consult a career service or recruiting agent.

In particular, "It says no experience needed, but am I really eligible?", "What is the actual rhythm of the shift work?", and "Will this experience lead to my next job?" are hard to judge alone. If you still have concerns after reading a posting, having a recruiting agent or job service review your documents and check conditions can reduce mismatches before you apply.

Summary: a job posting is a tool for finding "your entry point"

A semiconductor job posting looks hard if you try to understand it all at once. But if you split it into job content, required experience, work style, training, and growth path, it becomes much easier to read.

  • Read a job posting starting with the job content
  • Separate required and preferred qualifications
  • If it says "no experience needed," check the training too
  • Check work location, shift work, and business travel
  • See whether the experience can expand to other job types

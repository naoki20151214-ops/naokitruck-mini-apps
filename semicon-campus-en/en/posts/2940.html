① When and where was RCA cleaning born?

"RCA" is the name of an American company (Radio Corporation of America). Werner Kern, who worked at this company, created the basic procedure around 1965. He wrote it up with his colleague D. Puotinen in a 1970 paper.

Evidence

According to an article by Professor J. Ruzyllo of Penn State University in the US (The Electrochemical Society Interface, Spring 2010), the 1970 paper was the first systematic cleaning method based on hydrogen peroxide (a chemical that oxidizes things). It used a two-step approach of "oxidation and complexing": treatment with (1) a high-pH alkaline solution, then (2) a low-pH acidic solution. The point that it was developed around 1965 comes from the "RCA clean" entry in the encyclopedia Wikipedia.

"Oxidation" means combining a substance with oxygen to change its properties. A "complex," as we saw in Lesson 3, is a form in which a metal dissolves easily in a liquid. In other words, this two-step approach was based on the idea of "changing the contamination, dissolving it in the liquid, and removing it."

It was originally meant to remove organic films and metals. However, it turned out that the first alkaline solution (SC-1) was also very effective at removing particles, and it is still the foundation of cleaning today. Professor Ruzyllo says, in effect, that SC-1 "is still the cornerstone of every silicon cleaning procedure."

Let's think in numbers

The paper came out in 1970. Counting from 2026, 2026 − 1970 = 56 years. Even after more than half a century, the basic idea is still in use.

Quick quiz: What does "RCA" in RCA cleaning stand for?

Answer: It is the name of an American company, Radio Corporation of America.

② Why can SC-1 remove particles?

Particles stick to the surface of the wafer. To remove them, you need to either "beat the force that holds them" or "make them less likely to stick." For SC-1, the second explanation is well known.

The key term is zeta potential. It is the degree of electrical charge on the surface of an object in a liquid. And objects with the same type of charge repel each other.

Why does SC-1 make particles come off more easily?

According to the "RCA clean" entry on Wikipedia, SC-1 changes the zeta potential of both the wafer surface and the particle surface so that they repel each other, which is why it can remove even particles that do not dissolve in water. When they repel each other, a particle that has come off is less likely to stick again.

In addition, the hydrogen peroxide in SC-1 forms a thin oxide film of about 1 nanometer on the wafer surface. Because the ammonia slowly dissolves this film and the surface, you can also see it as particles coming off together with the film. (Explanations of this point differ from source to source, so we present it here as one view.)

Let's think in numbers

1 millimeter is 1,000,000 nanometers. So 1 nanometer is 0.000001 millimeters. An oxide film "about 1 nanometer" thick is about as thin as a few stacked layers of atoms.

Quick quiz: "Zeta potential" is the degree of what?

Answer: The degree of electrical charge on the surface of an object in a liquid. Objects with the same type of charge repel each other.

③ The wafer surface after SC-1 and SC-2

According to the "RCA clean" entry on Wikipedia, after SC-1, a small amount of metals such as iron remains. SC-2 removes it. This is the reason "the order matters," as we saw in Lesson 3. After SC-2, a thin protective film remains on the surface and protects it from contamination. That is because bare silicon is very reactive and gets contaminated right away.

When you deliberately want to remove that thin oxide film, you use dilute hydrofluoric acid (HF). According to the same entry, a solution of HF and water mixed at 1:50 or 1:100 is used at 25°C for about 15 seconds. It can remove the thin oxide film and some ionic contamination.

Why do you need to be careful after HF?

Because bare silicon gets contaminated very easily. Unless you use very high-purity chemicals and clean containers, contamination may stick again to the surface you worked to clean (recontamination). This is the flip side of what we saw in Lesson 2: "native oxide forms again right away."

Let's think in numbers

If you mix HF and water at 1:100, the share of HF is 1 ÷ 101 ≈ about 1%. At 1:50, it was 1 ÷ 51 ≈ about 2%. Also, the HF treatment takes about 15 seconds. Compared with about 10 minutes (600 seconds) for SC-1, that is 15 ÷ 600 = 1/40 of the time.

Quick quiz: Why is it a problem when metals such as iron remain after SC-1?

Answer: Because metals change how electricity flows in silicon and shift the characteristics of transistors. That is why SC-2 removes them.

④ How has today's cleaning changed from RCA?

According to Professor Ruzyllo's article (2010), cleaning is thought to make up at least one third of all process steps in advanced manufacturing. (The exact number has not been published, so it is an estimate.) That means the share has grown even further from about one fifth (about 80 out of 400 steps) in the 0.18-micrometer generation that we saw in Lesson 1.

The same article also says that today's cleaning is not that far from the original RCA recipe, but it has the following features.

  • Wide use of dilute chemicals (the IMEC method in Lesson 3 is an example)
  • Simplifying the procedure as much as possible
  • A focus on single-wafer cleaning, which cleans wafers one at a time
  • Wide use of ozonated water (water with ozone dissolved in it)

It also says that because tiny structures break very easily and new materials have increased, the role of dry cleaning, which uses no liquid, will grow. Examples given include supercritical CO2 (carbon dioxide put into a state between liquid and gas using pressure and temperature), cleaning by spraying tiny cryogenic nitrogen particles, and cleaning with low-pressure HF vapor.

Let's think in numbers

One third of 400 steps is 400 ÷ 3 ≈ 133 steps. The same article also says that the particles controlled today are two orders of magnitude smaller (1/100) than 20 years ago. For example, if you worried about 100-nanometer particles 20 years ago, today you worry about particles of about 1 nanometer. Metal contamination is also controlled down to levels two orders of magnitude lower.

Quick quiz: Which of these are described as features of today's cleaning? (dilute chemicals, single-wafer cleaning, ozonated water, heavy use of concentrated chemicals)

Answer: Dilute chemicals, single-wafer cleaning, and wide use of ozonated water. Heavy use of concentrated chemicals is not mentioned.

Key points of this extra lesson

RCA cleaning is a two-step approach, alkaline SC-1 and acidic SC-2, created by Kern in the 1960s and spread by a 1970 paper. SC-1 also works on particles, which it was not originally aimed at, and is still the foundation. However, today's cleaning is shifting toward dilute chemicals, single-wafer cleaning, ozonated water, and dry cleaning.

Word review

RCA: The name of an American company. RCA cleaning is the cleaning method born there.

Oxidation: Combining a substance with oxygen to change its properties.

Zeta potential: The degree of electrical charge on the surface of an object in a liquid.

Single-wafer cleaning: A method that cleans wafers one at a time.

Supercritical CO2: Carbon dioxide put into a state between liquid and gas using pressure and temperature.

Common misconceptions

Q. Is RCA cleaning old and no longer used?

No. According to the article, the chemicals used in cleaning today are not that far from the original RCA recipe. It is still used as a foundation, with the chemicals diluted and the procedure simplified.

Q. Is SC-1 a solution for removing metals?

Its original main targets were organic films and metals. However, it turned out to be very effective at removing particles too, and today it is also well known for its role in removing particles.

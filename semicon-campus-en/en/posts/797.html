SK hynix to mass-produce HBM4E in the US, building an AI memory supply chain with an Indiana plant worth over $4 billion

A plan to assemble and supply HBM, which powers computing in AI servers, in the US is now underway.

SK hynix is building an advanced packaging site for AI memory in Indiana, investing over $4 billion, and aims to mass-produce next-generation HBM there.

The key point is not just that a new plant is being built. In AI chips, what matters is not only the computing chip but also HBM, which sits close to it and feeds it huge amounts of data, and the advanced packaging that puts them together.

SK hynix's Indiana site will be part of a supply chain that handles HBM packaging, testing, and R&D close to its North American customers. Reuters reported that the company plans to start mass production of HBM4E in the third quarter of 2029, and expects tight memory supply to continue through the end of 2030.

What happened?

SK hynix is building an advanced packaging production site for AI memory in West Lafayette, Indiana. According to SK hynix's official announcement, the investment is over $4 billion, and the site is planned to be the company's first next-generation HBM production base in the US.

The company held the groundbreaking ceremony on August 27, 2026 (local time) at Purdue University. The site is planned to include not only HBM production lines but also an R&D testbed for advanced packaging. SK hynix has said it will supply US customers with next-generation HBM that is packaged and tested locally, starting in the second half of 2029.

Reuters reported a more specific start date for HBM4E mass production: the third quarter of 2029. The official "second half of 2029" and Reuters' "third quarter" describe the same plan at different levels of detail. However, since this is a plan, it does not rule out changes to the actual start date or production volume.

What is HBM?

HBM stands for High Bandwidth Memory. It is a high-bandwidth memory that delivers large amounts of data in a short time to computing chips such as AI accelerators. It stacks several DRAM chips vertically and connects them over short distances to create a wide data path.

The important point is that HBM performance is not decided by the memory chips alone. Product performance and reliability depend on how the stacked DRAM is connected, how electrical signals pass through, how heat is removed, and whether it runs stably in the final package.

So the Indiana site is not a "DRAM wafer fab" in the usual sense. It is an advanced packaging and test site that finishes HBM into products. "HBM mass production" in the title means the mass production process of assembling, inspecting, and supplying HBM products to customers. The front-end process that makes wafers and the back-end process that assembles chips need to be thought of separately.

Why Indiana?

According to SK hynix's official explanation, the Indiana site is close to US customers, research institutions, and partner companies, and will play a role in linking AI memory supply with R&D.

AI chips are not done once a design goes into mass production. They need tuning for the customer's computing chip and package, prototype evaluation, reliability testing, and quality control during mass production. If packaging and testing can be done near customers, design changes and test results can get back to production faster.

A local production site also helps spread the supply chain across regions. SK hynix's official announcement positions the Indiana site as a base for strengthening the US AI memory supply chain. However, this alone does not mean all US AI chips will be made entirely within the country. Memory wafers, base dies, equipment, materials, and assembly on the customer side all link processes across multiple regions to make a product.

What stage is HBM4E at?

In June 2026, SK hynix officially announced that it had shipped samples of 12-layer next-generation HBM4E to major customers. Sample shipment is the stage where customers evaluate the product and work out the conditions for mass production. Shipping samples to customers is not the same as a steady supply of mass-produced products on the market.

According to the company's official explanation, 12-layer HBM4E offers 48 GB of capacity and data processing speeds of up to 16 Gbps per pin, and improves power efficiency by more than 20% over the previous model. These figures are for SK hynix's HBM4E product and should not be applied directly to other companies' products or future mass production conditions.

In addition, the more layers HBM4E has, the harder it gets to keep the joints stable, manage thermal resistance, and inspect it. SK hynix says it uses an encapsulation and bonding technology called Advanced MR-MUF to improve structural stability and heat dissipation. In the race for mass production, you have to look beyond top speed to yield (making good products consistently), reliability, and customer qualification.

Will the memory shortage last until 2030?

Reuters reported that SK hynix's CEO suggested that, driven by AI demand, customer demand could keep exceeding supply capacity beyond the end of 2030. This is the company's outlook on future supply and demand, not a confirmed forecast that there will definitely be a shortage until 2030.

Increasing HBM supply requires not only DRAM production capacity but also capacity for stacking, bonding, packaging, and testing. Even after a new site is announced, installing equipment, ramping up processes, getting customer qualification, and improving mass production yield all take time. That is why the 2029 mass production plan should be seen less as news that will quickly increase supply now, and more as a move to prepare the AI memory supply chain several years ahead.

Public information alone cannot tell us how much the Indiana site will actually supply, which customers it will serve, or how it will share work with existing sites in Korea. Going forward, we need to check construction progress, cleanroom and equipment operation, the move from samples to mass production, and customer adoption announcements, one by one.

How will the US AI memory supply chain change?

This site is not just one more location for an HBM maker. It also gives us material to think about where the processes of AI chips are linked together.

In AI servers, computing chips, HBM, substrates, power, cooling, and software all work as one system. Even if only HBM gets faster, the system as a whole cannot reach its full performance if connections and thermal design cannot keep up. The plan to do advanced packaging and testing in Indiana points toward making it easier for the memory maker and North American customers to fine-tune product combinations close to each other.

On the other hand, a stronger supply chain does not immediately mean lower prices or an end to shortages. HBM4E mass production is planned for 2029, and current product supply needs to be kept separate from future capacity expansion. It is important not to judge competitiveness by the size of the investment alone, but to also look at product quality, mass production yield, customer qualification, and continuity of supply.

Common misconceptions

Once the Indiana plant is built, will all HBM be made in the US?

Not necessarily. The main functions shown in the official announcement are advanced packaging, testing, and R&D for AI memory. HBM products are completed through processes and companies linked across multiple regions, so you need to look at the site's role process by process.

Is HBM4E already in mass production?

SK hynix has shipped 12-layer HBM4E samples to major customers. HBM4E mass production in Indiana is a future plan, which Reuters reported as planned for Q3 2029. It is important not to confuse sample shipments with the start of mass production.

Has it been decided that the memory shortage will last until 2030?

No. The possibility of tight supply through the end of 2030 is SK hynix's outlook, as reported by Reuters. Actual supply and demand will change with AI investment, equipment ramp-up, customer demand, yield, and more, so read it as a forecast, not a confirmed fact.

When you look at the chips in smartphones and computers, you may wonder why they are almost all made of silicon.

The reason is not "because silicon conducts electricity the best."
Silicon is not perfect for everything, and for some uses other semiconductors have the advantage.
Even so, it became the mainstream because the oxide film that can be grown on its surface, wafers that can be mass-produced, and the manufacturing ecosystem all came together.

The short answer: Silicon has properties that make electricity easy to control at room temperature, and it can form high-quality silicon dioxide (SiO2) on its own surface through thermal oxidation. This film supported surface protection, insulation, choosing where to add impurities, and MOS transistors. On top of that, the technology for purifying silicon, growing single crystals, and mass-producing large-diameter wafers matured, and equipment, materials, design, fabs, and talent all came to be organized around silicon.

Silicon is a semiconductor that is "just right" to control

What matters in a semiconductor is not just that it can carry a lot of current. It is that you can switch between a state where current flows and a state where it hardly flows, using voltage, temperature, light, or impurities. This "controllable" property is what a transistor needs to work as a switch.

Silicon's band gap is about 1.12 eV near room temperature. The band gap is the energy difference an electron needs to move into a state where it can move easily. If it is too small, heat at room temperature increases the number of carriers, which tends to increase leakage when the device is turned off. On the other hand, a larger band gap is not always better; it has to work together with contacts, doping, insulating films, and processing methods.

Silicon also has a mass-production advantage: it is easy to make both n-type and p-type regions. By adding tiny amounts of impurities, you can make electrons the main carriers or make holes the main carriers, which leads to CMOS, which combines nMOS and pMOS.

One of the biggest deciding factors: SiO2 can be grown on the surface

When silicon is heated to a high temperature in an oxygen or water vapor atmosphere, a film of silicon dioxide, or SiO2, grows on its surface. Rather than just attaching a separate film from outside, it forms an interface by consuming the substrate itself. This was important for manufacturing with silicon.

This oxide film has several roles. First, it protects the semiconductor surface. If the surface is exposed, contamination and surface conditions disturb the electrical characteristics, leading to leakage and lower reliability. Second, the oxide film works as an insulating film that does not conduct electricity easily. Third, if you leave the film in some areas and open it in others, you can use it as a mask to choose where impurities go.

In other words, SiO2 is not just a "white film." It plays the roles of protective film, processing boundary, and insulating film all at once, and it supported planar technology, which builds circuits up on a flat surface.

SiO2 led to MOS and planar technology

MOS stands for Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor. A metal or conductive gate, an oxide film, and a semiconductor are stacked, and the gate voltage controls the carriers at the semiconductor surface. Because voltage can be applied across the oxide film, a channel—the path for current—can be created or removed.

Using the oxide film as a mask, you can also introduce impurities into only part of the substrate. This made it easier to place n-type and p-type regions on a flat surface and integrate transistors and wiring on the same wafer. The key point is that SiO2 did not just play one role; it supported protection, selective processing, insulation, and the MOS interface all together.

In modern advanced CMOS, high-k materials and others are also used for the gate insulator. But that does not mean SiO2 has become meaningless. The accumulated know-how in silicon substrates, Si-based channels, interface layers, and process integration including oxidation and cleaning carries over into today's manufacturing.

By now, you can see that silicon is not "a material chosen only for its electrical performance." Next, let's look at the path the material takes to reach the fab.

Purifying silicon and turning it into single-crystal wafers

In nature, silicon exists as SiO2 in sand and quartz, or as silicates. But sand is not carried straight into a semiconductor fab. First, metallurgical silicon is made from the raw material, and then it is brought closer to high-purity polycrystalline silicon for semiconductors through chemical refining, such as conversion to chlorosilanes and distillation.

Next, a single-crystal ingot is made. In the common CZ (Czochralski) method, high-purity polycrystalline silicon is melted in a crucible, a seed crystal is touched to the surface of the melt, and it is slowly pulled up while rotating. Because the silicon solidifies with the same crystal orientation as the seed crystal, a single crystal with neatly aligned atoms grows.

The ingot's outer diameter is trimmed, it is sliced thin, and the surface is polished to make wafers. A wafer is the base on which circuits are built. People often confuse wafers with the chips themselves, but many chips are made on one wafer and then cut apart at the end.

Large-diameter wafers support mass production and cost

A larger wafer makes it easier to get more chips from a single wafer. The work of handling and processing does not simply drop to zero, but each time the equipment runs, the process is shared across more dies, which helps lower the cost per chip.

However, moving to larger wafers is not just about increasing the diameter. Temperature uniformity during crystal growth, defects, flatness, thin-film thickness, lithography, handling, and inspection all have to be kept consistent. Because the technology to reliably make high-purity, large-diameter single crystals built up for silicon, it became the mass-production foundation for general-purpose logic and memory.

Germanium came first, but silicon became the mainstream

In the earliest transistors, germanium was an important material. Germanium has high mobility, and early crystal growth and refining technology for it was also ahead. However, its band gap is smaller than silicon's, so thermally excited carriers increase easily at high temperatures, which tends to be a disadvantage for leakage and operating temperature.

Silicon not only had a larger band gap but could also build a stable oxide film into the manufacturing process. Oxide masking and the planar process protected the junctions and opened the way to processing and wiring from one side. Here, silicon changed from "a material for a single transistor" into "a platform for making integrated circuits in large volumes."

Silicon is not perfect for everything

Silicon being mainstream does not mean it is the best for every use. For high-voltage, high-temperature, high-speed power conversion, wide-band-gap SiC and GaN can have the advantage. For high frequencies and light emission, compound semiconductors such as GaAs are sometimes chosen.

Also, modern advanced chips are not purely "silicon only." They combine many materials, such as SiGe, high-k insulating films, metal gates, wiring metals, and low-k (low dielectric constant) materials. Even so, the accumulated Si-based know-how remains in the base wafer, the channel, and the coordination of process, equipment, and design, so silicon stays at the center as a platform.

The strength of the material was joined by decades of industry build-up

A semiconductor is not a product you can mass-produce at the same quality the day after choosing a material. It only becomes a product when a continuous chain is in place: high-purity raw materials, crystal growth, wafer polishing, deposition, lithography, etching, cleaning, inspection, packaging, design rules, EDA, fab recipes, and engineers' experience.

For silicon, that continuous chain of processes was improved over several decades. Yields rose, supply chains for equipment and materials were established, designers could use existing rules and IP, and fabs gained quality control methods. Even if a new material is better in some aspects of performance, replacing this whole system at once is not easy.

Summary: silicon was chosen not for being "the best" but for how everything connects

Conclusions of this article
  • Silicon has moderate properties that make electricity easy to control at room temperature.
  • SiO2, grown by thermal oxidation, supported surface protection, masking, insulation, and MOS.
  • Technologies for purification, single-crystal growth, and mass production of large-diameter wafers matured.
  • Equipment, materials, design, fabs, talent, and quality control built up around Si.
  • SiC, GaN, GaAs, and others complement silicon depending on the application.

Silicon became the standard for semiconductors not because it ranked first in a simple ranking of physical performance. Its ability to grow a good oxide film on its surface led to MOS and planar technology, and from there it spread to high-purity wafers, mass-production equipment, design environments, and supply chains.

In other words, silicon's strength is not "the material alone" but "an unbroken path from material to product."

Common questions

Is silicon the semiconductor that conducts electricity best?

No. In terms of electron mobility alone, germanium and GaAs are well-known examples that are higher. Silicon became the mainstream because of its overall balance of electrical characteristics, oxide film, processing, mass production, cost, and reliability.

Are CPUs made directly from sand?

No. It is sometimes described as "from sand" in the sense that quartz and silica can be the starting point, but in reality many steps are needed, including reduction, chemical refining, ultra-high purification, single-crystal growth, wafer processing, microfabrication, and inspection.

Is SiO2 the only reason for silicon?

SiO2 is one of the historical deciding factors, but not the only reason. The build-up of purification, large single crystals, CMOS, yield, equipment, design environments, and supply chains was also essential.

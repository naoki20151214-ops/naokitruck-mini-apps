Imagine you step on a scale every morning and write down the number. Your weight goes up a little or down a little each day. That is your "normal."

But one day, it is suddenly much higher than usual. Or it keeps going up a little every day for 10 days. In both cases, you can notice that something is "different from usual."

Semiconductor fabs do the same thing. They line up the numbers measured in a process in time order and watch whether things are "normal." Here is the short answer: a warning sign is not only when a point goes outside the lines, but also when the pattern of points shows a "habit" even inside the lines.

The body weight example fits only in one way: "you notice a difference by comparing it with the usual range." Body weight and semiconductor processes are different things.

SPC is a way to watch "is it normal?" with numbers

SPC stands for Statistical Process Control. Statistics is a way of making judgments using the variation in numbers.

A handbook from the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) explains the basic idea behind SPC as "comparing what is happening now with what happened before." You first study from past data how the process usually behaves. Then you check whether newly measured data falls within that range.

The same handbook also introduces a paired idea: "statistical quality control (SQC)." Its purpose is to confirm that products being shipped meet the specs set by the customer. In other words, SPC watches "how the process is doing," while SQC confirms "whether products meet specs."

A control chart is a chart of points lined up in time order

A typical tool used in SPC is the "control chart." According to ASQ (American Society for Quality), a control chart is a graph for studying how a process changes over time. Measured values are plotted as points in time order.

The chart always has three lines. The "center line" in the middle shows the average. The upper line is called the "upper control limit (UCL)," and the lower line is called the "lower control limit (LCL)." These three lines are set from past data.

This chart shows two things. One is the "average." ASQ compares it to target shooting: where the shots cluster is the average. The other is the "range," which shows how tightly the shots are grouped. A control chart watches both the "average" and the "range."

For example, if you plot one point per day for values measured under the same conditions every day, you get a control chart.

  • Left: the average has shifted (shots cluster away from the center)
  • Right: the range has widened (shots scatter widely)

"Normal variation" and "warning signs"

Even when things are made under the same conditions, the measured numbers change a little every time. The NIST handbook distinguishes between variation caused only by chance causes and variation caused by an assignable cause.

Control limits are set so that, while the process is "normal," almost all points fall inside the upper and lower lines. If a point goes outside the lines, there may be an "assignable cause," so you investigate.

Even inside the lines, watch for "habits" in the pattern

If the points are inside the lines, is everything fine? ASQ lists "out-of-control signs" to watch for even inside the lines. For example:

  • One point falls outside a control limit.
  • Eight points in a row fall on the same side of the center line.
  • Two out of three points in a row are on the same side of the center line and farther than "2σ (sigma)."
  • Four out of five points in a row are on the same side of the center line and farther than "1σ."

σ (sigma) is a number called the "standard deviation," which shows the size of the variation. In a control chart, you think about a point's position by how many σ it is from the center line.

Key points of the diagram

  • All points are inside the lines
  • But 8 points in a row are on the same side of the center line
  • A habit in the pattern is also a sign

"Eight in a row on the same side" is like "kept going up for 10 days" in the body weight example. Even if each value is within the usual range, a "habit" in the pattern suggests that something is starting to change.

The limits are at "3 sigma," but false alarms still happen once in a while

Control limits are usually placed 3σ from the center line. These are called "3-sigma limits." According to the NIST handbook, if the variation follows a normal distribution (a symmetrical, hill-shaped distribution), the chance of a point falling outside the lines is 0.27% even when nothing in the process has changed.

From this probability, on average about 1 in 371 points will "fall outside the lines even though nothing happened." This is called a "false alarm." So, when a sign appears, do not jump to "it's broken." Investigate first.

What do semiconductor fabs watch?

In semiconductor processes, too, measured numbers are plotted on control charts. For example, according to Hitachi High-Tech, resist dimensions after development and line widths after etching are measured with an electron microscope called a CD-SEM. It says this measurement has little "variation from one measurement to the next," with repeatability of about 1% of the measured dimension at 3σ standard deviation.

For example, if you plot the measured line width as one point per measurement in time order, it becomes a control chart as is. You can also notice changes such as line widths slowly getting wider by looking at the pattern.

According to ASML, focus and layer alignment (overlay) are also monitored by measuring test patterns printed on the wafer. The results are sent from tools on the production line back to the exposure tool and used for correction. The purpose of monitoring is to raise yield (the share of good products).

What do you do when a sign appears?

ASQ explains the steps to take when an out-of-control sign appears. Mark the point and investigate the cause. Then record how you investigated, what you found, what the cause was, and how you fixed it.

Also, when you have just started a new control chart, the lines set from the first 20 points are temporary. Once you have 20 or more points from a period when the process is running "normally," recalculate the lines.

Equipment adjustment and recovery are done according to the official procedures of your fab or equipment maker, under the judgment of authorized people. This article introduces a way of looking at the data; it does not give instructions for operating equipment.

Word review

Here are the words from this article again, in simple terms. If something feels hard, come back to this list and read it again.

  • SPC: Statistical process control. A way to watch "is it normal?" with numbers.
  • Control chart: A chart of measured values lined up in time order.
  • Center line: The middle line that shows the average.
  • Control limits (UCL / LCL): The upper and lower lines, set from past data.
  • σ (sigma): A number that shows the size of variation (standard deviation).
  • 3-sigma limits: Control limits placed 3σ from the center line.
  • Out-of-control signs: Signals that something is "different from usual," such as a point outside the lines or points staying on one side.

Common misunderstandings

Q. If the points are inside the lines, is there no problem?

Not necessarily. "Habits" in the pattern, such as eight points in a row on the same side, are also signs. Even when points are inside the lines, it is important to make a habit of looking at the pattern.

Q. If a point goes outside the lines, does that always mean the equipment is broken?

Not always. Even with 3-sigma limits, a point can fall outside the lines when nothing has happened. First, investigate the cause and record it.

Q. Is SPC for confirming that products meet specs?

Not quite. SPC watches how the process is doing: "is it normal?" Confirming that products meet specs is statistical quality control (SQC).

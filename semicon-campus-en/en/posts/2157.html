Why Isn't Film Thickness Even? Why Thickness Varies Across One Wafer, and the Work of Equipment Maintenance

Same day, same tool, yet different results

Imagine an inspection scene at a fictional fab. The film thickness inspection results show some worrying numbers.

At the center of the wafer, the film came out at the target thickness. But toward the edge, it is a little thinner. The processing time was the same, and the set gas flow was the same. The tool did not stop.

"We made the film on the same disc for the same amount of time. Why is the thickness different?"

It seems strange, but this is an important question for understanding semiconductor deposition.

To give the answer first: time is not the only thing that decides film thickness. How material reaches each spot on the wafer, and what reaction conditions each spot sees, also play a part. In an actual PECVD study, researchers kept the same recipe, changed the setup of the gas distribution plate, and confirmed that the deposition rate distribution across the wafer changed.[8] Another ALD study looked at gas flow and temperature distribution as possible causes.[1][2] However, these are studies of different tools and film types, and they do not mean the PECVD experiment directly measured temperature differences across the wafer. The opening is a fictional inspection scene, not an explanation that pins down the cause of a fault.

On top of that, differences in the measured numbers can sometimes come from the measurement method rather than the film itself. Seeing a thickness distribution does not mean you should replace parts right away.[5][6][8]

In this article, we follow the topic from how films are formed, to why unevenness appears, how it is measured, and the order in which causes are checked. At the end, we also connect it to how equipment maintenance and production engineering staff deal with this problem.

How is this thin film made on the surface?

Inside a semiconductor are many thin layers with different roles, such as metal films that conduct electricity, insulating films that do not, and films that protect the surface.

Think of painting a wall: you ran the roller over it, but the edges came out thinner. If the amount of paint reaching each spot or the way it is applied differs, the finish changes too.

But here is the important part. Unlike paint, semiconductor films are made using the transport of atoms and molecules, chemical reactions, plasma, and so on. Nobody moves a roller. The comparison only applies to the idea that "conditions differ from place to place."

The process of making a film is called "deposition." There are methods such as CVD, PVD, and ALD, and each forms the film in a different way. The introductory article What Is Deposition? is also a good place to start.

How even does it have to be to count as "uniform"?

Film thickness is simply how thick a film is. For example, say one spot on a film is 98 nanometers and another spot is 102 nanometers. Even if the average is 100 nanometers, that does not mean the whole surface is 100 nanometers.

Looking at this variation within one wafer is called within-wafer uniformity (WIW). Differences between multiple wafers, on the other hand, are treated separately as wafer-to-wafer uniformity (WTW). In the deposition field, within-wafer, wafer-to-wafer, and batch-to-batch are handled separately.[11] Note that SEMI-NET's separate WIW entry[10] explains CMP (polishing) amounts, and is not a document that directly defines how to calculate deposited film thickness.

  • Within-wafer: Does it vary from place to place on one wafer?
  • Wafer-to-wafer: Do multiple wafers processed in the same step come out the same?
  • Change over time: Do the values shift between morning and night, before and after maintenance, or a few days later?

In practice, the average thickness and the thickness variation are checked separately. Even if only the average is right, the edge alone may be out of spec.

Always state the formula with "2% uniformity"

As a teaching example, say you measured 5 spots at 98, 99, 100, 101, and 102 nanometers.

The average is 100. The difference between the maximum and minimum is 4. Using the "half-range method," which divides the max-min difference by twice the average, you get (102−98)÷(2×100)×100 = 2%. This is one definition actually used in a published study of ALD thin films, but it is not the only formula shared by all tools and film types.[1]

However, with the same data, dividing the max-min difference directly by the average gives 4%. Using standard deviation gives yet another value. The "relative standard deviation (1σ/mean)," which divides the standard deviation by the average thickness and multiplies by 100, is another way to compare. This definition is also used in thin-film research.[3] But it does not give the same number as the half-range method. The CMP glossary[10] is not used as a basis for deposition formulas.

So the number "uniformity is now 2%" alone does not let you compare whether things got better. It only means something once the formula, measurement positions, number of points, and how much of the edge was excluded are all aligned.

Why does thickness vary on the same wafer?

The key point is that the settings shown on the tool's screen are not the same as what actually happens on the wafer surface.

For example, even if the tool is set to "supply gas at this flow rate," that gas does not necessarily reach every spot on the wafer at the same concentration and the same timing. The center and the edge also sit in different positions relative to the flow and the exhaust.

Temperature can also vary. How heat moves to and from the parts that hold the wafer, heat loss from the edge, and the state of temperature control can differ from place to place. Depending on the deposition method, temperature and supply conditions can affect how the film grows.[1][2] However, this paragraph is an explanation of possible causes. The PECVD comparison experiment mentioned earlier[8] did not measure or prove temperature differences across the wafer, and because it depends on the film and reaction conditions, you also cannot say that "hotter spots are always thicker."

So what is especially difficult for each method?

CVD: adding gas does not guarantee an even reaction

CVD is a method that uses gaseous materials to form a film on the surface. Besides methods that use reactions driven by heat, there is also PECVD, which gets help from plasma.

The thing to watch is how the gas arrives. Even with a part called a showerhead that spreads the gas widely, conditions on the wafer can vary depending on the position and shape of the holes, the gas flow, and so on. In PECVD, plasma-related conditions can also affect deposition.[8]

In an actual published study, researchers compared results on a 300-millimeter PECVD tool using the same recipe while changing things like the structure of the showerhead holes. They reported that the distribution of the silicon nitride film's deposition rate and indicators related to film composition changed.[8]

Even when processed with "the same settings," if the tool's internal structure differs, the same reaction does not necessarily happen at every spot on the wafer. That is the point the study teaches.

PVD: even the direction the material flies in matters

In sputtering, a typical PVD method, atoms and other particles are knocked off a material called a target and stick to the wafer to form a film.

This time, the issue is not only gas reactions but also the direction and distribution of the incoming particles. The distance and position between the target and the wafer, scattering due to pressure, wafer rotation, and so on can affect the thickness distribution. The relationship between rotation/revolution and a target thickness distribution has been verified in experiments on curved optical substrates.[9]

A 2021 paper used a mechanism that rotates and revolves the substrate relative to the target, predicted how film thickness would vary by location, and confirmed it by measurement. However, this was a study of special curved optical substrates, and it does not mean every semiconductor wafer will give the same result.[9]

What this shows is that variation is not just "chance." It can come from the geometric conditions of the tool.

ALD: it builds one layer at a time, so why isn't it perfectly even?

ALD is a method that grows a thin film by sending materials in alternately and using the fact that surface reactions saturate. It is used because it makes it easier to finely control thickness and coverage of complex shapes.[4]

But just because the reaction stops at the surface, that does not guarantee that enough material for the reaction reaches every spot. Supply time, purging, the condition of the reacting surface, material transport, and so on all play a part.[4][1][2]

A published 2017 ALD study examined the thickness distribution on 200-millimeter wafers and reported that the variation changed in tests where the substrate's orientation was changed partway through deposition. The authors list the effects of flow and temperature inside the tool as possibilities, but they did not pin down a specific fault.[1] A separate 2025 study used fluid analysis and experiments to examine how changing the setup of the gas inlet changes the thickness variation of ALD films.[2]

What goes wrong when the thickness differs?

All of this is not just about making things look even. Films have jobs to do.

For example, an insulating film separates areas where electricity should not flow. If the film thickness differs, then depending on conditions, the electric field, capacitance, and how easily current leaks can change, which can affect electrical margins. For a metal film, depending on its shape, resistivity, and other conditions, thickness can affect electrical resistance.

However, a difference in thickness alone does not automatically make something a defect. The effect depends on the film's material, composition, defects, which circuit it is used in, and the design tolerance. In particular, whether leakage current goes up or down cannot be determined from film thickness alone, and electrical measurements suited to the structure are needed.

What matters is where the thickness variation sits relative to each product's specs, and then linking that to electrical test results and effects on later process steps.

A thickness map gives you "the next question," not "the answer"

A film thickness map is the measured thickness values laid over their positions on the wafer. Color-coding the values reveals patterns such as a thicker center, a thinner edge, or a difference on one side only.

But if you see a map with a thin edge, can you declare, "The heater is broken"?

No. It could be a heat difference, but there are several possibilities, such as gas distribution, the direction material flies in, how the substrate is placed, and measurement conditions.

Even maps with similar patterns do not necessarily have the same single cause. The value of a map is not that it pins down the cause, but that it helps you decide the order in which to investigate.[5][8][2]

Why you question the measurement itself first

The thickness of a thin film is sometimes found using changes in the reflection and polarization of light. A typical example is spectroscopic ellipsometry.

However, the instrument does not directly display "a thickness measured with a ruler." It measures how light is reflected and estimates the thickness with a model that assumes things like the film's optical properties. If the film's refractive index or the conditions of the underlying layer differ from what was assumed, the calculated thickness is affected too.[5][15]

NIST, the U.S. measurement science institute, has studied how differences in measurement methods and analysis models affect results. It is also working on combining ellipsometry and X-ray reflectometry (XRR) to check the condition of thin films on wafers.[6]

The fact that "the measured values differ" and the fact that "the actual film differs" need to be considered separately.

In what order do equipment maintenance and production engineering track down the cause?

Engineers on the floor do not decide to replace parts the moment they see a graph.

First, they confirm the issue is real. They remeasure the same wafer under the same conditions to see whether the same distribution appears. They align the measurement positions, the edge width being excluded, the formula, and any differences from the previous measurement.

Second, they check how far the effect reaches. Is it just one wafer, the whole lot, or only a specific chamber? When did the abnormality first appear? How production wafers are handled follows each fab's quality procedures and the judgment of authorized staff.

Third, they compare against the tool's records. Gas supply, temperature, pressure, plasma, part replacements, cleaning, startup after maintenance. Even if values are within the normal range, have they been shifting little by little compared with before?

Here, the ideas of SPC (statistical process control), which tracks trends in film thickness and variation, and FDC (fault detection and classification), which checks things like the tool's sensor time series, are useful. NIST explains process monitoring with SPC control charts and EWMA, and STMicroelectronics has published a case of feeding sensor signals from real manufacturing equipment into FDC.[7][14][13] This describes real monitoring methods, but it does not mean this fictional thickness abnormality was diagnosed with FDC. Also, FDC uses SPC methods too, so it is not accurate to see the two as completely independent systems. And a change in a control chart alone does not automatically pin down the cause.

Fourth, they test their hypothesis. If they think a specific gas line is the cause, are there records or measurement results that fit? What about another chamber? The necessary checks are carried out under safety procedures and with approval.

Finally, with proper approval, they check the tool's functions and confirm, by measuring the necessary monitor wafers or product characteristics, that the process has returned to the required quality. This is the idea behind post-maintenance Qual (requalification). Applied Materials has explained that thin-film metrology on CVD tools helps with requalification after maintenance.[12] However, how many wafers to measure and what to use as pass criteria are decided by the specific tool, film type, the change made, and the fab's official procedures. It does not mean that mass production is approved to restart just because an alarm cleared.

Who fixes this problem?

Equipment maintenance is involved in inspection, repair, part replacement, managing maintenance history, and confirming recovery, so that tools run in a stable state. Production engineering and process engineering verify whether the film meets the required quality and under what conditions it stays stable. Metrology and quality staff also check whether measurements can be trusted and how far the effect has spread. The actual division of roles varies by fab.

Experience gained in manufacturing, such as "noticing changes in equipment," "keeping work records," "confirming that a defect can be reproduced," and "narrowing down causes with related departments," is a foundation for thinking about these jobs. However, in semiconductors you will need to newly learn safety knowledge specific to the field, such as hazardous process gases, vacuum, high voltage, and plasma, as well as knowledge of thin-film metrology.

What they look at is not just whether the tool is broken. It is whether the product comes out at the target quality.

Summary: thickness unevenness is a starting point for thinking about what happened inside the tool

Even if the same film is made for the same time, the same conditions are not necessarily achieved at every spot on the wafer.

Gas may arrive differently. Heat moves differently. The direction material flies in, whether reactions saturate, and the tool's aging also play a part. You also have to check whether the measured values really reflect the true film thickness.

What matters is the flow of forming a hypothesis from the thickness distribution, confirming it with records and additional measurements, and verifying that quality has recovered after maintenance.

And the goal of this work is not just fixing the tool. It is to keep producing the same quality on the next wafer, too.

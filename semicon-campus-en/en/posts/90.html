How semiconductors are made · Leveling the surface

CMP is a process that removes excess insulating film or metal from a wafer during manufacturing and evens out the height of the surface. It combines chemical action from chemicals with mechanical polishing by a pad and other parts. Steps on the surface are reduced so that the next fine circuits can be made accurately. CMP is used repeatedly wherever needed, such as while building insulating structures that separate devices or metal wiring.

This article starts with "why we flatten the surface," then connects how the equipment and materials work, what is checked after processing, and the jobs in a fab. The first point to grasp is that being flat, keeping the needed film, and having few scratches or contaminants are conditions that are each checked separately.

Why are semiconductors flattened again and again?

CMP stands for Chemical Mechanical Planarization, or Chemical Mechanical Polishing. Planarization means making flat, and polishing means grinding smooth. Removing material is only the means; in semiconductor manufacturing, the main goal is to create a surface that can be used for the next process step.[1][3] It is not a process where a mirror-like shine means you pass.

A wafer is a disk-shaped base on which many chips are made together. When you add insulating film on top, open holes and trenches, and fill them with metal, the height varies from place to place. Simply putting a thin film on top can carry over the steps underneath. The more circuits and wiring are built up in three dimensions, the more these height differences need to be evened out along the way.

If the steps are large, it becomes hard to keep the surface within the range where light can be focused in the next lithography step. They also affect the condition of coated films and later processing.[4] To stack fine patterns correctly over many layers, it is important not to carry over the bumps and dips from the previous layer.

"A smooth surface" and "a flat surface" are also not the same. Even with little fine roughness, waviness or steps left over a wide area cause problems for the next step. On the other hand, even if the height is even over a wide area, a single deep scratch can damage a circuit. In CMP, several quality aspects viewed at different scales are managed together.

Even after CMP is done once, forming new insulating film or wiring creates new bumps and dips. So CMP is not a one-time final polish of the finished product; it is a step inserted as needed during manufacturing. However, CMP does not always follow every deposition, and it does not grind the silicon base every time. Its target is the film or metal that must be removed at that step.

How do the pad and slurry work?

In basic equipment, a head holds the wafer and presses its processed surface against a polishing pad, and the two move relative to each other. Slurry is supplied to the contact area. The pad transmits force to the wafer surface and spreads the slurry, and the slurry is a liquid containing abrasive particles and chemical components. The wafer, pad, slurry, pressing force, and motion all work as one set.

Combining chemical and mechanical action

Chemical action makes the target surface react so it becomes easier to remove, or suppresses removal and corrosion of materials you want to keep. Mechanical action removes surface material through contact with the pad, abrasive particles, and relative motion. Using both at once lets you adjust how material is removed depending on its type. It is different from a process that simply dissolves material with liquid, and from one that grinds only with a hard grindstone.

Oxide films, copper, tungsten, and other materials differ both in surface reactions and in which layers must remain. So you can't just use the same slurry for every material. The property of removing one material while making it easier to keep another is called "selectivity." Selectivity is important, but it does not guarantee that processing stops completely the moment it reaches the layer below, so time and endpoint control are also needed.[7][8]

Pressure, speed, and time can't be set in isolation

The pressing pressure and relative speed affect how fast material is removed. But higher pressure doesn't always give better results. Contact conditions and removal behavior change between the center and edge of the wafer, and between areas where circuits are dense and areas where they are sparse. If you only extend the polishing time, you may fix under-polishing in one place while over-polishing another.

In mass production, it's not just the average removal amount that needs to be consistent. Results also need to be stable across the surface of each wafer, from wafer to wafer, and from day to day. Engineers check pressure distribution, motion conditions such as rotation, liquid supply, and pad condition, and choose a set of conditions that fits the materials and structure. Specific settings differ by product and tool.

The pad changes as it is used

The pad surface carries slurry and contacts the wafer. If the surface changes during use, polishing results can change even with the same settings. So a conditioner is used to keep the pad surface in shape. Replacing the pad and conditioning its surface during use are separate tasks. Only when you also manage the condition of consumables do tool settings and processing results line up.[9]

From deposition to CMP, inspection/metrology, and the next step

The overall relationship is: make a film → required processing and filling → CMP → cleaning and drying → inspection/metrology → processing of the next layer. This is a typical order to help you understand the relationship, not a fixed procedure for every product. To understand CMP, look at three things together: "what was just made," "what should remain," and "what is checked before moving on."

  1. Prepare materials by deposition and filling. An insulating film is added, or trenches and holes are filled with metal. To fill the needed places completely, extra material may also be left on the surface.
  2. Use CMP to remove excess material and even out steps. Unneeded film on the surface is removed while protecting the structures that should remain. Differences in how each material is removed, and the timing of when to stop, become important.
  3. Clean, dry, and check the results. Particles and residues from the slurry are removed, the wafer is dried, and then film thickness, distribution across the wafer, scratches, foreign particles, and so on are checked. Finishing polishing is not the same as being ready to hand off to the next step.
  4. Pass the results on to the next processing step and to condition management. The wafer moves on to the next deposition or patterning, and changes in measurement results are used to monitor the process. Abnormalities are handled according to the production line's criteria.

For copper wiring: "keep what's in the trench, remove the excess copper on top"

Copper damascene wiring is an easy-to-understand example of CMP's role. Trenches for wiring and holes that connect layers are made in the insulating film, barrier layers that keep copper from spreading into the surroundings are formed, and then copper is filled in. At this point, copper is not only in the trenches but also on top of them.

CMP removes the excess copper and barrier material on top, leaving the copper in the trenches as wiring. If the metal on the surface isn't removed completely, wires that should be separate can end up connected. On the other hand, grinding too deep into the needed copper changes the shape of the wiring. This is why you look not only at "how much was removed" but also at "whether the needed shape remained."

CMP is also used to flatten after filling trenches that separate devices with oxide, and to remove excess tungsten filled into contact holes. The copper example can't be applied directly to the processing conditions for all materials. Understand the role for each target material, underlying layer, and structure to be kept.

What's difficult, and what is measured after processing?

The difficulty of CMP isn't just being able to remove material. It's about reducing differences across locations on the wafer, keeping the needed film, and finishing without adding scratches or contamination. One defect doesn't always correspond to one cause, so materials, equipment, previous steps, and cleaning are investigated separately.

Five problems to check for in CMP
ProblemWhat's the issue? / What is checked?
Over-polishingThe shape departs from the target, for example a film that should remain gets thinner or metal areas sink below their surroundings. Remaining film thickness, local height, and polishing time/endpoint are checked.
Under-polishingExcess film or metal remains, which can lead to steps or unwanted electrical connections. Whether material remains and the distribution of the removal amount are checked.
Flatness and variationEven if the average is good, large differences between locations affect later steps. The center vs. edge, pattern density, and wafer-to-wafer differences are viewed separately.
ScratchesScratches from large particles and the like can affect wiring or insulating films. The location, shape, and number of scratches are examined, along with particles and contact conditions.
Slurry residue and contaminationParticles, reaction products, metal contamination, and so on may be carried into the next step. The surface condition after cleaning is checked as well.

In the copper wiring example, the phenomenon where the metal surface sinks like a dish below the surrounding insulating film is called "dishing." "Erosion" is when the insulating film between wires is also removed in areas with dense wiring, so the whole area becomes lower. Both are height-change problems, but they involve different structures and extents. Because they relate not only to polishing conditions but also to wire width and density and the selectivity of each material, it's important not to explain them simply in terms of time.[5]

Separate the decision to stop from checking the finish

Deciding where to end processing is called "endpoint detection." Depending on the target film and tool, changes in optical signals or electrical characteristics are used. However, getting an endpoint signal alone doesn't tell you that the thickness across the whole surface and the scratch level pass. Control of stopping is combined with verification of the processing results.[6]

Film thickness measurement is used to learn the thickness of the remaining film and its distribution. Defect inspection is used to find problem spots such as foreign particles and scratches. Even an even film thickness is a problem if there are scratches, and few scratches is still a problem if the needed film is too thin. Each measurement method suits some materials and structures better than others, and no single measurement can check everything.

The equipment, materials, and companies behind CMP

CMP can't be done with a single product from a single company. The tool controls pressure, motion, and liquid supply, while the slurry and pad create the contact environment that removes material. Cleaning removes leftover particles, and inspection/metrology checks the results. When changing processing conditions, engineers evaluate not only tool performance but also how it combines with materials and the steps before and after.

Equipment: controls holding, motion, supply, and transfer
It combines the head, polishing table, liquid supply, wafer transfer, and more. Some configurations build cleaning and film thickness measurement into the tool. Ebara is one example of a company that makes CMP equipment. Read about the role of Ebara's CMP, bevel polishing, and plating equipment.
Slurry: matched to the materials to remove and keep
It combines the action of abrasive particles and chemical components to meet requirements such as removal rate, selectivity, and surface defects. Fujimi Incorporated supplies polishing materials for CMP. Learn about Fujimi's slurries by film type.
Pad and surface conditioning: keeps contact conditions stable
The pad is a consumable whose hardness, structure, and surface condition affect polishing results. Including the conditioner that keeps the surface in shape, it is managed by watching changes in results, not just hours of use.
Cleaning and metrology: confirm the wafer is ready for the next step
These steps reduce particles and contamination left right after polishing, and examine film thickness, height, defects, and more depending on the purpose. Some functions are performed inside the CMP tool, and some evaluations are done on separate tools.

When researching companies, look past the phrase "CMP-related" and check what each company supplies and which quality aspects it affects. That makes the differences clear. Rather than ranking equipment and materials companies in a simple list, it's easier to understand them as companies supporting the same process from different roles.

What do people who work with CMP do?

Even within "CMP work," a job that keeps the tool running stably and a job that improves the film finish mainly focus on different things. Below is a typical division of roles. Job titles and scopes overlap from company to company, so don't decide by job title alone; check the actual tasks involved.

Equipment engineer
Checks tool startup and operating status, and investigates problems with pressure, motion, supply systems, and so on. On the equipment-maker side, the title can also mean design and development, so look at the employer and the process covered together.
Equipment maintenance
Handles inspections, consumable replacement, breakdown response, and checking the tool's condition after work. The job isn't done when a part is replaced; it extends to confirming that the tool runs safely and processing results have returned to normal. Read about maintenance work and the knowledge it requires.
Process engineer
Evaluates conditions such as pressure, time, and slurry to meet requirements for remaining film thickness, flatness, and defects. Links the condition of films from previous steps to post-CMP data and verifies the effect of condition changes.
Quality and metrology
Reviews film thickness and defect data and catches process changes. When an abnormal value appears, checks not only for changes on the product side but also the measurement conditions and the state of the measurement tool, and investigates with the process team.
Field service engineer (FSE)
Mainly works for equipment makers and similar companies to support customer fabs, handling installation, inspection, repair, and recovery. Works with the fab's maintenance staff, but the scope of work and travel and work conditions are not the same. Read about what FSEs do and how they work.

Experience with machine inspection, parts replacement, recording measurement results, and isolating problems is a good entry point for understanding CMP work. On the other hand, the reactions of each material, particle control, cleanroom work, and the safety procedures for each tool are new areas to learn. Simply knowing what CMP means doesn't determine your skill level or hiring suitability.

To keep learning, choose from these next questions

The core of CMP isn't just reducing material; it's setting up the conditions for the next processing step while keeping the needed structures. If you can explain in your own words "why steps are reduced," "what the pad and slurry do," and "what is checked after processing," you've grasped the basic connections.

Check your understanding: If the average film thickness is on target, does CMP pass?

That alone isn't enough to judge. You also need to check thickness at each location, local dips, scratches and foreign particles, residue after cleaning, and more. The average value and surface quality are separate pieces of information, and you look at both to see whether the conditions needed for the next step are met.

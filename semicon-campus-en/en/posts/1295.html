What happened: sorting out the dates and details

In an official blog post dated September 24, 2026, Applied Materials explained in detail "Producer Selectra Mo Etch," which precisely separates molybdenum (Mo) word lines in 3D NAND. Note, however, that September 24 is the publication date of the technical blog. The tool itself was announced on June 15, 2026, and the two are not the same event. Applied Materials' technical blog and the company's press release give the respective dates and overviews.

The focus this time is a process that removes Mo only where needed inside the deep, narrow structures of 3D NAND. In 3D NAND, after Mo is deposited as the word line material, the excess Mo left on the vertical slit sides must be removed to electrically separate neighboring word lines. Applied Materials proposes a tool that uses gas-based selective removal for this step. Official blog

Keep in mind that the statement "validated in volume production processes" is the company's own claim. The published materials do not show customer names, specific process conditions, comparison data against conventional methods, improvement rates, or when volume production will start. So the tool announcement and technical explanation should be kept separate from confirmed wide adoption in volume production. Official press release

Why is it hard? Understanding 3D NAND processing from the basics

3D NAND is memory that does not just line up data-storing cells on a flat plane but stacks layers of cells vertically. In conventional planar NAND, storage density was raised by making cells smaller horizontally. But as scaling progressed, the distance to neighboring cells and the processing margins shrank. That made the idea of increasing capacity by stacking cells vertically in 3D important. Micron's explanation of NAND

As the number of layers grows, the structures to be processed become "deep and narrow." Such shapes are called high-aspect-ratio structures. The aspect ratio is the ratio of a hole's or trench's depth to its width. The smaller the width relative to the depth, the higher the aspect ratio, and the harder it is to deliver process liquids or reactive gases evenly to the bottom.

Word lines are conductive layers used to select memory cells. Mo is one example of such a conductive material. During deposition, it can reach not only the horizontal word line cavities but also the vertical slits used for processing. If excess Mo remains, it can form a conductive path that unintentionally connects neighboring word lines. On the other hand, removing too much can damage the word line shape and may affect how well the cells are controlled. Applied Materials' technical explanation

The Selectra Mo Etch technology: selectivity and uniformity are key

Another key term is selective removal. This means processing that acts strongly on the material you want to remove while acting as little as possible on the surrounding materials you want to keep. For this tool, the company describes a gas-based process that targets and removes Mo while protecting the surrounding materials. Product page

With conventional liquid-based wet etching, it can be hard to make chemicals act evenly deep inside tall stacked structures. As a result, the process profile may end up over-etched at the top of the structure and under-etched at the bottom. Applied Materials says it aims to improve the uniformity of word line separation from the top to the bottom of the stack through gas delivery and process control. Official blog

The phrase "atomic precision," used in article titles and elsewhere, also needs careful reading. The published materials do not show error values at the atomic-layer level, specific selectivity ratios, etch rates, or process temperatures and pressures. So it is appropriate to understand it here as a technical expression meaning "aiming for very fine process control," and not to treat it as a measured atomic-level performance figure.

Expected effects and what is still unknown

Applied Materials says that uniform word line separation aims to reduce issues related to cell-to-cell variation, leakage, and data retention. Leakage is current escaping through paths it should not flow through. Data retention is the ability to keep written information correct for a certain period. Less process variation could contribute to the electrical stability of memory, but this is the company's explanation, not published independent test results. Official press release

The company also mentions that this selective removal technology could be extended in the future to advanced DRAM, leading-edge logic, and more. However, this describes potential applications and business plans, not a fact that it has been adopted for volume production of those products. Materials, structures, and process conditions differ by application, so the 3D NAND explanation cannot be applied directly to other semiconductors. Product page

What to watch next

First, we want to see concrete volume-production adoption, including customer names and target products. Second, measurement data showing how much uniformity, leakage, yield, and data retention improved compared with wet etching. Third, whether the same performance holds with higher layer counts and narrower structures.

As 3D NAND gets taller, it becomes more important not just to remove material but to achieve uniformity in the depth direction and selectivity that protects the materials that stay. This news is less a flashy new memory product announcement than an improvement to a manufacturing process that supports 3D stacking.

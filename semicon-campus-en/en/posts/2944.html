What you will learn in this article

  • Why native oxide is a problem (contact resistance)
  • The surface after HF (hydrogen termination)
  • Roughly how long it takes for the oxide to come back
  • How thin native oxide is (the world of nanometers)

1. What is native oxide?

In Lesson 2, we saw native oxide as the fourth type of contamination removed by cleaning. It is a very thin oxide film (a film bonded with oxygen) that forms on the surface of a silicon wafer when it is exposed to air or water.

This film does not conduct electricity well. So if it remains where the device connects to metal wiring, patents and other sources point out that contact resistance (how hard it is for electricity to pass through the joint) goes up. This can lower device performance.

Check quiz: What is the problem if native oxide remains where the device connects to metal?

Answer: The joint becomes harder for electricity to pass through (higher contact resistance), and device performance drops.

2. What happens to the surface after dilute HF?

Native oxide is removed with dilute HF, which we saw in Lesson 2 and the Lesson 3 extra (HF mixed with water at 1:50 or 1:100, about 15 seconds at 25°C).

At this point, the silicon surface is not simply left bare. The "bonding hands" of the surface silicon atoms (the hands they use to connect to other atoms) are capped by hydrogen atoms. This state is called hydrogen termination. A surface capped with hydrogen does not easily bond with oxygen or contaminants right away.

By the way, there is also a method that removes the oxide with HF "vapor." According to patents and other sources, this leaves quite a lot of fluorine on the surface. The resulting surface is different from the one you get with liquid HF.

Check quiz: After the oxide is removed with HF, what covers the silicon surface?

Answer: Hydrogen atoms (hydrogen termination).

3. How quick is "forms again quickly"?

In Lesson 2, we said native oxide "forms again quickly when exposed to air or water." So how quick is "quickly"?

Measurement reports and patents describe it like this:

  • A silicon surface treated with HF barely changed for more than 2 hours. Even after 3–4 days, the amount of re-oxidation was very small (one measurement report).
  • After HF, it takes about 10^3 to 10^4 minutes for a thin oxide to form again (one patent's description).
Let's think in numbers

10^3 minutes is 1,000 minutes. 1,000 ÷ 60 ≈ about 17 hours. 10^4 minutes is 10,000 minutes. 10,000 ÷ 60 ÷ 24 ≈ about 7 days. So even "quickly" covers a range of several hours to several days.

However, a hydrogen-capped surface does not hold up well to high temperatures. Also, oxidizing substances in the air can get through the capped surface. That is why fabs move wafers to the next process as soon as possible after HF.

Why move "right on to the next process"?

Because the oxide slowly comes back over several hours to several days. And that is not all. As we saw in the Lesson 3 extra, a nearly bare surface also picks up contamination easily. The basic rule: once you remove it, don't make the wafer wait.

Check quiz: Why is the wafer moved to the next process "as soon as possible" after HF?

Answer: Because the oxide slowly comes back, and the surface also gets contaminated easily.

4. How thin is native oxide?

In one measurement example, the native oxide on silicon left in clean air at room temperature for about a week was 5–7 angstroms. One angstrom is 0.1 nanometers.

Let's think in numbers

5–7 angstroms is 0.5–0.7 nanometers. One nanometer is 0.000001 millimeters, so that is 0.0000005–0.0000007 millimeters. It is about as thin as just a few stacked layers of atoms.

At this thinness, even measuring it is hard. According to sources, looking at a cross section with an electron microscope (TEM) is the most reliable way to measure film thickness, but it is said to be unable to measure native oxide thinner than 10 angstroms (1 nanometer).

Key points of this extra lesson

When native oxide is removed with HF, the surface is covered with hydrogen (hydrogen termination). It takes several hours to several days for the oxide to come back. So after removing it, move on to the next process as soon as possible. Native oxide is about 0.5–0.7 nanometers thick (one measurement example).

Word review

Oxide film: A thin film formed by bonding with oxygen. For silicon, it is silicon dioxide.

Contact resistance: How hard it is for electricity to pass through the joint between a metal and a semiconductor. The smaller it is, the better the performance.

Hydrogen termination: A state where hydrogen atoms cap the "bonding hands" on the silicon surface.

Angstrom: A unit of length. One angstrom is 0.1 nanometers.

Common misconceptions

Q. Does native oxide form the instant the surface touches air?

No. Sources include a report that an HF-treated surface barely changed for more than 2 hours, and re-oxidation was small even after 3–4 days. However, the longer you wait, the more the oxide comes back.

Q. After HF, is the surface perfectly clean, "bare silicon"?

The surface is covered with hydrogen atoms (hydrogen termination). Some sources also point out that using HF vapor leaves fluorine behind.

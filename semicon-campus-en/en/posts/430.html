Why is dicing needed?

In the front-end process (FEOL), many identical circuits are built on a silicon wafer. The wafer is still a disk, so it can't be used as a part inside a smartphone, car, or server. So, early in the back-end process, the wafer is cut into individual chips. This cutting process is called dicing.

You can picture it like breaking a big chocolate bar into small pieces. But with semiconductors, each chip contains fine circuits. If the cutting path shifts a little, an edge chips, or contamination remains, it can cause problems later in connection, encapsulation, or inspection.

In other words, dicing looks simple, but it affects quality and yield. It's easiest to think of it as an important bridge that keeps the circuits built over a long time in the front-end process as good parts until the end. DISCO also describes blade dicing as a series of steps: fixing the wafer on tape, cutting it with a blade, and handling the separated chips.

Where does it fit in the back-end process?

The back-end process turns the circuits made in the front-end process into a form that can be used as real products. Roughly, dicing cuts the chips apart, die bonding attaches them to a substrate or lead frame, wire bonding or bumps connect them electrically, molding protects them, and finally they are inspected.

Dicing is the entrance to this flow. If a chip gets chipped or cracked here, it may become a defect no matter how carefully it is assembled later. That's why, in the back-end process, "separating cleanly" first is so important.

Front-end processBuilds circuits on the wafer. Mainly deposition, lithography, etching, CMP, inspection, and more.
Back-end processCuts, attaches, connects, protects, and inspects the circuits on the wafer to make products.

Basic dicing flow

The detailed flow of dicing varies by product and equipment. But for beginners, it's easiest to understand in the following 5 steps.

1. Mount the wafer on tape to hold it

If you cut the wafer as is, it may move during cutting, and the finished chips may scatter. So the wafer is mounted on special tape and fixed to a frame. When a job posting says "wafer setting," "material setting," or "setup," it may include preparation work like this.

2. Align the position

On the wafer, there are paths for cutting between the chips. The equipment checks the position with a camera or similar and cuts along those paths. If this shifts, it may cut into the edge of a chip. Alignment is a key point that determines dicing quality.

3. Cut with a blade or laser

The most common method is blade dicing, which cuts with a thin blade spinning at high speed. There are also methods that use a laser or plasma. With any method, the goal is to "cut into the required shape with as little damage as possible."

Naoki

So "following the cutting path" means running the blade through the center of the street.

4. Clean and dry

Cutting produces debris and fine contamination. If it stays on the chips or tape, it can cause visual defects or trouble in later steps. So after cutting, the wafer is cleaned and dried to get it ready for the next step.

5. Pick up the good chips

The separated chips are sent to the next step, such as die bonding. Even when picking up chips, they must be handled so they don't crack or chip. In the back-end process, quality is decided by how the chips are handled before and after cutting, too.

Professor Owl

The tape doesn't just hold the wafer during cutting. It also keeps the cut chips lined up as they move on to the next step.

Blade dicing and laser dicing

There are several dicing methods. For beginners, it's enough to roughly understand that there are "methods that cut with a blade" and "methods that use light." Hamamatsu Photonics describes stealth dicing, which uses a laser, as a method that creates a modified layer inside the wafer and splits it. KLA introduces plasma dicing as a separation method different from conventional mechanical cutting.

Quality points in dicing

What matters in dicing is whether the cut chips can be used in the next step. The four quality points beginners should know are chipping, cracks, particles, and misalignment.

Chipping

This is when small pieces break off the edge of a chip. Large chipping can let cracks spread in later steps or affect reliability. Blade condition, cutting speed, cooling water, and the condition of the wafer and films are all factors.

Cracks

This is when a crack forms in a chip. Besides cracks you can see, there are tiny cracks that cause problems later. Cracks directly affect product reliability, so they are a very important check item in the back-end process.

Particles

This is when debris or contamination remains. In semiconductors, even tiny particles can lead to defects. Cleaning and drying after cutting, cleanliness inside the equipment, and work procedures are important.

Misalignment

This is when the cut drifts off the path it should follow. Large misalignment may damage areas close to the chip's circuits. Equipment alignment, camera recognition, and recipe settings are all factors.

The link to yield
Dicing defects directly affect back-end yield. Even a chip that was good after the front-end process can become an unshippable product if it chips or cracks during cutting. That's why dicing connects to jobs in manufacturing operation, inspection, quality, equipment maintenance, and process improvement.

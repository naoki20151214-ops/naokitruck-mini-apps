1. There are two ways to clean

So far, we have looked at the "chemicals" and the "order" of cleaning. So what kind of equipment does the job? Broadly, there are two types.

  • Batch cleaning: Cleaning many wafers together (placed in a carrier)
  • Single-wafer cleaning: Cleaning wafers one at a time

Each has strengths and challenges, and fabs choose between them depending on the purpose. In this extra lesson, we first look at one major step in the evolution of batch cleaning, and then at why single-wafer cleaning is getting more attention.

Check quiz: What is the method of cleaning wafers one at a time called?

Answer: Single-wafer cleaning.

2. The evolution of batch cleaning: from multiple baths to one

According to materials from the Semiconductor History Museum of Japan, which records the history of Japan's semiconductor industry, batch cleaning used to be a system with several baths lined up. Baths filled with different chemicals were lined up, and a carrier holding the wafers was dipped into them one after another.

Evidence

According to the same source, Dainippon Screen Mfg. (later SCREEN Holdings) launched the "FS-820L," a one-bath cleaning system, in 1994. In this system, the whole cleaning sequence is done in a single bath by switching between chemicals and pure rinse water.

According to the source, the one-bath system has two advantages.

  • The wafers are not exposed to the air while the chemicals are switched, so a higher level of cleanliness is possible
  • The equipment becomes much more compact and also saves energy
Let's think in numbers

The flow in Figure 1 of Lesson 3 was SC-1 → rinse → SC-2 → rinse → dry. A multi-bath system needs 4 baths. The wafers are moved from bath to bath and then to drying 3 + 1 = 4 times. Each move exposes the wafers to the air. In a one-bath system, only the liquid is switched, so the wafers are taken out of the bath 0 times during cleaning.

The evolution continued after that. According to the same source, low-pressure drying after cleaning became possible in 1995. Then in 2001, a one-bath system for 300-millimeter wafers (FC-3000) appeared and became the main tool for 300-millimeter batch cleaning. By switching the rinse between chemicals to a method that sprays pure water like a shower, the amount it could process (throughput) is also said to have risen greatly.

Check quiz: What were the advantages of the one-bath cleaning system that appeared in 1994?

Answer: The wafers were not exposed to the air while the chemicals were switched, which raised the level of cleanliness. The equipment was also compact and saved energy.

3. Why single-wafer cleaning is getting more attention

In Lesson 4, we looked at challenges of wet cleaning such as "over-cleaning" and "chemicals bringing in particles." Single-wafer cleaning is closely tied to these challenges.

In the explanation by Professor Ruzyllo of Penn State University (2010), introduced in the Lesson 3 extra, the features of today's cleaning are listed as wide use of diluted chemicals, simpler procedures, a focus on single-wafer cleaning, and wide use of ozonated water.

With one-at-a-time cleaning, it is generally said to be easier to fine-tune the chemicals and time on each wafer. The strength of batch cleaning is that it can clean many wafers at once. In other words, the idea is: batch when you need volume, single-wafer when you need fine control for each wafer.

Why is one-at-a-time cleaning getting more attention?

As lines get thinner and structures get more fragile, over-cleaning and chemicals bringing in particles become bigger and bigger problems. The idea is that single-wafer cleaning, which makes it easier to adjust the amount of chemicals and time for each wafer, suits these problems. (This is a general explanation.)

Key points of this extra lesson

Cleaning equipment comes in two types: batch, which cleans many wafers together, and single-wafer, which cleans one at a time. Batch systems evolved from multiple baths to a one-bath system that switches the liquid (1994), which avoids air exposure, is compact, and saves energy. Today's cleaning puts a focus on single-wafer cleaning.

Word review

Batch cleaning: A method that puts wafers in a carrier and cleans them together.

Single-wafer cleaning: A method that cleans wafers one at a time.

Bath: A large container that holds liquid.

One-bath system: A method that does the whole cleaning sequence in a single bath by switching between chemicals and pure water.

Throughput: The amount that can be processed in a given time.

Common misconceptions

Q. Is batch cleaning an outdated method?

No. SCREEN's product pages still list batch-type equipment today. Because it can clean many wafers together, it is used when volume is needed.

Q. Can a one-bath system use only one type of chemical per cleaning?

No. A one-bath system does the whole cleaning sequence in a single bath by switching between chemicals and pure water.

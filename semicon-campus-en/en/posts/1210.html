How Do Wafers Get Into Equipment? Following FOUP and EFEM Transfer

The answer first

The semiconductors used in smartphones and cars are not put into equipment one wafer at a time by hand at the factory. To reduce how often people hold wafers directly, wafers are carried to the front of the equipment inside a closed carrier called a FOUP.

The answer is that the FOUP transport system, the load port, and the robot inside the EFEM divide up the work. The FOUP is carried by an AMHS or OHT, the load port sets its position and door, and the EFEM hands wafers to the equipment one at a time. In configurations that need it, an aligner corrects the center and orientation.

Knowing this order shows you the links between positioning, cleanliness, and signal management that a simple phrase like "a transfer robot carries the wafer" hides.

First, what gets carried is the FOUP, not the wafer

In typical automated transport of 300 mm wafers, wafers move through the fab while stored inside a FOUP. FOUP stands for Front Opening Unified Pod, a carrier accessed from the front. From the outside it looks like a box, but inside it has slots that hold wafers one by one, and it keeps wafers from being directly exposed during transport.

AMHS refers to the fab's entire automated material handling system, and OHT is one example of a transport method that runs on rails near the ceiling. What an OHT grabs and carries is normally the FOUP, not bare wafers. The key distinction here is that the FOUP is not "a container where wafers are processed" but a carrier for transport and protection.

The FOUP's door stays closed until it arrives in front of the equipment. This limits how much the wafers are exposed to the fab's logistics space. However, simply meeting the FOUP standard does not automatically guarantee particle counts or the cleanliness of every tool. The condition of the door, bottom surface, and surrounding mechanisms is also managed.

The load port is the boundary between logistics and equipment

When the FOUP arrives in front of the equipment, wafers are not taken out right away. The load port receives the carrier and places it at the reference position the equipment expects. A load port looks like a stand, but it is a mechanical handoff interface between the carrier transport system and the manufacturing or inspection equipment.

This boundary lets you think about fab-side transport and equipment-side transport separately. The OHT carrying the FOUP, the load port receiving it, and the EFEM handling the wafers inside are each separate jobs. If the carrier is placed out of position, it affects the door connection and robot access later, so the initial positioning matters.

From the outside, a FOUP on a load port looks like it is just sitting there. But being able to place what will be handed to the equipment at the same reference position, repeatably, is the precondition for the next mechanical operation.

From OHT to load port: looking at carrier handoff step by step

When picturing fab transport, it gets confusing if you imagine wafers dropping straight from a ceiling rail into the equipment. In reality, the ceiling-side transport mechanism carries the FOUP and hands it to the load port in front of the equipment. The wafers stay inside the FOUP.

This division of work also helps standardize operations in front of the equipment. If you separate the system that carries the FOUP, the system that receives the carrier, and the system that opens the carrier to access the wafers, it becomes easier to trace at which stage things stopped. Transport methods and equipment configurations vary by fab and tool, so here we use OHT as a representative example.

Position the whole FOUP first

After the FOUP arrives at the load port, the first thing to set is not the center of a single wafer but the position of the whole carrier, the FOUP. A kinematic coupling is an example of a mechanical connection that supports a 300 mm carrier and positions it to a defined reference. Multiple contact points fix the carrier's posture and reduce variation in the later door connection and access.

Even when we say "positioning," not every tool uses the same part layout. What matters is that the FOUP's bottom surface and the receiving part on the load port do not interfere, and that the FOUP settles repeatably at the expected reference. Foreign matter, wear, deformation, or misaligned parts may carry over into the door connection and robot motion.

Connecting the front door to the equipment through FIMS

Once the FOUP's position is set, the next step is to align its front with the equipment-side interface. FIMS is the mechanical interface that connects the front of a 300 mm FOUP to manufacturing and metrology equipment. A mechanism on the equipment side handles the door and creates a state where the wafers can be accessed.

This step is not simply opening a door. If the FOUP front, the load port frame, and the equipment-side opening move while misaligned, contact or rubbing may occur. So beyond checking whether the door opened, the connection position, the return state, and interference with surrounding parts are all monitored as part of the equipment's condition.

Managing position and rubbing before and after the door opens

The load port's rails and latches are parts that support placing the FOUP and connecting its front. In a photo they look like a collection of metal parts, but in equipment maintenance you check separately whether moving parts interfere, whether the return position is stable, and whether contact surfaces have foreign matter or wear.

What can be stated firmly here is only the cause-and-effect link that misalignment and rubbing can lead to particle generation and transport stoppages. Specific tolerances, replacement intervals, and manufacturer-specific structures depend on the equipment's specifications and maintenance standards. This article covers only the mechanical relationships that can be generalized.

Cleanliness is not protected by a single part

The idea of handling wafers cleanly is easier to understand if you view the FOUP, load port, and EFEM as layers. First, the FOUP is carried closed and opened only as much as needed in front of the equipment. Next, in some setups the EFEM is built as a mini-environment, with filters and airflow managing the transfer space. Some configurations use N2, but this is not common to all EFEMs.

Cleanliness problems are not decided only by whether there is a filter. Mechanical conditions also matter, such as the door or frame rubbing, foreign matter on the carrier bottom, or the robot's holding part going outside its contact conditions. Equipment staff look not only at the result, particles, but also at candidate causes: which part's position or motion has changed.

Taking wafers out one at a time in the EFEM

Once the FOUP front is connected to the equipment, the transfer robot inside the EFEM accesses the slots. The EFEM is the front-end mechanism at the front of the equipment, combining a cleaned transfer space, a wafer handoff mechanism, and the necessary sensors and peripheral equipment. The robot takes wafers out of the FOUP one at a time and carries them to the equipment's handoff position.

Here, keep FOUP transport and wafer transfer separate. What the OHT carried was the whole FOUP. What the EFEM robot handles is each individual wafer inside it. Because separate mechanisms connect at the boundaries, wafers can be loaded into equipment without people walking around holding exposed wafers.

Do not assume a single holding method

The tip of the robot that holds the wafer is called the end effector. Publicly available transfer robots use several holding methods, such as vacuum suction, edge grip, and Bernoulli. So it is not accurate to say "wafers are never touched at all." It is safer to say that people do not touch them directly, and the equipment handles them under designed contact conditions.

Different holding methods change where contact happens, what conditions are detected, and what motion is allowed. The purpose of the images in this article is not to state a specific manufacturer's specifications, but to help you understand that there is a tip mechanism that hands off wafers, and that holding conditions are managed as part of the equipment.

Where needed, an aligner sets the center and orientation

After the robot takes out a wafer, depending on the equipment configuration and recipe, the wafer may pass through an aligner. An aligner is a mechanism that corrects the wafer's center offset and matches its rotational reference, such as the notch. This makes the position and orientation easier to handle at the next handoff.

However, not every tool uses an external aligner in the same order. Some have a correction mechanism inside the equipment, and some configurations are tailored to process conditions. The boundary here is to understand it as "position and orientation correction used when needed," not to call it a required step common to all equipment.

Track status, not just mechanical motion

Even if the FOUP is placed on the load port, the door opens, and the robot carries the wafers, fab management is not necessarily complete. The AMHS and the equipment need to share how far the handoff has progressed. SEMI E84 is the standard covering the signals, sequences, and error detection and recovery for carrier handoff.

In addition, carrier-level status and the status of each wafer inside the carrier are separate levels. SEMI E87 covers Carrier Management, and E90 covers Substrate Tracking. Even if wafers are physically moving, it becomes a manufacturing control problem if the FOUP status or wafer location seen from the host is out of sync.

For this reason, maintenance checks not only motors and latches but also whether handoff status, carrier identification, and wafer tracking line up on the same timeline. Specific signal names and equipment screens depend on the product configuration, so here we limit ourselves to the relationship that "physical transport and status management work together on separate layers."

Common misconceptions

Q. If you carry wafers in a FOUP, is their cleanliness automatically guaranteed?

A. No. A closed FOUP limits how much wafers are exposed to the open environment, but management also covers the condition of the door, bottom surface, load port, EFEM, and robot. Meeting a standard is not the same as the particle condition of an individual tool.

Q. Does every wafer always pass through an aligner?

A. No. An aligner is a center and orientation correction mechanism used in the equipment configurations and recipes that need it. Some configurations correct inside the equipment, and not every tool has an external mechanism in the same order.

Q. Are wafers never touched by the robot at all?

A. No. There are several examples of holding methods, and the equipment handles wafers under defined contact conditions. "People do not hold them directly" and "machines do not touch them" mean different things.

Summary

The FOUP carries wafers to the front of the equipment while staying closed.
The load port positions the carrier and connects it to the equipment through FIMS.
The EFEM layers on the needed correction plus cleanliness and status management, and hands wafers to the equipment one at a time.

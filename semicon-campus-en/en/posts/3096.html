This lesson answers one question.

"How is a semiconductor different from a conductor or an insulator?"

Answer: A semiconductor is a material in which we can change the number of electrons that carry electricity.

What you will learn

  • (2) Conductors and insulators
  • (3) Semiconductors are in between (band gap)
  • (4) Ways to increase electrons: temperature
  • (5) Ways to increase electrons: impurities (n-type and p-type)

The "Take a closer look" parts of each section are for readers who want to check things with equations and diagrams. You can skip them and still move on.

(1) Electricity flowing means electrons moving

Key point: What carries electricity is mobile electrons.

Most electrons in a material are bound to atoms and cannot move. Only the electrons that break free and can move around carry electricity.

Quick quiz: Which electrons can carry electricity, bound electrons or mobile electrons?

Answer: Mobile electrons.

(2) Conductors and insulators differ in their number of mobile electrons

Key point: It comes down to one thing: are there many mobile electrons, or almost none?

Materials like copper and aluminum, which have many mobile electrons, are conductors. Materials like glass and rubber, which have almost none, are insulators. This is the left-versus-right comparison in the first figure.

Quick quiz: Is a material with almost no mobile electrons a conductor or an insulator?

Answer: An insulator.

(3) Semiconductors are in between

Key point: A semiconductor is a material that has "just a few" mobile electrons, and that number can be changed.

The best-known example is silicon, which is the most widely used. The second most common is gallium arsenide.

Why can only "a few" electrons move? First, electrons cannot sit at just any energy level. Think of the floors of a building: there are "floors electrons can be on" and "floors they cannot be on."

  • Lower floor (valence band): the floor where electrons are bound to atoms. Electrons here cannot move.
  • Upper floor (conduction band): the floor where electrons are free from atoms and can move.
  • Between floors (band gap): energy levels where electrons cannot exist. This difference in height is the band gap.

To become mobile, an electron has to receive enough energy to climb from the lower floor to the upper floor, by the amount of this height difference. If the difference is large, almost no electrons can climb. If it is small, a few can.

The gap is 5.5 eV for diamond, about 1.14 eV for silicon, about 0.67 eV for germanium, and about 1.43 eV for gallium arsenide (eV is a unit of energy). In general, materials with a gap larger than about 4 eV are not called semiconductors. However, this dividing line is partly a matter of convention.

Quick quiz: Which has the larger gap, silicon or diamond?

Answer: Diamond (5.5 eV).

(4) Changing the number of electrons, method 1: temperature

Key point: Raising the temperature increases the number of mobile electrons. This is the opposite of metals.

Why does temperature lift electrons to the upper floor? A high temperature means the atoms in the crystal are shaking hard. Shaking atoms bump into nearby electrons and share their energy. The amount each electron receives is not fixed. Most electrons get only a little, and a tiny fraction happen to get a lot. Only electrons that receive more than the height difference between floors can climb to the upper floor. The higher the temperature, the harder the atoms shake, so more electrons get a large amount. In materials with a smaller gap, electrons can climb with less energy, so more of them make it.

Because heat energy increases the number of electrons that cross the gap, semiconductors conduct electricity more easily as temperature rises. In metals, rising temperature makes the atoms vibrate and get in the way, so electricity flows less easily. At absolute zero (0 K), a semiconductor is in the same state as an insulator.

Quick quiz: When temperature rises, what happens to how easily a semiconductor conducts electricity?

Answer: It increases.

Take a closer look: How much do electrons increase? (equation and calculation)

In a pure semiconductor, the number of electrons that cross the gap, ni, roughly follows this form (a general equation in semiconductor physics, used here to get a rough idea).

ni ∝ exp( −Eg / 2kT )

Eg is the gap, T is the temperature, and k is the Boltzmann constant. At room temperature (300 K), kT is about 0.0259 eV.

A gap only about 5 times larger means the number of electrons differs by 36 orders of magnitude. This is the real difference between a semiconductor and an insulator. You can also see from the equation that the value rises sharply as T grows, which is why raising the temperature makes conduction easier.

(5) Changing the number of electrons, method 2: impurities (doping)

Key point: A tiny amount of impurity can change how easily electricity flows by orders of magnitude. This is the greatest strength of semiconductors.

Mixing in a small amount of a different kind of atom is called doping. The amount is about 1 atom per 100 million. For example, adding 0.001% arsenic is said to add about 10 to the 17th power free electrons per cubic centimeter, making conduction about 10,000 times easier.

Silicon has 4 electrons it uses for bonding.

  • n-type: Add atoms with 5 electrons (phosphorus, arsenic, antimony). One electron is left over, and that electron carries electricity.
  • p-type: Add atoms with only 3 electrons (boron, gallium). This creates a spot that is short one electron, and this "hole left by a missing electron" is called a hole. It moves like a particle carrying positive charge.▶ More in Level 2
Quick quiz: Is silicon with boron added n-type or p-type?

Answer: p-type.

Take a closer look: How small the amount is, and the conductivity equation

Silicon is a crystal with 8 atoms in a cube 0.5431 nm on each side (a general property of the diamond structure).

8 ÷ (0.5431×10−7 cm)3 ≈ 5.0×1022 atoms/cm3

The added electrons are about 1017 per cm3, so the ratio is 1017 ÷ (5×1022) = 2×10−6 (about 2 in 1 million). That alone makes conduction about 10,000 times easier.

The resistivity of pure silicon is about 2.3×103 Ω·m at 20°C (the larger it is, the harder it is for electricity to flow). If conduction becomes about 10,000 times easier, the resistivity works out to about 1/10,000 (around 0.2 Ω·m) as a rough estimate.

Conductivity σ is determined by the number of charge carriers and how easily they move (a basic equation in semiconductor physics).

σ = q ( n μn + p μp )

q: the charge of one electron; n, p: the numbers of electrons and holes; μn, μp: how easily they move (mobility). The conclusion of this lesson is that temperature and doping can change n and p dramatically.

Key points of this lesson

Mobile electrons carry electricity. Conductors have many, insulators have almost none, and semiconductors are in between.
Semiconductors have a small band gap, so raising the temperature increases the number of mobile electrons.
Even a tiny amount of impurity changes conductivity by about 10,000 times. In n-type, electrons carry the current; in p-type, holes do.

In the next lesson, we will see how electrons and holes actually carry electricity.

Word review

  • Electron: A tiny particle with an electric charge. Mobile electrons carry electricity.
  • Band gap: The gap between the valence band and the conduction band where electrons cannot exist.
  • Doping: Adding a small amount of impurity to set how easily electricity flows.
  • n-type / p-type: A semiconductor where electrons carry the current / one where holes carry it.
  • Hole: The gap left by a missing electron. It moves like a particle carrying positive charge.

When people think of a semiconductor fab, the steps that shine light on wafers or add films tend to stand out. But in fact, the "washing" step also comes up again and again.

In this article, we explain step by step why wafers are washed so often, based on numbers from research and public materials.

Yellow highlights = key points that appear on this course's tests

What you will learn in this article

  • How common cleaning is (about one-fifth of all steps)
  • How small a particle can cause a defect (about one-quarter of the line width)
  • How the basic cleaning method came about (RCA cleaning in 1970)

(1) Cleaning comes up many times in the process

A Northeastern University explainer notes that in 0.18-micrometer generation CMOS, about 80 of roughly 400 steps were cleaning. Even 64-megabit DRAM had 60 to 70 cleaning steps. In other words, cleaning makes up about one in every five steps.

Evidence

An explainer from a Northeastern University research center (updated 2013). An article in The Electrochemical Society's magazine also states that at least one-third of all steps for advanced ICs are cleaning.

Why wash so often?

Each time a film is added, material is removed, or light is applied, dust and chemical residue are left on the wafer. If the wafer moves on to the next step as is, the contamination gets trapped inside the chip. That is why wafers are washed frequently between steps.

Let's think in numbers

Share of cleaning = 80 ÷ 400 = 0.2, or 20% (one in five steps).

Skipping one cleaning step might look like removing just 1 of 400 steps. But the contamination left behind by that one skip affects every step that follows. The large number of cleaning steps reflects how many chances there are for contamination to get in.

Quiz: In 0.18-micrometer generation CMOS, about how many of the roughly 400 steps were cleaning?

Answer: About 80 steps.

(2) Even very small contamination can cause defects

The same explainer states that "particles larger than about one-quarter of the minimum line width can cause fatal defects."

Why can even a small particle cause a defect?

A circuit is made of thin lines lined up with gaps between them. If a single particle lands there, it can connect two lines and cause a short circuit, or block a line and cause an open circuit. The finer the circuit, the smaller the particles that can cause defects.

Let's think in numbers

For the 0.18-micrometer generation, the minimum line width is about 0.18 micrometers = 180 nanometers (1 micrometer = 1,000 nanometers).

Rough size of a defect-causing particle = 180 ÷ 4 = 45 nanometers. That is about 1/1,500 the thickness of a human hair (about 70 micrometers = 70,000 nanometers).

For a generation with a 90-nanometer line width, 90 ÷ 4 = 22.5 nanometers. The finer the circuit, the smaller the particles you need to worry about.

Quiz: How large does a particle need to be to cause a defect?

Answer: Larger than about one-quarter of the minimum line width.

(3) Most defects were thought to be caused by contamination

The same explainer cites researchers' estimate that "contamination accounts for 75% of yield loss in integrated circuits." Yield is the share of chips made that turned out to be good.

Evidence and a caution

From the Northeastern University explainer. However, the page carries a note that "some information is outdated." It is safest to treat the 75% figure as an older estimate.

Why does yield matter?

Hundreds of chips are made from a single wafer. The more defects contamination causes, the fewer good chips you get from the same materials and time, and the higher the cost per chip. Cleaning is a step tied directly not only to quality but also to money.

Let's think in numbers

Yield = number of good chips ÷ number of chips made × 100. For example, if 400 chips are made from one wafer and 320 are good, then 320 ÷ 400 × 100 = 80%.

If contamination defects drop and 360 chips are good, then 360 ÷ 400 × 100 = 90%. That is 40 more good chips (12.5% more) from the same single wafer. (These numbers are examples to show the idea.)

Quiz: What is the safest way to treat this figure?

Answer: Because there is a note saying the information is outdated, treat it as an "older estimate."

(4) The basic cleaning method: RCA cleaning

In The Electrochemical Society (ECS) magazine, Professor Ruzyllo of Penn State University describes the method that became the basis of cleaning. Werner Kern and Puotinen presented a cleaning method based on hydrogen peroxide solutions in a 1970 paper. It is a two-step method that treats the wafer first with an alkaline solution and then with an acidic solution. This is "RCA cleaning."

Evidence

The Electrochemical Society Interface, Spring 2010 issue. This is Professor Ruzyllo's explainer; the original paper is W. Kern and D. A. Puotinen, RCA Review, 31, 187 (1970).

Why use two kinds of solution?

Contamination comes in different types, such as particles, organics, and metals, and it is hard to remove all of them with a single solution. The alkaline solution in RCA cleaning was originally meant to remove organic films and metals, but it turned out to be very effective at removing particles too, and it is still the foundation of cleaning today.

Key points of this lesson

Cleaning makes up about one-fifth of all steps. Even small particles can cause defects. The basic cleaning method is RCA cleaning from 1970, which uses solutions with different properties in order.

Quiz: In what order does RCA cleaning use the alkaline and acidic solutions?

Answer: First the alkaline solution, then the acidic solution.

Word review

  • Cleaning: The step that removes contamination from the wafer surface.
  • Particle: A tiny bit of dust stuck to the wafer surface.
  • Yield: The share of chips made that turned out to be good.
  • RCA cleaning: The basic cleaning method presented in a 1970 paper.

Common misconceptions

Q. Is cleaning just an extra task done between steps?

No. Cleaning makes up about one-fifth of all steps and is a step that supports quality.

Q. Is contamination big enough to see with your eyes?

No. Even an invisible particle can cause a defect if it is larger than about one-quarter of the circuit line width.

On September 22, 2026, Valens Semiconductor announced a collaboration with onsemi on a cost-optimized integrated sensor for high-volume 3-megapixel (3MP) automotive cameras. Under the plan, the two companies will jointly develop a way to integrate Valens's MIPI A-PHY connectivity solution into onsemi's image sensors. This is not the launch of a mass-produced product or an adoption decision; it is a development plan that aims to simplify automotive camera design and broaden adoption of MIPI A-PHY. This can be confirmed in Valens's announcement and its filing with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC).

What was announced

The subject here is a concept to build the connectivity function that sends automotive camera video to the in-vehicle computer into the image sensor itself. A typical camera module may combine an image sensor, a short-range interface, a SerDes chip for long-distance transmission, and so on. The two companies aim to simplify the camera's parts and design by integrating the A-PHY connectivity function close to the sensor.

Valens describes 3MP as an important resolution range for high-volume vehicles. It also says many automotive image sensors target 1 to 3MP, but this is the company's market description included in the announcement, not something confirmed by independent market statistics. So separate research is needed to know how large the 3MP segment is in the overall market.Announcement details

What is MIPI A-PHY?

MIPI A-PHY is a SerDes (serializer/deserializer) physical layer for transmitting data over long distances between devices such as automotive cameras. SerDes is a mechanism that bundles multiple signals, sends them at high speed, and restores the original signals on the receiving side. Because in-vehicle cameras and the vehicle computer are placed far apart, interfaces designed for short traces on a circuit board alone may have trouble handling this. A-PHY is designed for these long in-vehicle cables.

According to the MIPI Alliance, A-PHY is designed to carry high-speed one-way data, two-way control data, and, depending on the configuration, power over the same physical cable. Because it can carry not only video but also control signals used for camera settings and status checks over the same connection, it may reduce cabling and peripheral parts. Targets include ADAS (advanced driver assistance systems), ADS (automated driving systems), and in-vehicle infotainment.MIPI Alliance explanation of A-PHY

Another important term is CSI-2. CSI-2 is MIPI's camera protocol, widely used to handle image data from camera image sensors. A-PHY is designed to carry CSI-2 and other data over long-distance links through a Protocol Adaptation Layer. This makes it possible to aim for a setup that reduces the dedicated bridges connecting camera signals on the sensor side with long-distance transmission in the vehicle.

MIPI's official page shows A-PHY v2.0 as the current version and describes features such as downlink speeds of up to 32Gbps and uplink speeds of up to 1.6Gbps. However, the two companies' announcements do not reveal which A-PHY v2.0 features or speeds the integrated sensor will use. The specifications of the standard as a whole cannot be treated as the same as the specifications of the product being developed.

Why integrating automotive camera parts matters

Automotive cameras are input devices for driver assistance functions such as recognizing the surroundings, parking assistance, and lane keeping. As the number of cameras grows and they handle higher-resolution video, the cabling that sends signals reliably over distance and the electronic parts on the camera side become important.

If the connectivity function can be integrated on the sensor side, it may simplify board area, part count, cabling, and power design compared with adding a separate chip. Fewer parts can also reduce design and assembly work in some cases. onsemi aims to use this integration to simplify camera architecture and make it easier for OEMs and Tier 1 suppliers to use standard technology.Announcement filed with the SEC

However, "integrating" is not the same as "always cheaper." New chip designs cost money to develop, and automotive use requires checking electromagnetic noise, temperature, vibration, safety, long-term reliability, mass-production yield, and more. Even if the part count goes down, if more functions are required of a single part, the impact of a failure and the number of evaluation items may increase.

What this announcement does not settle

What we can confirm is a plan by Valens and onsemi to collaborate and develop an integrated sensor for 3MP automotive cameras. The following items have not been announced.

  • Official product part number
  • Sample timing and mass-production start date
  • OEMs, Tier 1 suppliers, and vehicle models that will adopt it
  • Price or specific cost savings
  • Measured power consumption, area, and transmission performance
  • Mass-production yield and results of compliance with safety standards

So the announcement cannot be read as "a product already in mass production." Also, statements by the two companies such as "speeding up A-PHY adoption" and "reducing part count" are goals and expectations of the collaboration, not achieved results. Company forecasts need to be viewed separately from independent verification using prototypes or mass-produced units.

Points to watch

Going forward, the first focus is when evaluation samples will be available and whether the official specifications of the integrated sensor will be shown. In particular, which A-PHY generation is used, how it connects to CSI-2, the actual transmission distance, power consumption, temperature range, and tolerance to electromagnetic noise will be important.

Next, we need to check qualification as an automotive part and mass-production results. Even after a carmaker decides to adopt a part, there are several stages before mass production starts, such as design verification, reliability testing, and evaluation after integration into the vehicle. Only when adopting vehicle models, mass-production timing, and price are announced can we judge concretely how much impact this collaboration will have on the market.

This news reflects a move to build A-PHY into image sensor options, not just use it as a standalone connectivity part. If realized, it may give more design freedom for automotive cameras, but for now it is only at the development collaboration stage. Going forward, it makes sense to focus on evidence such as actual sample performance, mass-production quality, and customer adoption, rather than statements of support for the standard.

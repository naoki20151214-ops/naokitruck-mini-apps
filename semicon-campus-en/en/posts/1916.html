On October 9, 2026, Microchip Technology announced that it had acquired VORAGO Technologies, a privately held company based in Austin, Texas. The acquisition includes radiation-hardened MCUs (microcontrollers) and MPUs (microprocessors) used in harsh environments such as space and defense, as well as VORAGO's patented HARDSIL process technology. Microchip describes the deal as a complement to its own aerospace and defense product lines.(Microchip official announcement)

What happened: the acquisition announcement on October 9, 2026

The core fact we can confirm is that Microchip publicly announced its acquisition of VORAGO. The financial terms of the deal were not disclosed, and Microchip says it does not expect a material impact on its consolidated results. In other words, the purchase price and short-term sales effects were not revealed at the time of the announcement.(Microchip official announcement)

Also, an announcement that a company "has acquired" another is different from the fact that "new products have gone into mass production after the acquisition." This announcement does not show the post-integration product roadmap, application to specific manufacturing lines, customer adoption, or actual contribution to results. So, at this stage, the company has only shown its plan to bring in products, technology, and people, and we cannot say that the effects of the acquisition have been proven. As of the morning of October 10, 2026, no additional explanation correcting or updating this announcement could be found in Microchip's official news list.(Microchip official IR news list)

What is a radiation-hardened MCU?

An MCU is a control chip that combines a processing unit, memory, input/output control, and more on a single semiconductor. It is well suited to tasks that monitor a device's status and give commands, such as satellite attitude control, power management, and sensor processing. An MPU is generally a processor that handles more complex software and advanced processing. In spacecraft, if these chips malfunction, it can lead to lost communication or loss of control.

In space, stronger radiation than on the ground affects electronic parts. A key term is total ionizing dose (TID). This refers to a phenomenon in which radiation causes charge to build up inside a semiconductor, changing transistor characteristics over time. In long missions, it causes performance to degrade little by little.

Another is single event effects (SEE). This is a phenomenon in which a single particle, such as a proton or heavy ion, passing through a semiconductor causes a temporary bit flip, a malfunction, or in some cases destruction of the device. There is also non-ionizing damage, in which particles damage the crystal structure. NASA explains that radiation effects should be divided into TID, SEE, non-ionizing damage, and so on, and evaluated together with the mission's orbit, shielding, lifetime, and use.(NASA technical document)

Key points of HARDSIL technology

HARDSIL, as VORAGO describes it, is a technology called RHBP (Radiation-Hardened by Process), which increases radiation resistance on the semiconductor manufacturing process side. According to VORAGO, it adds an extra implant step using standard fab equipment, and aims for a setup that is not locked to a specific manufacturing node, foundry, or product. Another feature it describes is the ability to reuse existing IP, design libraries, and design flows.(VORAGO's HARDSIL description)

Other ways to radiation-harden semiconductors include adding redundancy to circuits or adding circuits that detect and correct errors. This is close to RHBD (Radiation-Hardened by Design), which addresses the problem on the design side. RHBP, on the other hand, is positioned to make it easier to use existing design assets by adjusting the conditions of the manufacturing process. Because it may avoid major redesigns, it is a technology worth watching as a way to widen development options.

However, HARDSIL's general advantages need to be kept separate from the real effects in individual products. How much design time, cost, power consumption, performance, and chip area change depends on the product and manufacturing conditions. This acquisition announcement does not show product-by-product comparison test results or any track record of application to Microchip products.

Why it matters for Microchip

Microchip already offers semiconductors for the aerospace and defense field. By adding VORAGO's radiation-hardened MCUs and MPUs and its process IP for radiation hardening, it may be able to expand its range of control and computing devices for harsh environments. This can also be seen as a move to bring in expertise in design and manufacturing, not just to expand its parts lineup.(Microchip official announcement)

In spacecraft, the work does not end with choosing parts. Engineers design reliability by combining the expected orbit, mission length, radiation dose, shielding with aluminum and other materials, error correction in circuits, and redundancy across the whole system. NASA also cautions that even radiation-hardened parts cannot always withstand every radiation effect.(NASA technical document)

Limits and what to watch next

What this news confirms is the acquisition announcement and the scope of what was acquired. The purchase price, the sites and employees to be integrated, which products and manufacturing lines HARDSIL will be used for, and when customers will adopt it are not clear. Effects such as Microchip growing its market share, shortening development time, or greatly increasing sales are, for now, the company's expectations and future possibilities, not completed results.

Going forward, first, we need to check the product roadmap after integration. Second, it is important to know what level of performance is guaranteed against which radiation environments and failure modes, such as TID and SEE. Third, adoption, certification, and mass production status in actual spacecraft and defense systems will be worth watching. It is important not to judge suitability from the words "radiation-hardened MCU" alone, but to compare test conditions with product specifications.(VORAGO's technical description) (NASA technical document)

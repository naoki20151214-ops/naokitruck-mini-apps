The short answer: decide "your entry point" before the service name

Career-change services include sites where you search jobs yourself, agents you consult with, hiring pages that companies post directly, and entry points strong in manufacturing or staffing jobs. No single one is the right answer.

The semiconductor industry is divided into manufacturing processes, equipment, materials, inspection, quality, design, back-end processes, and more. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's job tag also describes semiconductor manufacturing as work that operates and monitors facilities and equipment, and semiconductor engineers as people involved in a wide range of areas such as process, design, equipment, and materials.

In other words, before choosing a career-change service, it is important to tentatively decide "which entry point am I closest to?"

First choose the semiconductor career route that fits you

In a semiconductor career change, the entry point you should use depends on your background. A complete beginner who wants to start on the factory floor and someone aiming to become an equipment engineer using mechanical, electrical, or maintenance experience should look at different services. First choose the one closest to you from the three routes below.

Route A: Complete beginners who want to start on the floor of a semiconductor fab

Example candidates: Kojo Works / manufacturing job sites

This route enters the semiconductor industry through jobs such as manufacturing operator, inspection, equipment operation, cleanroom work, and shift work. More than education or specialized experience, it is important to check working conditions, location, training, dorms or company housing, and whether there is a path to full-time employment.

Good fit for: people new to manufacturing, people comfortable with factory work, people who want to gain floor experience first, and people open to regional jobs or jobs with dorms. Not a good fit for: people who want to avoid night shifts or shift work, and people who want to aim only for R&D or design from the start.

Route B: People who want to use mechanical, electrical, equipment maintenance, or servicing experience

Example candidates: Meitec Next / agents for technical jobs

This route connects experience in equipment maintenance, tool maintenance, field service, startup, and electrical or mechanical troubleshooting to semiconductor equipment jobs. In job postings, check the equipment you will handle, how often you travel, night shifts and on-call duty, and training.

Good fit for: people with experience in auto repair, industrial machinery, equipment maintenance, electrical work, service engineering, or mechanical and electrical fields. Not a good fit for: people who cannot yet explain their technical experience clearly, and people who just want to collect job posting terms broadly first.

Job sites

Good for people who first want to collect job posting terms.

Search with words like semiconductor, manufacturing, equipment, quality, materials, and FSE. Many jobs come up, so be careful: if you don't narrow down the job type, it is easy to get lost.
Recruitment agents

Good for people who want advice on how to connect their experience to jobs.

Check whether the agent knows semiconductors, manufacturing, and technical jobs well. If you go in without a clear target job type, the suggestions tend to be vague.
Company hiring pages

Good for people who already know which company or process they are aiming for.

Check the department, location, required experience, training, and whether travel is involved. It is important not to judge by company name alone, but to read the job details too.
Manufacturing and staffing jobs

Good for people who want to start as a manufacturing operator or in inspection.

Check the work schedule, shift work, dorms, training, and whether there is a path to full-time employment. Look not only at pay and benefits but also at the duties and the experience you will gain for the future.
Engineer staffing and outsourcing

Good for people who want to gain floor experience in equipment, maintenance, evaluation, analysis, and similar areas.

Check the assignment site, training, equipment you will handle, and long-term career support. The duties change depending on where you are assigned, so checking in advance is important.

Five notes to prepare before talking to an agent

When you use a recruitment agent, just saying "I'm interested in the semiconductor industry" makes the suggested jobs too broad. Writing down the following five points before the meeting helps the conversation move forward.

Start by writing down "just five things"

Preparing for a meeting does not mean writing a perfect résumé. At first, it is enough to briefly give the agent information they can easily connect to semiconductor jobs.

1. ExperienceList experience that seems close to semiconductors, such as manufacturing, maintenance, inspection, quality, chemistry, mechanical, electrical, or data analysis.
2. InterestsNarrow down the entry points you are curious about, such as equipment, materials, cleaning, CMP, inspection, or process improvement, to one or two.
3. ConditionsDecide your must-have conditions first, such as location, shift work, night shifts, travel, relocation, and annual salary.
4. ConcernsNote what you want to confirm, such as whether it is possible without experience, whether there is training, and whether your technical knowledge is enough.
5. Next stepPick just one process article to read or one company name to research before applying.

Also check, in writing, the ways of using services to avoid

On the other hand, using services in the following ways makes it harder to see which semiconductor jobs suit you.

  • Feeling reassured just because you signed up
  • Judging only by the phrase "no experience needed"
  • Asking for advice without stating your target job type, such as manufacturing, equipment, quality, or materials
  • Judging only by company name and not reading the duties
  • Moving forward while leaving location, shift work, and travel unclear

Don't choose based only on "no experience needed"

For people new to semiconductors, "no experience needed" is a reassuring phrase. But if you judge by that alone, you may miss differences in the work. Even among jobs labeled "no experience needed," equipment operation, inspection, equipment maintenance, quality control, and FSE differ in how you work and what experience you gain.

In job postings, check items like these.

  • Whether the process or equipment you will handle is stated
  • Whether the schedule is day shift or shift work
  • Whether there is a training program or training period
  • Whether the employment type is clear: full-time, temporary staffing, contract work, etc.
  • Whether you can gain experience that will expand into maintenance, quality, equipment, or process improvement in the future

Points to check before moving forward
Job availability, selection criteria, pay and benefits, and whether you can be referred vary by company, region, timing, and experience. This page suggests candidates by background, but before applying, always check each service's official information, the job posting, and what is said in your meetings.

Note: the basics of using employment placement services

Employment placement services like recruitment agents are subject to rules on licensing, providing information, and more. When signing up, it is reassuring to check how the service works, how personal information is handled, the range of jobs they introduce, and how you will communicate with the agent.

Job sites tend to be an entry point where you search jobs yourself, and agents tend to be an entry point where you choose where to apply while getting advice. Both are useful, but don't leave everything to them. It is important to know the job posting terms and process terms yourself.

Summary: use career-change services with semiconductor vocabulary in hand

Semiconductor industry job postings can look a little difficult to beginners. But if you split the entry points into manufacturing, equipment, quality, materials, process, and FSE, it becomes easier to see which service to use and how.

  • Tentatively decide your entry point before the service name
  • Complete beginners: manufacturing job sites. Mechanical or electrical experience: agents for technical jobs. Science or manufacturer experience: also look at general services
  • Use job sites to collect terms and agents to translate your experience
  • On company hiring pages, check the department, location, and required experience
  • For manufacturing jobs, check the work schedule, training, and the experience you will gain for the future
  • Before talking to an agent, write down your experience, interests, conditions, and concerns

If you are unsure, follow this order
Complete beginners: "Look at semiconductor manufacturing and inspection jobs on sites like Kojo Works." Mechanical, electrical, or maintenance experience: "Look at equipment jobs through an agent strong in technical jobs, such as Meitec Next."

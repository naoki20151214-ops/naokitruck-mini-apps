In Lesson 1, we saw that cleaning makes up about one-fifth of all process steps, and we looked at the size of particles that cause defects. In this extra lesson, we look at where contamination comes from in the first place, and how fabs prevent it.

The word "cleanroom" is well known, but what "clean" means is defined by numbers.

Cleaning is the process that removes the contamination the cleanroom could not keep out.

① Putting "clean" into numbers: the idea of a class

How clean a cleanroom is depends on "how many particles are allowed in 1 cubic meter of air." The international standard ISO 14644-1 expresses this as a number called the class.

The way the number is set is easy to follow. The class number is the base-10 logarithm of the maximum number of particles 0.1 micrometers or larger (per cubic meter). For example, ISO 5 allows up to 10^5, or 100,000, particles 0.1 micrometers or larger.

Evidence

According to the "Cleanroom" article in the encyclopedia Wikipedia, ISO 5 allows 3,520 particles of 0.5 micrometers or larger per cubic meter, and ISO 7 allows 352,000 particles of the same size.

If the word "logarithm" is hard, read it as "10 to what power." When the class number goes up by 1, the number of allowed particles goes up 10 times.

Let's think in numbers

Comparing particles 0.5 micrometers or larger, ISO 5 allows 3,520 and ISO 7 allows 352,000. 352,000 ÷ 3,520 = 100 times. When the class numbers differ by 2, the number of allowed particles differs by 100 times.

"Class 100" in the old standard FED-STD-209E means up to 100 particles 0.5 micrometers or larger per cubic foot (about 0.0283 cubic meters). Converted to 1 cubic meter, that is 100 × 35.3 ≈ 3,530, almost the same as ISO 5's 3,520. (This standard was canceled in 2001, but it is still widely used.)

Quick quiz: How many times different is the number of allowed particles between ISO 5 and ISO 7?

Answer: 100 times (3,520 vs. 352,000 for particles 0.5 micrometers or larger).

② What class are semiconductor fabs?

According to Wikipedia's "Cleanroom" article, semiconductor fabs often run at ISO 7 or ISO 5, and ISO 1 is very rare. Also, unlike biotech plants, semiconductor cleanrooms do not need to be sterile. What matters is contamination from particles and chemicals, not microbes.

As we saw in Lesson 1, particles that cause defects are about one-quarter to one-half of the line width. Even ISO 5 still allows 3,520 particles 0.5 micrometers or larger per cubic meter. So even if the cleanroom air is clean, cleaning is still needed.

③ Air flows from top to bottom

The core of removing particles is filters and how the air flows. According to Wikipedia's "Cleanroom" article, the ceiling is lined with very fine filters called HEPA or ULPA. These filters catch particles mixed in the air.

A typical way to move the air is one-way flow (laminar flow). Air flows from the ceiling to the floor, all in the same direction. This keeps particles from being stirred up and carries them together into the gaps in the floor. This flow is commonly used over about 80% of the ceiling.

Rooms in semiconductor fabs are kept at positive pressure, with air pressure slightly higher than outside. This keeps dirty outside air from coming in even when a door is opened.

Why make the air flow "in one direction"?

Because if the air swirls, particles that have settled get stirred up again. If air flows in one direction, particles are carried in order toward the side without filters (the floor).

Quick quiz: Why does a cleanroom make air flow from the ceiling to the floor in one direction?

Answer: So particles that fall are not stirred up again and are carried into the gaps in the floor.

④ The biggest source of contamination is "people"

According to Wikipedia's "Cleanroom" article, the biggest source of contamination in a cleanroom is said to be the people working there. Particles flake off people's skin and hair. That is why there are rules like these.

  • Before entering, pass through an airlock (a small room with double doors) or an air shower (a booth that blows air to knock off particles)
  • Wear a cleanroom suit (hood, mask, gloves, boots, and coveralls)
  • Do not bring in things that shed particles easily, such as paper, pencils, and natural-fiber cloth (they are banned in many places)

Also, if the air gets too dry, static electricity can build up and damage components. For this reason, ionizers (devices that remove static) are used, and workers often wear conductive boots.

Only the "lithography" area, where light is shone on the wafer to transfer patterns, has yellow lighting. This keeps the light-sensitive chemical (photoresist) from being exposed by short-wavelength light (blue or ultraviolet).

Let's think in numbers

Let's get a better feel for the number "up to 3,520 particles 0.5 micrometers or larger" in ISO 5 air. In a room with 1 square meter of floor and a 2-meter ceiling, or 2 cubic meters of air, that is 3,520 × 2 = 7,040 particles. For ISO 7, it is 352,000 × 2 = 704,000. By this math, that many invisible particles are floating around.

Quick quiz: What is the biggest source of contamination in a cleanroom?

Answer: The people working there (because particles come off their skin and so on).

Key points of this extra lesson

How clean a cleanroom is, is expressed as a "class." ISO 5 and ISO 7 differ by 100 times in allowed particles. Semiconductor fabs are often ISO 7 or ISO 5, and air flows one way from the ceiling filters to the floor. People are the biggest source of contamination, so cleanroom suits and air showers are needed. Particles still remain, so cleaning is needed.

Word review

Class: A number that shows how clean a cleanroom is. When the number goes up by 1, the number of allowed particles goes up 10 times.

HEPA/ULPA: Very fine filters that catch particles mixed in the air.

Laminar flow: A way of moving air in one direction without letting it swirl.

Positive pressure: Keeping the air pressure in a room higher than outside so outside air does not come in.

Air shower: A booth that blows air to knock off particles stuck to your body.

Common misconceptions

Q. If a wafer is in a cleanroom, does it stay free of contamination?

No. Even ISO 5 allows up to 3,520 particles 0.5 micrometers or larger per cubic meter. Contamination also comes from chemicals, equipment, and people. That is why cleaning is needed.

Q. Is a semiconductor cleanroom a sterile room with no bacteria?

No. Semiconductor fabs do not need to be sterile. What matters is contamination from particles and chemicals.

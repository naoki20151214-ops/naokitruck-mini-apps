Even when a job posting says "equipment," "process," "FSE," or "quality," the job title alone makes it hard to tell whether the job fits you. The scope of the same job title also varies from company to company.

Start by searching from the work you have done so far, not from job titles. If you operated machines, look at manufacturing operator jobs. If you inspected and repaired equipment, look at maintenance and equipment jobs. If you fixed machines at customer sites, look at FSE jobs. If you inspected products, look at inspection and quality jobs. If you improved results by looking at conditions and data, you have a link to process jobs.

This article briefly sorts out the work, related experience, and the words to search for in job postings. When you find a job that interests you, check the detailed job article for its scope and working style.

Why is it hard to pick a job close to your experience from the title alone?

An "equipment engineer" might keep equipment running stably inside a fab, or might go from the equipment maker to customer sites to support equipment. "Quality" jobs also differ in focus: inspection and measurement, quality control, or root-cause analysis.

So at first, compare your daily work with the job description in the posting, rather than looking for a matching title. Having related experience does not guarantee you meet the requirements or will be hired, but it is a good starting point for reading postings.

Find a related job from the work you have done

I operated machines and made products following set procedures

Experience loading materials into equipment, checking screens and displays, and reporting problems overlaps with the work of a manufacturing operator at a semiconductor fab. In job postings, look for words like "manufacturing," "equipment operation," "machine operator," "cleanroom," and "shift work."

You can compare entry points and working conditions for factory jobs, including manufacturing, inspection, and maintenance, in our guide to factory jobs for people new to semiconductors.

For the next question, "Who fixes the equipment when it stops?", see our guide to maintenance jobs, which covers inspections, parts replacement, and recovery.

I inspected equipment, replaced parts, and narrowed down the causes of breakdowns

Experience inspecting or repairing factory equipment, machinery, electrical systems, or cars has links to equipment maintenance for semiconductor manufacturing equipment. In job postings, check for "preventive maintenance," "parts replacement," "recovery from abnormalities," "troubleshooting," and "shift work."

The scope of maintenance and equipment engineer jobs overlaps depending on the company. To see how they differ from jobs that also handle equipment start-up and improvement, read what equipment engineers do.

I went to customer sites to install and repair machines

Experience repairing machines at customer sites, working as a service engineer, or doing on-site maintenance may be close to a field service engineer (FSE) on the equipment maker side. In job postings, look for "customer site support," "equipment start-up," "business travel," "on-call," and "work reports."

Maintenance staff based inside a fab and FSEs who support customers from the maker side differ in their role and how much they travel. For details, see what semiconductor FSEs do and how they work.

I inspected products and checked measurements and quality records

Experience with visual inspection, dimensional measurement, quality records, and judging against standards connects to semiconductor inspection and quality evaluation. In job postings, look for "visual inspection," "measurement," "defect inspection," "quality evaluation," and "reliability evaluation."

For the work involved in inspection and quality evaluation, and how inspection, quality control, and analysis differ, see our guide to inspection and quality evaluation. If you want to know what kinds of problems are found in semiconductors, you can also read our guide to defect inspection.

After a problem is found, the work of finding out "what caused it" is covered in failure analysis. If you want to track a drop in the good-product rate across multiple lots and measurement results, read yield analysis next.

I adjusted conditions and used data to find causes and improvements

Adjusting process conditions, comparing measurements, and investigating the causes of variation and defects all have links to the work of a process engineer. In job postings, look for "condition setting," "mass-production start-up," "process improvement," "SPC," and "yield improvement."

To learn what process engineers look at when tuning conditions, see our guide to process engineers. To learn how to spot process problems from changes in measurements, see our guide to SPC.

Besides the job title, what words should you look for in a job posting?

  • Manufacturing operator: equipment operation, machine operator, inspection, cleanroom, shift work
  • Maintenance / equipment: inspection, preventive maintenance, parts replacement, start-up, troubleshooting
  • FSE: customer site, business travel, installation, field service, on-call
  • Inspection / quality: visual, measurement, defect inspection, quality records, reliability evaluation
  • Process: condition setting, mass-production start-up, SPC, process improvement, yield

Job titles vary by company. If you see these words, read the job description section and check which equipment, processes, and data you would handle, and whether training is provided.

Once you understand the work, check the conditions in the job posting

After you understand the work, look at the location, employment type, working hours, shift work, business travel, requirements, and training. Even if the job title is close to your experience, working style and required qualifications differ from posting to posting.

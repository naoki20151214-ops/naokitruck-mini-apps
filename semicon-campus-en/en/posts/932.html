Do you think a chip's power wiring is built only on the front side?

In leading-edge logic, engineers are not only shrinking transistors. They are also trying to change the design of the "roads" that deliver power.

Backside Power Delivery is a group of technologies that builds the power wiring network, which used to share the front-side wiring layers with signals, separately on the back side of the wafer. The goal is not simply that the back side is closer. It is to untangle the problem of signal and power fighting over limited front-side wiring resources, and to make room for delivering power with lower voltage loss.

Main sources: imec Backside power delivery, Intel PowerVia Test.

  • Why the signal and power "roads" get crowded as chips shrink.

Power inside a chip is not a single wire

Power received from outside the chip does not reach every transistor all at once. The power distribution network (PDN), which carries VDD and VSS, divides power from thick main lines into fine branch lines. Along the way, there are multiple metal layers and the vias that connect them.

Traditionally, this PDN has been placed in the front-side BEOL, on the side where the transistors are built. The BEOL contains not only power wiring but also signal wiring. Signals carry information between circuits, and power supplies voltage and current to each circuit. Their roles differ, but they share the same wiring space.

Naoki: What's the problem with putting signal lines and power lines on the same layer?

Professor Owl: Signals need to be routed densely, while power lines need to be wide and plentiful to keep resistance low. As transistors increase, the two demands collide, like more and more cars on the same road.

The smaller the wiring, the harder it is for power

For an idealized conductor, resistance R can be thought of in terms of resistivity ρ, length L, and cross-sectional area A, as R=ρL/A. For the same material and length, a smaller cross-section tends to increase resistance. Leading-edge wiring also needs a barrier and liner around the metal, so even when a line is made thinner, the metal does not necessarily stay in the same proportion.

When current I flows through a power line with resistance R in its path, the load point loses roughly I×R of voltage. This is IR drop. Even if the same voltage is supplied from outside, if the voltage that arrives drops depending on location in the chip, it can affect transistor drive strength and timing margins.

When the load switches suddenly, voltage droop appears: a change over time that involves not only resistance but also the impedance and capacitance of the whole power network. Static IR drop and dynamic droop are not the same term, but both relate to power integrity, meaning the challenge of delivering the needed voltage stably.

So the power roads are split off to the back side

BSPDN is the idea of using the front side mainly for signals and the back side mainly for power. It does not mean removing power wiring from the front side completely. The core idea is to move power paths to the back side so front-side wiring resources are easier to use for signals.

Backside metal is built on the back side and connected to standard cells and the area near the source/drain through backside vias, nano-TSVs, BPR, direct backside contacts, and so on. BPR stands for buried local power rail, a local embedded rail; it does not mean the same thing as BSPDN as a whole. The combination of structures differs among companies and research institutes.

The effect of this approach depends on design conditions. It can sometimes relieve front-side congestion and may improve power path resistance, IR drop, and freedom in cell placement. But it is not a story of "moving power to the back always makes chips faster" or "IR drop becomes zero."

The real challenge is connecting accurately from the back side

To put metal on the back side, you must align the flipped wafer precisely without damaging the fine structures already built on the front side. In a typical flow, the wafer is bonded to a carrier, flipped, ground thin, vias are formed from the back side, and insulating films and metal are deposited.

Thinning the wafer is important to shorten the distance from the back side to the connection points on the front side. On the other hand, thin wafers are hard to handle, and bonding, grinding, and polishing can cause warping and stress. Because the fine front-side structures must be aligned from the back side, overlay and metrology also become harder. Beyond designing an electrically good structure, you need integration that allows mass production without breakage, misalignment, or variation.

The move to mass production differs by company

Backside Power Delivery is neither a technology limited to research nor one that every company has already adopted in the same form. Intel has announced that 18A, which includes PowerVia, entered production in 2025. TSMC's A16 uses Super Power Rail, and official materials say mass production is planned for the second half of 2026. Samsung's SF2Z also features an optimized BSPDN, with mass production said to be planned for 2027.

Company announcements may include PPA figures for a specific test chip, node, or set of design conditions. For example, the improvements reported for Intel's PowerVia test chip are not guaranteed values for BSPDN in general. It is important to read the technology concept and the published figures for individual products separately.

Summary

1. As chips shrink, signal and power compete for front-side wiring resources.

2. Thin metal and vias make resistance, IR drop, and reliability problems worse.

3. BSPDN separates power to the back side, making the front side easier to use for signals.

4. The key to success is connecting accurately and mass-producing without breaking thin wafers.

In other words, Backside Power Delivery is not just about moving power to the back of the chip. It is an update to the wiring architecture that splits the roads inside the chip into front and back, so power and signals can still be delivered as the number of transistors keeps growing.

Common questions

Does moving power to the back side always improve performance?

Not always. The results depend on power path resistance, cell placement, signal routing, voltage conditions, the type of design, and more. BSPDN is an option for reducing the constraint of power and signals sharing the same front-side roads. It is important not to treat improvements shown for a specific test chip or product as guaranteed values for every chip.

Are BPR and BSPDN the same thing?

No. BPR refers to a buried power rail, a local embedded power rail. BSPDN is the concept of the entire power delivery network, including the backside metal network and connection structures. BPR may be used as a building block of BSPDN, but swapping the terms leads to misunderstanding the structure.

Are GAA transistors and BSPDN one technology?

They are technologies at different layers. GAA is a device architecture in which the gate surrounds the channel to control the transistor's current. BSPDN is an interconnect architecture that delivers VDD and VSS to circuits that include such transistors. They may be combined in the same process generation, but their purposes are different.

Why does the wafer need to be thinned?

To reach the connection points on the front side from the back side with vias and so on. But it is not enough to simply make it thin. Carrier bonding, flipping, grinding, polishing, backside lithography, and metal formation all have to be done stably. Warping and stress in thin wafers, alignment with front-side structures, and reliability around vias make mass production difficult.

How to read the news

When you see the terms PowerVia, Super Power Rail, or BSPDN in the news, first check "which company, which node, and which structure." Next, separate whether the story is about freeing up front-side signal routing, about IR drop or voltage droop, or about manufacturing integration and yield. Finally, check whether it is already in mass production, planned for mass production, or a research demonstration. That makes it harder to overestimate where the technology stands today.

Even if you place chiplets side by side in one package, they cannot work as one system if each one "talks" in a different way. UCIe is an industry standard that sets common rules for connecting dies to each other over short distances at high speed inside a package. It makes chiplet designs easier to combine.

First, what makes chiplet-to-chiplet connections hard?

A die is a single piece of circuitry cut from a wafer. Chiplet design combines multiple dies, so it needs new connections that send data across die boundaries.

But if each company uses its own signal voltages, connection-point layout, communication speed, error detection, startup settings, and way of appearing to higher-level software, a custom design is needed every time separately made dies are connected. It is not enough for the signal just to arrive. Both sides must read the information the same way, recover from problems, and start up as one system.

What is UCIe?

UCIe (Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express) is an open industry specification that standardizes the die-to-die interface inside a package. The UCIe Consortium released its first specification in 2022. It covers the physical layer, die-to-die protocol, software model, and compliance testing as one framework.

The name includes "Chiplet," but UCIe does not define everything about a die's internal circuits or manufacturing process. Its main focus is the communication interface at the boundary between neighboring dies. It also does not lock packaging into a single method.

You now understand the basics. UCIe is "a specification that aligns the entry points and procedures dies use to talk to each other." Below, we look at those entry points in three layers: the physical layer, the protocol, and the software model.

Answer to Episode 36: How does a UCIe signal travel?

In one line for beginners: the sending PHY outputs bits as electrical signals, they pass through connection points and short wiring in the package, and the receiving PHY reads them.

1. Sending sideThe PHY's transmit circuit outputs 0s and 1s as electrical changes.
2. Crossing the boundaryThe signal passes through connection points such as bumps and wiring in the package to reach the neighboring die.
3. Receiving sideThe other die's PHY receive circuit reads the signal and passes it on to the next communication step.

This is a conceptual picture to help you understand the signal path. It does not pin down the actual circuits, waveforms, or wiring details. The physical layer makes electrical sending and receiving possible, and the protocol sets the order and format for handling the data that arrives. Die-to-die communication does not work with only one of them.

Understanding the three layers in plain terms

1. Physical layer: actually carrying 0s and 1s

The physical layer (PHY) is the lowest layer, which sends and receives bits as electrical signals. It covers the layout of bumps (the connection points), the lanes that carry data, clocks, electrical characteristics, and the steps to bring up the link. UCIe is designed for use with both standard organic substrates and higher-density advanced packaging, and with each generation it has expanded its scope to 3D integration.

The key point is that it is optimized for short distances within the same package, not for external PC cables. Short distances make it easier to raise bandwidth density and power efficiency, but the precision of package wiring and bumps affects performance.

2. Protocol: aligning the meaning and reliability of the information

A protocol is the set of conversation rules: what form information is sent in and how order and errors are handled. UCIe's Die-to-Die Adapter supports link state management, error detection with CRC, retransmission, and more. CRC is a method for checking whether sent data has changed along the way.

Above it, there are standard ways to carry PCIe and CXL, and depending on the use, other streaming protocols such as Raw Mode can also be handled. Raw Mode is not "a mode where everything automatically becomes compatible"; the protocol being used also needs alignment and verification.

3. Software model: making it easier to use from existing systems

The software model is the approach to how the connected device appears to, is configured by, and is used by the OS, firmware, and drivers. By using the established PCIe and CXL models, software may be able to treat the connected functions much like existing devices or memory resources, even though the dies are separate.

However, using UCIe does not mean applications will always run without changes. Firmware and drivers still need to be designed for the connected functions, the chosen protocol, and the system configuration.

What does standardization improve?

Less rebuilding of custom connectionsUsing a common boundary specification reduces the burden of designing proprietary I/O from scratch for each die.
A common basis for verificationCompliance and interoperability tests make it easier to check connection problems from a shared viewpoint.
More reusable IPVerified PHY and controller IP becomes easier to use across multiple products.
Supplier choicesIn the future, it can be the basis for a market that combines dies from different design companies and foundries.

IP here means circuit blocks and design data that can be reused in semiconductor design. If standardization leads to shared PHYs, controllers, verification environments, and package designs, it may help reduce development time and design risk.

"Freely mixing dies from different companies" is still a way off

The UCIe Consortium envisions a future where chiplets from multiple vendors are combined. But we are not yet at the stage where any die that supports the specification can be picked like a mail-order part and is sure to work.

Same supported range?Speed, lane count, protocols, optional features, generation, and package conditions must match.
Can it be physically assembled?Die size, bump layout, pitch, package wiring, and assembly process must be aligned.

The official UCIe 2.0 materials also explain that a broad "plug-and-play" chiplet market still faces challenges, including testing, management, and debugging. A standard is not magic that guarantees a finished product. It is a foundation that lets everyone involved solve problems from the same starting point.

Where is it used?

Expected uses include cloud, servers, high-performance computing, AI accelerators, memory, storage, networking, 5G, edge devices, and automotive. Possible configurations include adding more compute dies, separating out I/O dies, placing accelerator or memory functions close by, and connecting to optical I/O chiplets.

However, not every small device needs UCIe. For products where a single die is enough, the benefit may be small compared with the extra PHY area, packaging, and verification costs.

Frequently asked questions

Q. Is UCIe the chiplet itself?

No. A chiplet refers to the idea of building a system from multiple dies, or to the dies used for that. UCIe is the common interface that connects those dies.

Q. Are UCIe and PCIe the same?

No. PCIe defines a general-purpose I/O architecture and protocol. UCIe lets the PCIe model and traffic be used over die-to-die connections inside a package.

Q. What is CXL used for?

It is used to handle cache coherency and the meaning of memory reads and writes between CPUs, accelerators, and memory. UCIe can serve as the base that carries that traffic between dies.

Q. If a die supports UCIe, can I freely connect another company's die?

Not without conditions. Supported speeds and protocols, bump layout, package, power, heat, verification, and supply terms all need to match. Standardization is an important prerequisite, but on its own it does not guarantee compatibility as a finished product.

Q. Does using UCIe always mean higher speed and lower power?

No. The specification aims for efficient short-distance connections, but actual performance and power depend on the PHY, package, speed settings, traffic volume, assembly quality, and system design.

Q. What changed in UCIe 3.0?

UCIe 3.0, released in 2025, added data rates of 48 GT/s and 64 GT/s and expanded management, power saving, emergency notifications, and more. GT/s is the number of signal transfers per second, which is different from the amount of real data an application receives.

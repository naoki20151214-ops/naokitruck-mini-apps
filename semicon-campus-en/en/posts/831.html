Inside smartphones and computers, voltage states are treated as 0s and 1s.

But the numbers "0" and "1" are not flowing through the transistors.
What actually moves is voltage, charge, and current.
A transistor uses voltage to control a path for current, and we give the resulting voltage at a circuit node the meaning of 0 or 1.

The short answer: The voltage between the gate and the source changes whether a MOSFET conducts. Circuits that pull a node up toward the power supply (pull-up) are combined with circuits that pull it down toward GND (pull-down). As a result, a connection point in the circuit becomes High or Low, and voltage ranges set by the specification are read as logic 1 or logic 0. Building these up into CMOS inverters, logic gates, adders, registers, and ALUs turns them into CPU computation.

CircuitPaths to the power supply and to GND create the node voltage.

0 and 1 are "meanings" given to voltages, not single voltages

The 0 and 1 of digital circuits are an abstraction for treating the continuous voltages and charges of the physical world as two states. A node in a circuit has a real voltage, but designers judge that voltage as Low or High. In other words, numbers themselves are not moving inside the CPU. We are reading changes in electrical states as numbers.

The important point here is that 0 and 1 are not always "0 V" and "5 V." Different supply voltages and logic families use different voltages. Inputs have a range below which Low is guaranteed and a range above which High is guaranteed. In between is a region where the circuit cannot guarantee either 0 or 1.

Outputs also have a maximum voltage they can output as Low and a minimum voltage they can output as High. When there is a margin between what the output guarantees and what the next stage judges, the next circuit can still receive the same logic value even if a little noise or wiring loss is added. This margin is called the noise margin.

A MOSFET changes the current path with its gate voltage

Now let's see how voltage states are created. A MOSFET is a voltage-controlled device with four terminals: gate, source, drain, and body. For beginners, people sometimes say "applying voltage to the gate makes current flow." More precisely, the voltage between the gate and the source, called VGS, changes the state of the channel.

In an nMOS, when VGS gets high enough, an inversion layer forms under the gate and acts as a path for current, making it easier for the source and drain to connect electrically. If VGS does not meet the condition, the main path does not form. A pMOS has the opposite polarity: it turns on more easily when the gate is sufficiently lower than the source.

However, "off" in a real device does not mean the current is exactly zero. There is subthreshold current, leakage, and parasitic capacitance. Digital circuits convert these analog device properties into High and Low, which are easier to handle in design.

Check the boundary: The threshold voltage VTH is a guide for when a channel forms and conduction begins. On the other hand, the digital input levels VIH and VIL are specifications for how the next stage judges High and Low. The symbols look similar, but they are not the same thing.

Pull-up and pull-down decide the circuit node voltage

The on/off state of a single transistor is not simply called 0 or 1. Several transistors are combined, and the voltage of a circuit node is created by connecting that node either to the VDD power side or to the GND side. The path that pulls the node up toward the power supply is the pull-up, and the path that pulls it down toward GND is the pull-down.

This difference is why we cannot simplify things to "on is 1, off is 0." In some circuits, when an nMOS turns on, it pulls the output node down toward GND and creates Low, meaning logic 0. A transistor's state is the device's conduction state, while a logic value is the meaning given to the node voltage created by the whole circuit.

A CMOS inverter flips its input to make 0 and 1

One of the most basic circuits is the CMOS inverter. A pMOS on top acts as the pull-up and an nMOS below acts as the pull-down. They are placed in series, and the same input goes to both gates. When the input is Low, the pMOS turns on and the nMOS turns off, so the output is pulled up toward VDD and becomes High.

When the input becomes High, the pMOS turns off and the nMOS turns on. The output node is pulled down toward GND and becomes Low. This creates a NOT gate, which outputs the opposite logic value of its input.

One reason CMOS is so widely used is that, in a stable High or Low state, mainly only one of the pMOS or nMOS is on, so a DC path from VDD to GND rarely forms. However, because of leakage current, both transistors conducting briefly during switching, and the charging and discharging of load capacitance, power consumption is not zero.

With the flow so far in mind, you can see that 0 and 1 are not "numbers inside a transistor" but voltage states created by combinations of transistors. Next, we combine these inverting circuits and move on to computation.

Combining logic gates lets you compute conditions

Extending the CMOS inverter lets you build logic gates such as NAND, NOR, AND, OR, and XOR. A gate is a circuit that returns a set output based on the combination of 0s and 1s at its inputs. For example, AND returns 1 only when all inputs are 1. XOR returns 1 when the two inputs are different.

In CMOS, the pull-up network on the pMOS side and the pull-down network on the nMOS side are designed to complement each other. By deciding under which input conditions the output connects to the power supply and under which it connects to GND, a logic expression can be turned into voltage paths.

NAND and NOR are also called universal gates. Using only NAND, or only NOR, you can build NOT, AND, and others, and eventually any Boolean logic. A real CPU is not a simple circuit that can be fully explained with these alone, but they are an important starting point for building up logic.

An adder makes a "sum" and a "carry" from 0s and 1s

Once you have logic gates, you can also express arithmetic as circuits. First, consider a half adder, which adds two 1-bit values. 0 plus 0 gives a sum of 0. 0 plus 1, or 1 plus 0, gives a sum of 1. 1 plus 1 gives a sum of 0 with a carry of 1. The "sum" can be made with XOR, and the "carry" with AND.

Real multi-bit addition also has to take in the carry from the previous digit, so it uses full adders. Chaining as many full adders as there are digits gives a multi-bit adder. Here, simply converting High and Low voltages with logic gates produces the two results needed for computation.

Registers hold logic states, and the ALU builds up computation

In combinational circuits, the output changes when the input changes. But a CPU needs a way to temporarily hold values in the middle of a computation. So it uses registers, which hold logic states in sync with a clock. A register is a memory circuit made of several bits side by side, and it passes data inside the CPU on to the next step.

Around the registers are MUXes that decide which data to select, circuits that compare values, and circuits that decode instructions and control the paths. The ALU, which brings together adders and logic gates, carries out parts of instructions such as addition, logic operations, and comparisons.

On top of that, register files, control logic, and the wiring that carries data combine to form the CPU's datapath. In other words, going from transistors to a CPU is not just connecting huge numbers of switches. It is a hierarchy that combines voltage states as logic and holds, selects, and computes them in step with time.

Common misconceptions, sorted out

If a transistor is on, is it always 1?

No. It depends on which path it is placed in. A typical example is the CMOS inverter, where an nMOS turning on pulls the output down toward GND and creates logic 0.

Is 0 always 0 V and 1 always 5 V?

No. Low and High are voltage ranges in the circuit specification. The boundaries differ by supply voltage and logic family, and 0 V and 5 V are just one example.

Does a single electron represent 0 or 1?

In ordinary CMOS logic, collective physical states such as the voltage or charge of a circuit node are treated as binary values. Particles of numbers are not flowing through it.

Does one transistor store one bit?

A typical SRAM cell is usually made of several transistors, and DRAM combines a transistor with a capacitor. The storage method differs by type of circuit.

Summary
A transistor is not 0 or 1 itself. It is a device that uses voltage to control a path for current.
Pull-up and pull-down make a circuit node High or Low, and that range is read as logic 1 or 0.
Building up into logic gates, adders, registers, and ALUs creates CPU computation.

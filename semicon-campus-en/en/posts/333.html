CD-SEM and SEM review are electron-beam tools that often come up in semiconductor inspection and metrology. Both are similar in that they "look closely with a SEM," but their roles are a little different. A CD-SEM measures dimensions such as line width and hole diameter. SEM review takes suspicious spots found by defect inspection, examines them at high magnification, and classifies what kind of defect they are. In this beginner-friendly article, we explain the difference between CD-SEM and SEM review, how they relate to yield improvement, and the keywords you see in job postings. The goal is not just to memorize tool names, but to be able to show in interviews and job applications that you understand inspection and metrology work.

First, what is a SEM?

SEM stands for Scanning Electron Microscope. It shines a tightly focused electron beam onto the surface of a sample and uses signals that come off it, such as secondary electrons and backscattered electrons, to view the shape and roughness of the surface as an image.

An optical microscope uses light, so there is a limit to how fine a detail it can see. A SEM, on the other hand, uses an electron beam, so it is good at observing the fine patterns and tiny defects of semiconductors. In semiconductor fabs, it is used not just to "take nice pictures," but to judge whether a process is going as intended.

In beginner terms, a SEM is "a tool that turns tiny shapes you can't see into images using an electron beam."

But in semiconductor manufacturing, looking is not the end. Based on the images, you measure dimensions, classify defects, and review process conditions.

A CD-SEM is a SEM that measures dimensions

The CD in CD-SEM stands for Critical Dimension. In semiconductors, it refers to important dimensions that affect performance and yield, such as the line width, hole diameter, and space width of circuit patterns. A CD-SEM is a tool that looks at the fine patterns made on a wafer in SEM images and measures their dimensions as numbers.

For example, a line made by lithography is too thick, a hole becomes too small after etching, or the line width varies by location even in the same pattern. Problems like these affect the performance and yield of the final chip. A CD-SEM makes that deviation visible as a number, such as "off by so many nm," instead of "something seems off."

This is also important for your career.

As semiconductors keep shrinking, "measuring technology" becomes as important as "manufacturing technology." If you see words like "CD measurement SEM," "CD-SEM," "metrology recipe," or "process control" in a job posting, it is easier to understand if you think of it not as simple tool operation, but as work that looks at process variation in numbers.

SEM review is a SEM that examines defects in detail

SEM review is a step where suspicious spots found by defect inspection tools are magnified with a SEM and examined in detail. A defect inspection tool scans the whole wafer and outputs coordinates saying "this spot looks suspicious." Then the SEM review tool moves to those coordinates and takes a close look at what the defect actually is.

There are many types of defects, such as particles, scratches, bridges, missing patterns, foreign matter, and film residue. Just knowing that a defect exists is not enough to fix the process. It is important to classify the defect so you can think about whether "this came from cleaning," "from etching," "is a lithography condition problem," or "is a scratch made during transfer or inside the tool."

The difference between CD-SEM and SEM review

CD-SEM and SEM review are both SEM-type tools, but their purposes differ. A CD-SEM is mainly a tool for measuring dimensions. SEM review is mainly a tool for examining defects in detail.

Why they help with yield improvement

Yield is the percentage of chips made that can actually be used as good products. CD-SEM and SEM review are not magic tools that directly raise yield. But they are tools that make the causes of lower yield visible.

For example, if a CD-SEM shows that line width is thinner than the target, that gives you a clue to review lithography or etching conditions. If SEM review shows that a defect is a particle, you can suspect cleaning, transfer, or contamination inside the tool. If there are many bridges, you need to review pattern formation or etching conditions.

In other words, inspection and metrology do not just "reject bad chips." They find where, why, and how defects are appearing, and create information to make the next lot and the next process better.

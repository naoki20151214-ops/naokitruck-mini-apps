① Rinsing after cleaning uses pure water

In Figure 1 of Lesson 3, there was a "rinse with pure water" step after both SC-1 and SC-2. It is an important task that washes away the chemicals. The water used here is not tap water but ultrapure water (UPW). It is water with almost all impurities, such as ions, removed.

How clean it is, is expressed by resistivity. Resistivity is how hard it is for electricity to pass through. The more ions are dissolved in water, the more easily electricity passes (lower resistivity), so the higher the resistivity, the fewer the ions and the cleaner the water. The unit is MΩ·cm (megohm-centimeters).

Evidence

ASTM's (a US standards organization) "guide for ultrapure water used in the electronics and semiconductor industries" (D5127) lists 18.2 MΩ·cm (at 25°C) as the resistivity at the point of use for generations with fine line widths (0.35 to 0.032 micrometers). The theoretical upper limit, even for water with no ions at all, is said to be 18.25 MΩ·cm. The 2015 ITRS guidelines call for a value greater than 18.0.

Let's think in numbers

The difference between the theoretical limit of 18.25 and the standard's 18.2 is 18.25 − 18.2 = 0.05. This means using water that is clean almost to the limit.

The inverse of resistivity is how easily electricity passes (conductivity). 1 ÷ 18.2 ≈ 0.055 microsiemens per centimeter. This water barely conducts electricity.

Quick quiz: What does high resistivity in ultrapure water mean?

Answer: That there are few ions (impurities) in the water.

② What happens to impurities in rinse water?

If ions remain in the rinse water, they can stick to the wafer surface and harm its electrical properties. Also, references say pure water has the property of dissolving a very small amount of the wafer's silicon. Clean does not mean safe. You need to think about rinse time and even how the wafer is dried afterward.

③ Drying can leave "watermarks"

After rinsing, the water is dried off. If the water evaporates here, silicon compounds and other substances dissolved in it are left on the wafer surface. These marks are called watermarks (water stains). They look like small stains, but if they get between fine lines, they cause defects.

In other words, instead of just letting water evaporate, it is important to remove the water from the wafer before it evaporates.

Quick quiz: How do watermarks form?

Answer: Water left on the wafer surface evaporates, and silicon compounds and other substances dissolved in it are left behind as marks.

④ Marangoni drying: using a difference in surface tension

According to patent documents, spin drying (spinning the wafer to fling off water) or IPA vapor drying alone sometimes cannot fully eliminate watermarks. That is why Marangoni drying is widely used.

In Marangoni drying, IPA (isopropyl alcohol) vapor is floated above the water surface, and the wafer is slowly pulled up through it. IPA has lower surface tension (the force that makes a liquid's surface try to shrink) than water, and this difference is generally explained as acting in the direction that pulls water away from the wafer surface.

Let's think in numbers

One patent gives the surface tension at 20°C as about 21 for IPA and about 72 for water (in dynes per centimeter). 72 ÷ 21 ≈ about 3.4 times. Water's surface pulls inward about 3.4 times more strongly.

Key points of this lesson

Rinsing uses ultrapure water, and its cleanliness is expressed by resistivity (around 18.2 MΩ·cm). When water evaporates during drying, watermarks are left. So the water is removed before it evaporates. Marangoni drying uses the fact that IPA has lower surface tension than water.

Word review

Ultrapure water (UPW): Water with almost all impurities, such as ions, removed.

Resistivity: How hard it is for electricity to pass through. For water, a higher value means fewer ions.

Watermark: A mark left by impurities that remain after water evaporates.

IPA: Isopropyl alcohol. A liquid with lower surface tension than water.

Surface tension: The force that makes a liquid's surface try to shrink.

Common misconceptions

Q. Is pure water safe, so it never harms the wafer?

No. References say pure water has the property of dissolving a very small amount of the wafer's silicon. You need to think about time and even how the wafer is dried.

Q. Is drying just a matter of letting the water evaporate?

No. Evaporation leaves watermarks. It is important to remove the water before it evaporates.

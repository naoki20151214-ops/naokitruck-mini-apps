Have you ever taken a photo with your phone and found it blurry because the focus was off? Or it was so bright that it washed out to white? Or so dark you could barely see anything?

Something similar happens in the step that "prints" semiconductor circuits with light. The focus can be off. The amount of light can be too much or too little. When that happens, the width and shape of the printed lines no longer match the design.

Here is the short answer: in the step that prints with light, the shape falls apart unless both "focus" and "amount of light" are within the right range. That is why the pattern is measured and inspected right after it is printed and developed.

The camera comparison only fits in one way: "if focus and brightness are off, the result changes." How a camera works and how a semiconductor exposure tool works are different things.

Lithography is the step that prints circuit patterns with light

Semiconductor circuits are built on a thin disk called a wafer. According to Hitachi High-Tech, each circuit layer is made mainly through three steps: "deposition," which adds a thin film; "pattern transfer," which prints a shape with light; and "etching," which removes film to finish the shape. Of these, "pattern transfer" is lithography (photolithography).

In lithography, a film that changes its properties when hit by light is first coated on top of the film on the wafer. This is called "photoresist." Next, a "photomask" is used. A photomask is a glass master plate with the circuit pattern drawn on it.

The exposure tool shines ultraviolet light through the photomask. The light passes only through the parts of the mask without a pattern. Photoresist that is hit by the light is exposed and changes chemically. A lens shrinks the pattern to a fraction of its size and prints it onto the wafer. After that, the wafer is "developed" to keep the printed shape.

Two conditions that decide the shape: focus and amount of light

When printing with light, "focus" and "dose" are important. Focus means how sharp the image is in focus. ASML explains focus as "how sharp the image is." Dose is the amount of light, meaning the exposure energy given to the resist.

These two are very similar to focus and brightness (exposure) in a photo. When focus is off, the image blurs. When there is too much or too little light, the resist reacts too little or too much.

So how do you find the right values? A document from the nanofab at the University of California, Santa Barbara introduces a test called an "FEM (focus-exposure matrix)." The dose and focus are changed little by little along the rows and columns, and each square of the grid is exposed. Then each square is checked to see whether the resulting shape is within the allowed range. The document notes that in failed squares, sometimes no pattern can be seen at all.

Key points of the diagram

  • Vertical: focus; horizontal: amount of light
  • Blue squares: pass. Gray: fail
  • Choose the center of the passing range

The right range: the "process window"

The area where the passing squares gather is called the "process window." Like a window, it is the region where "printing works well within this range." The UCSB document recommends choosing the center of the window. You only know where the center is after checking all the bad results: dose too high, dose too low, focus too high, and focus too low.

It also warns against choosing the "best square." The best square is often at the edge of the window, so even a small variation can push it out of spec.

A doctoral thesis from TU Wien explains the same idea. The effect of focus changes with dose, so the two are studied by changing them together. A graph of the results is called a "Bossung plot." Besides line width (CD), the graph can also show the slope of the walls (sidewall angle) and how much resist is lost (resist loss). The process window is the set of focus and light-amount combinations where all of these are within spec.

The thesis also says that when defocus (focus error) is large, the pattern becomes more sensitive to other errors, so the usable focus range in practice is narrower than the theoretical value.

There is also a limit to how fine light can print

ASML expresses the smallest printable dimension (CD) as "CD = k1 × wavelength ÷ NA." Wavelength is the length of the light wave. NA (numerical aperture) is a number that shows how well a lens gathers light. k1 is a factor that depends on the process.

From this formula, you can see that the shorter the wavelength of light, and the better the lens gathers light, the finer the shapes that can be printed.

Measure right after developing, while you can still redo it

Whether the printed shape is correct is checked by measuring after development. According to Hitachi High-Tech, after development the dimensions are measured with a CD-SEM (an electron microscope that measures line width). This measurement step is sometimes called "ADI," short for After Development Inspection. Measurements are also taken after etching in the same way. That one is "AEI," or After Etch Inspection.

Why measure right after developing? According to an announcement from KLA, an inspection equipment maker, right after development, the wafer can be inspected while it can still be "reworked." Once film has been removed by etching, it likely cannot be redone in the same way. Finding defects from the printing step before etching is much better.

The same announcement also says that early defects in the printing step used to be hard to catch reliably. That is why measurement and inspection technology matters.

Position error, or "overlay," is also a lithography defect

Not only the "width" and "shape" of the printed pattern matter, but also its "position." Circuits are built by stacking many layers. ASML calls the accuracy of aligning two layers "overlay."

Key points of the diagram

  • Left: the two layers line up
  • Right: the two layers are shifted
  • The shift is measured and sent back to the exposure tool for correction

Focus and overlay are monitored by measuring how well repeating test patterns (metrology targets) printed on the wafer turned out. The results are sent from tools built into the production line back to the exposure tool and used for correction. In other words, measuring is not only for keeping records; it is also used to fix the next exposure.

How equipment maintenance and production engineering look for the cause of errors

When an error in the printed shape is found on site, it is likely checked in an order like this.

  1. Check the measurement results after development. See how much, and in which direction, the line width and position are off.
  2. Review the focus and dose records. Compare the exposure tool settings with the values actually used.
  3. Sort out where it happens. Summarize where on the wafer, in which lot, on which tool, and when it happened.
  4. Check where the process window is with a test (FEM). See whether the current conditions are at the center of the window or near the edge.
  5. Confirm with more data. Things may have just happened at the same time by chance, so confirm before deciding.

Equipment adjustment and recovery are done according to the official procedures of your fab or equipment maker, under the judgment of authorized people. This article introduces a way of thinking about causes; it does not give instructions for operating equipment.

The work of tracking down this kind of problem is shared among process, production engineering, equipment maintenance, and inspection / metrology staff. Manufacturing experience handling condition records and variation against specs is likely to help you think about "which conditions are within the right range." Word review

Here are the words from this article again, in simple terms.

  • Lithography: The step that prints circuit patterns with light.
  • Photoresist: A film that changes its properties when hit by light. The shape is printed onto it.
  • Photomask: A glass master plate with the circuit pattern drawn on it.
  • Focus: How sharp the image is.
  • Dose: The amount of light. The exposure energy given to the resist.
  • Process window: The range of focus and amount of light where printing works well.
  • ADI: Measuring and inspecting after development.
  • Overlay: The accuracy of aligning two layers.

Common misunderstandings

Q. If the focus is right, does the amount of light not matter?

It does matter. The effect of focus changes with the amount of light. That is why the two are changed together to find the range where printing works well.

Q. Is it safe to use the conditions that gave the best result?

Not necessarily. The best conditions are often at the edge of the range where printing works well, so a small variation can push them out of spec. The basic rule is to choose the center of the range.

Q. If you measure after development, do you not need to measure after etching?

No. You measure both after development and after etching. After development, you check at a stage where rework is still possible; after etching, you check the shape after the film has been removed.

Summary: the printing step depends on the "right range" of focus and amount of light

In the step that prints with light, the shape falls apart unless focus and amount of light are within the right range.
That range is called the process window, and you choose its center, not the best single point.
Measuring and inspecting right after development is done to find defects while rework is still possible.

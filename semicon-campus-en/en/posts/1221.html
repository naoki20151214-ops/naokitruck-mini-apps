On September 22, 2026, ASM announced "XP8 Vertos Flowable Carbon" for semiconductor manufacturing. According to ASM, it uses a deposition technology called PECVD to fill gaps in narrow, deep structures with a carbon film while self-planarizing the film surface. ASM also indicated applications in logic and memory, and initial adoption in high-volume manufacturing (HVM). However, customer names, target products, applied layers, and production volumes have not been disclosed. ASM press release

What was announced

As semiconductors become more 3D, the places where films must go tend to be "narrow and deep." In such areas, it is important to get material all the way inside and fill the space while limiting voids and seams. ASM says Vertos Flowable Carbon fills high-aspect-ratio structures without voids or seams and self-planarizes across the whole wafer.

High aspect ratio means a shape whose depth is large compared with the width of its opening. Because the film must form from a narrow entrance all the way to a deep bottom, uneven filling can leave voids inside or create steps on the surface. This technology gives the film flowability so it spreads inside the structure, positioning gap-fill and planarization on a single PECVD platform. These are ASM's descriptions of product performance, not results of independent mass-production comparison tests. ASM Flowable Carbon technology page

In conventional processes, the surface unevenness after filling is sometimes smoothed with CMP (chemical mechanical polishing) or etch-back. ASM claims that Vertos can, under some conditions, eliminate downstream CMP or etch-back, reducing process complexity, cost, and defect risk. However, it has not disclosed under which conditions how many steps are cut, or whether the steps are fully eliminated or partly reduced. Announcement details

PECVD and planarization explained for beginners

What is PECVD?

PECVD stands for "plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition." Gaseous materials are fed into the tool, and plasma drives chemical reactions that form a thin film on the wafer. Compared with deposition methods that rely on heat alone, it makes it easier to form films at lower temperatures. In semiconductors, the thermal budget, which keeps already-built structures from being damaged by heat, is important, so low-temperature deposition suits complex layered processes. ASM PECVD explanation

Gap-fill and self-planarization

Gap-fill is the step of filling trenches between wires and gaps in 3D structures with film. Planarization means reducing the unevenness left on the surface after filling, preparing it for the next processing step. If surface steps or roughness are large, dimensional variation may become a problem in later lithography or etching.

The aim of Flowable Carbon is to reduce the burden of handling these two as separate steps. ASM's background patent explains that conventional gap-fill with SOC (spin-on carbon) can require additional equipment and steps such as a coater, an oven, coating, baking, and curing. It also describes an embodiment that uses multiple carbon layers with different flowability to fill multiple trenches and obtain a flat, smooth surface. However, we cannot conclude that the patent content is the same as the XP8 Vertos mass-production recipe or its full specs. Published patent US20260082832A1

Why it matters for 3D logic and memory

In logic semiconductors and memory, structures are becoming 3D, and there is a growing need to process narrow grooves and deep trenches uniformly. In 3D NAND, DRAM, 3D logic, and advanced packaging, besides reliably filling gaps, surface flatness, pattern shape, and topography control over regions of different density are important. ASM describes Flowable Carbon as a technology that can be applied to these logic and memory uses. ASM technical explanation

The industrial focus here is not just the novelty of the material name "carbon film." The core question is whether a flowable film can uniformly fill deep structures, become flat enough after filling, and withstand heat and later processing, all at the same time on a PECVD platform built for mass production. If it succeeds, it could simplify processes that combine multiple coating, baking, curing, and polishing steps.

Points to note when calling it "mass production entry"

What we can confirm is that ASM announced the product and technology and said there is initial adoption in HVM. This marks an entry point toward mass-production use, but it does not mean broad adoption across the semiconductor industry or improved mass-production yield. Customer names, fabs, adoption timing, product generations, and production scale have not been disclosed either.

Also, quantitative data such as how much CMP or etch-back is reduced, processing time, throughput, yield, defect density, film thickness, deposition temperature, deposition rate, film stress and hardness, and etch selectivity cannot be confirmed. Phrases like "industry first" and "without voids or seams" must also be treated as company claims. Until third-party comparison tests or independent mass-production data are shown, the performance cannot be generalized as an industry-wide fact. ASM news release

What to watch next

  • Will customer names, target products, and the process layers actually used be disclosed?
  • Will data be shown on defect density, yield, and throughput compared with conventional SOC, CMP, and etch-back?
  • Will fill performance and surface flatness stay stable across structures with different pattern densities and depths?
  • Will the carbon film's thermal stability, shrinkage, stress, and compatibility with etching, ashing, and cleaning be confirmed under mass-production conditions?
  • Will "initial adoption" lead to broad mass-production use and to ASM's revenue?

A fair summary for now is: "ASM announced Flowable Carbon technology that combines gap-fill and self-planarization in PECVD, and disclosed initial HVM adoption for logic and memory." It shows a direction toward simpler processes, but judging how widely it is adopted and how much performance improves requires waiting for customer information and independent mass-production data.

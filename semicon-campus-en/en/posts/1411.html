You close the door of a vacuum tool and start the pump. The number on the display goes down.

But when that number doesn't drop as far as usual, what is going on?

Once you understand how vacuum gauges work, "looking at a number" turns into "thinking about the state of the equipment."

Semiconductor manufacturing equipment pumps a chamber down from atmospheric pressure to low pressure, and processes wafers while letting gas in as needed. The core of this article is how to measure pressure that changes over a huge range, and why several vacuum gauges are used for different jobs. After covering the measurement principles, we look at how people in equipment maintenance and production engineering read the displayed values.

What you will learn in this article

  • Why pressure still exists in a vacuum, and why we measure it
  • What Pirani, diaphragm, and ionization gauges actually sense
  • How to think about the numbers during pumpdown, gas introduction, and abnormal situations

1. Without a vacuum gauge, you cannot tell whether the chamber has reached the state you need

In semiconductor manufacturing equipment, a chamber is the vessel or space where wafers are processed. A running pump does not mean the chamber has reached the pressure it needs. A vacuum gauge is a sensor that turns the pressure in that space into a number.

For example, even with the pump running, the pressure will drop differently if gas keeps leaking in from outside or if the condition of the exhaust path changes. Without a vacuum gauge, you know that "pumping has started," but you cannot use pressure to check "what is happening inside."

Pressure during processing affects the gas, the plasma, how materials are transported, and reaction conditions. Plasma is a state in which part of a gas is ionized and contains electrons and ions. Because a different pressure can mean different process conditions, vacuum gauges are used not only to confirm pumpdown but also to control process conditions. However, it is not as simple as "higher pressure always means better quality."

A photo of a semiconductor cleanroom shows that many tools surround the wafers. The pressure measurement covered here is not about signals inside a chip. It is measurement for knowing the state of this kind of manufacturing equipment.

A wafer being taken out of processing equipment also helps you picture the link between equipment and product. You cannot tell the tool's pressure or vacuum gauge setup from a photo alone, but you can keep in mind the idea of setting up the conditions inside the tool before handling the product.

2. A "vacuum" is not an empty space but a state with little gas

The word vacuum may make you picture a space where all gas is gone. In real vacuum equipment, though, there is usually some gas left. Vacuum is a term for states with a pressure lower than normal atmospheric pressure. It is easier to understand "high vacuum" if you read it as "low pressure."

Pressure is the force that a gas exerts on a wall or other surface, expressed per unit area. At the same temperature, you can think of it this way: the lower the number density of gas molecules, the lower the pressure. Pumpdown (evacuation) is the process of removing gas from the vessel and lowering the pressure.

The unit used is Pa (pascal). Some documents and tools use Torr or mbar, so when you compare numbers, you need to read the units too. Also, "absolute pressure," which treats a perfect vacuum as 0, and "gauge pressure," which is the difference from a reference such as atmospheric pressure, use different references. Even if a product name includes "Gauge," its reading is not necessarily gauge pressure.

A "0" on the display does not prove that there is no gas at all. Read it after checking the instrument's measurement range and its display and adjustment conditions. Zero-point adjustment and calibration, which checks measurement characteristics against a standard, are also two different things.

As pressure drops, the vacuum range changes

Vacuum covers a wide range of pressures. The ranges below are based on an academic society paper about the revision of JIS Z 8126-1:2021. Rather than memorizing the range names, notice that equipment pressure changes by orders of magnitude.[R1]

Vacuum range Pressure p (Pa)
Low vacuum Below atmospheric pressure, 100 or more
Medium vacuum 0.1 or more, below 100
High vacuum 10⁻⁶ or more, below 0.1
Ultra-high vacuum 10⁻⁹ or more, below 10⁻⁶
Extreme-high vacuum Below 10⁻⁹

10⁻⁶ Pa is 0.000001 Pa. Near atmospheric pressure and at such low pressures, the size of the change an instrument detects is also different. Not every semiconductor tool pumps down to the lowest pressures in the table. The pressure needed depends on the process and the equipment design.

3. Why one type of vacuum gauge is not enough

To measure very different pressures, you need a detectable change across a wide range. Gauges that use gas carrying heat, gauges that use a membrane deforming under pressure, and gauges that ionize gas and measure current each have different strong ranges and different conditions that affect the reading.

A method that works when pumpdown starts will not necessarily work with the same accuracy at much lower pressures. On the other hand, a method that measures the high-vacuum side cannot track the whole change from near atmospheric pressure on its own. So several methods are combined, including ranges where their measurement ranges overlap.

In a photo of a cleanroom lined with semiconductor manufacturing equipment, you can imagine instruments mounted inside and around the tools. However, you cannot decide the type of vacuum gauge or where it is connected just from the outside. In practice, you check the equipment documentation.

You do not choose a gauge by a fixed rule like "a Pirani covers X Pa to Y Pa." Check the product model, measurement range, accuracy, target gas, and so on. Even with the same method name, product specs differ.[R6][R9]

4. Reading pumpdown progress from the heat carried by gas: the Pirani gauge

A Pirani gauge is a "thermal conductivity" vacuum gauge that uses the fact that gas carries heat. Inside the sensor is a sensing element, such as a thin heated wire. The gauge uses the fact that the amount of heat escaping to the surrounding gas changes with pressure.

For example, in a design that keeps the sensing element at a constant temperature, the gauge looks at the power supplied to do that. As pumpdown proceeds and there is less surrounding gas, the heat lost through the gas changes. The gauge converts that change into pressure and displays it. ULVAC's official explanation also describes the principle in terms of heat loss and supplied power.[R4]

In the photo of a Pirani sensor with a flange, you can see the part that connects to the equipment and the connector. A flange is the rim-like part used to connect instruments and piping. Pressure readings come through real sensors and wiring like this.

Looking at the sensor part of a Pirani gauge helps you understand that there is a sensing element behind the number on the screen. The photo is a real example to help you picture the principle. This article does not instruct you to take apart or replace any specific model.

A different gas can change the reading, even at the same pressure

How heat is carried also depends on the type of gas. So with a Pirani gauge, you need to think not only about pressure but also about the gas being measured and the instrument's calibration conditions. If you measure a gas different from the one assumed in calibration, the reading can change.[R5]

This is one reason not to treat the number as an absolute truth. Watching the trend of pressure falling after pumpdown starts and comparing exact pressures across different gases are not the same situation. The condition of the instrument itself, such as temperature and contamination of the sensing element, is also something to check.

5. Reading process pressure from membrane deformation: the diaphragm gauge

A capacitance manometer uses the deformation of a thin membrane called a diaphragm. When pressure moves the membrane, the capacitance between the membrane and an electrode changes. Capacitance is a quantity that describes the ability to store electric charge. Because pressure is calculated from this change, it is also called a capacitance diaphragm gauge.

Unlike a Pirani gauge, which measures heat carried by gas, it directly uses the deformation of the membrane caused by pressure, so its key feature is a small dependence on gas type. Official manufacturer documents describe it as gas-type-independent measurement, and it is also used to monitor pressure during processing.[R2][R3]

However, this method also cannot measure all pressures equally well with a single unit. Products differ in full scale, meaning the upper limit of the measurement range, and so on. The idea stays the same: choose one that matches the range and accuracy the process needs.[R9]

6. When there is even less gas, read pressure using ionization

Ionization is when atoms or molecules of a gas become charged ions, for example by losing electrons. An ionization gauge calculates the pressure of the remaining gas from a current related to this phenomenon. It is used to measure the high-vacuum side.

Hot cathode: heating to emit electrons

A hot cathode gauge emits electrons from a heated cathode and uses those electrons to ionize the residual gas. It collects the resulting positive ions, measures the current, and converts it into pressure. The cathode is the electrode that emits electrons in this setup. If you think of it as probing a low-gas state through interactions between gas and electrons, the difference from a Pirani gauge becomes clear.[R7]

Hot cathode gauges also come in different designs, such as the Bayard–Alpert type and the Extractor type. Do not fix the lower and upper measurement limits by method name alone. Check the individual specs. Also, sensitivity changes with gas type, so you need the gas conditions when comparing readings.[R8][R10]

Cold cathode: using a discharge with high voltage and a magnetic field

A cold cathode gauge does not heat a cathode to emit electrons like a hot cathode gauge. Instead, it uses a discharge driven by high voltage and a magnetic field. It measures pressure from the fact that the current related to discharge and ionization changes with pressure. Types include the Penning type and the inverted magnetron type. It is not right to assume, just from the word "cold," that the instrument is unaffected by temperature.[R11]

7. Connecting the roles of vacuum gauges along the equipment timeline

Let's lay the principles so far over how the equipment operates. The following is a general flow for understanding the roles. It is not the operating procedure of a specific tool or a fixed sensor configuration.

  1. Start pumping down from near atmospheric pressure. First, watch how the pressure falls with a vacuum gauge that covers that measurement range.
  2. The pressure drops further. Use the value from a method suited to the high-vacuum side. Where ranges overlap, check the state while keeping each instrument's characteristics in mind.
  3. Reach the required pumpdown state. The pressure reached under specified pumpdown conditions is called the base pressure. Read it against the standard for each process.
  4. Introduce gas and process. Now both gas coming in and gas going out affect the chamber pressure. The role of the instrument that monitors and controls process pressure becomes important.

An MFC (mass flow controller) is a device that controls the flow rate of the gas being introduced. A throttle valve, on the other hand, is a valve that changes how easily gas flows through the exhaust path. Depending on the tool, the system reads the vacuum gauge value and adjusts valves and other parts to control pressure. Setting the flow rate alone does not automatically determine the chamber pressure.

The photo of a research vacuum deposition chamber is there to help you picture concrete things: the vessel, the connections, and the peripheral devices. We do not claim that a photo of research equipment is an actual example of a specific mass-production semiconductor tool or gas control method.

A combination vacuum gauge is an instrument that combines different principles, such as a Pirani and a cold cathode gauge. Even if one product covers a wide range, a single principle is not measuring every pressure in the same way. The product links the roles of several methods inside it.[R12][R13]

8. In equipment maintenance, look at how the number moves, not just how high it is

Even with the same ultimate pressure, the time it took to get there and the changes along the way can differ. Maintenance technicians compare not just a single number but the pressure trend from the start of pumpdown against normal history and equipment conditions. A pumpdown curve is a record of how pressure changed over time.

For example, start from an observation like "it is harder to pump down than usual." At that point, do not immediately decide that the vacuum gauge is broken. There are equipment-side candidates such as leaks letting gas in, the condition of pumps and valves, seals, and gas released from components. There are also measurement-side candidates such as sensor contamination, wiring, power supply, and the state of adjustment or calibration.

Even with the same reading, separate the candidates you consider

  • Pressure does not drop during pumpdown: Compare with normal pumpdown history and list candidates such as the pump, exhaust path, gas inflow, and instrument condition.
  • The reading changed when gas was introduced: In addition to gas flow and valve state, consider the instrument's dependence on gas type.
  • Only one instrument shows a different value: Compare under matched conditions for the other instruments' ranges, measurement locations, and gas conditions, and also look into measurement-side factors.

This is not a table for pinning down the cause from the reading alone. For example, high pressure is a reason to consider a possible leak, but that alone does not prove a leak. If you record the facts you observed separately from the possible causes you inferred from them, it becomes clear what to check next.

Looking again at the exterior of a Pirani gauge, you can be aware of the real chain of "sensor," "connection," and "display." This means there are things to check not only in the equipment's internal state but also in the path that produces the number.

Compare with another vacuum gauge only after matching conditions

When two readings differ, it does not necessarily mean one of them is broken. Pirani and ionization gauges depend on gas type, and sensitivity and calibration factors come into play. Read them while also considering measurement range, location, temperature, contamination, and so on.[R14]

Calibration means checking an instrument's measurement characteristics by comparing it against a standard. Do not assume that a zero adjustment makes the gauge accurate across the whole range. Also, rather than setting one calibration interval for every vacuum gauge, manage it based on the required accuracy, operating conditions, and history.

Whether the equipment stops in an abnormal situation also depends on the design of interlocks and controls. An interlock is a mechanism that allows or restricts operations depending on conditions. This article does not recommend disabling protective functions or doing adjustment work. Follow that equipment's procedures and your assigned scope for any actual checks or operations.

9. "Thinking of possible causes from the numbers" connects to the job

When it comes to understanding equipment maintenance and production engineering jobs, the goal of this lesson is not just to memorize the names of vacuum gauges. What matters is being able to think about which principle produces a number, and what conditions change it.

If you have experience on a factory floor comparing equipment sounds, temperatures, times, and product condition with what is normal, you will find a similar order of thinking here. First, record concretely what is "different from usual." Next, compare it with the movements of the process and equipment. Then, organize the possible causes. In semiconductor equipment, pressure is one of the things you observe.

For example, if you can turn a report of "the vacuum gauge reading is high" into "at this point after pumpdown started, it is higher than the normal history, and the gas introduction conditions were these," it becomes easier to share the situation. Then you think about the candidates separately: the exhaust side, the gas side, and the measurement side. This is practice in turning knowledge from vocabulary into material for making decisions on the job.

Common misconceptions

Q. If the vacuum is high, is the pressure number also large?

It's the opposite. The higher the vacuum, the lower the pressure. Do not mix up "degree of vacuum" and "pressure," and read the display after checking its units and reference. Also, the displayed value alone cannot prove that there is no gas at all.

Q. Should I just use diaphragm gauges, since they depend less on gas type?

Having a small dependence on gas type is different from being able to measure every range. Choose a product that matches the range and accuracy you need, and use other methods depending on the purpose, such as the high-vacuum side. Multiple instruments complement each other in measurement range and use.

Q. If the pressure won't drop, will replacing the vacuum gauge fix it?

The cause of an abnormal reading is not necessarily the instrument. There may be a real change in gas inflow or pumping. Cross-check the normal history, other instruments, pumps and valves, gas conditions, and the state of the measurement system to narrow down the possible causes.

Summary

  • Even a vacuum contains residual gas, and measuring pressure tells you the state of the equipment.
  • Different principles—heat, membrane deformation, and ionization—are used depending on range and purpose.
  • In maintenance, compare how readings move with the conditions, and consider possible causes on the exhaust, gas, and measurement sides.

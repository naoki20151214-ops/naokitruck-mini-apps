On September 25, 2026, STATS ChipPAC, a company that handles semiconductor back-end processes, announced that it had signed a license agreement with the Singapore research institute A*STAR Institute of Microelectronics (A*STAR IME) for Co-Packaged Optics (CPO) advanced packaging technology for AI and high-performance computing. The announcement was presented at A*STAR's "Innovate Together 2026," held the same day. This news is not about CPO itself being newly invented. It is a collaboration that links electronic and optical technologies developed by a research institute with the manufacturing capabilities of an OSAT, a semiconductor assembly and test company, to bring them closer to mass production. However, it does not show that mass production had started as of the announcement. STATS ChipPAC official announcement, A*STAR official event information

What was announced

According to STATS ChipPAC's announcement, the company will license A*STAR IME's advanced packaging solution for CPO and combine it with its own expertise in advanced packaging and high-volume manufacturing. The goal is to move technology from the R&D stage into a form that is easier to manufacture and to speed up the shift to high-volume production. Target markets listed include AI, high-performance computing, data centers, and edge AI.

Let's sort out the dates. September 25 is both the day of the event where the collaboration was presented and the day STATS ChipPAC posted its official announcement. Note that being presented at an event is a separate fact from the details of the agreement or how finished the product is. What the announcement confirms is the agreement and a plan aiming for mass production. It is not the start of mass production of customer products or a record of adoption. A simple guide to CPO and advanced packaging

CPO (Co-Packaged Optics) is the idea of placing optical communication functions near electrical chips such as switch ASICs or AI accelerators, or on the same package substrate. Traditionally, the common setup placed the electrical chip and the optical transceiver on the board as separate parts. As the amount of data between chips grows, driving long electrical wires at high speed increases signal loss and power consumption. Placing the optical part near the chip may ease these wiring limits.

However, CPO is not a technology that is finished just by placing optical parts next to the chip. Electronic ICs that handle electrical signals, photonic ICs that handle optical signals, lasers, optical fibers, power supply, and cooling all have to be connected precisely in a tight space. If the optical alignment is off, communication performance suffers, and thermal expansion and manufacturing variation also become reliability challenges.

Silicon photonics is a technology that converts and transmits electrical and optical signals using silicon-based optical waveguides, modulators, photodetectors, and similar devices. It can take advantage of its compatibility with the silicon manufacturing base used for electronic circuits. On the other hand, connecting to light sources and optical fibers, and combining with electrical circuits, requires advanced packaging. A*STAR IME describes technologies for this kind of electronic-photonic heterogeneous integration, including TSV, interposers, fan-out wafer-level packaging, 3D stacking, and chip-to-wafer bonding. A*STAR IME technical explanation

A closer look at the terms

  • TSV: An electrical connection that passes vertically through silicon. When chips are stacked, it links the layers over a short path.
  • Interposer: An intermediate substrate that holds multiple chips and connects them with fine wiring. It serves as a base for placing electronic ICs and photonic ICs close together.
  • OSAT: A company that assembles, packages, and tests chips on behalf of semiconductor makers. It plays an important role in moving research prototypes into stable mass production.

Why it matters in the AI era

AI servers move huge amounts of data between accelerators, switches, memory, and servers. Raising computing power alone will not unlock full system performance if the communication part that carries the data cannot keep up. In data centers especially, it is important to raise bandwidth while keeping power consumption, heat, and wiring density down.

A*STAR IME explains that bringing optical interconnects into the rack and server layers of hyperscale data centers requires heterogeneous integration packages that connect and wire photonic ICs and electronic ICs. CPO is one promising direction for this challenge, but this announcement does not give measured values, such as how many times a specific product's bandwidth increased or by what percentage power consumption fell. Background on optical interconnects and heterogeneous integration

In Singapore, the government describes the National Semiconductor Translation and Innovation Centre (NSTIC) as a platform for silicon photonics research, prototyping, and small-scale manufacturing. A policy environment is taking shape to scale up R&D, including advanced packaging, and turn it into business. This collaboration can be seen as a move that links such research results with manufacturing. However, fitting with policy does not guarantee the agreement's commercial success or mass production. Limits on the way to mass production, and what to check next

In semiconductor mass production, there is a big gap between building a prototype that works in the lab and manufacturing large volumes reliably over a long time. With CPO, you need to manage optical-electrical alignment, bond strength, thermal management, assembly time, test methods, manufacturing variation, and ease of repair or replacement all at once. Even with high performance, low yield or high assembly cost would make wide adoption in data centers difficult.

The published materials do not show the contract amount or term, the patents or processes covered, the manufacturing site, specific product specs, customer names, the mass-production start date, yield, or reliability test results. Also, the low-power and high-performance goals on A*STAR IME's technical page describe the technology platform. They do not mean performance already achieved in STATS ChipPAC products under this deal.

Going forward, it will be important whether the following information is announced.

  1. When manufacturing actually started on a pilot line or mass-production line
  2. The configuration of supported photonic ICs, electrical ICs, lasers, and optical fibers
  3. Measured results for bandwidth, power consumption, thermal characteristics, yield, and reliability
  4. Customer adoption and volume shipments by data center companies or semiconductor makers
  5. Cost, serviceability, and replacement methods compared with conventional optical transceivers

In conclusion, this news is not an announcement that commercial mass production of CPO is complete. It is a concrete step, backed by an agreement, to move A*STAR IME's research results into STATS ChipPAC's manufacturing capabilities. As communication demand grows in AI data centers, the success of CPO will depend not only on the performance of optical devices but also on whether advanced packaging can be made reliably in large volumes. Going forward, we need to check whether the companies move from announcing that they "aim for mass production" to actual lines, yield, and customer adoption.

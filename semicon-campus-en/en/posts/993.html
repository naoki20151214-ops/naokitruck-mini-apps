What was announced

On September 14, 2026, SEMI announced that SEMI Europe has joined ReSiLient, a European research and development project. ReSiLient aims to diversify supply routes for silicon (Si) and silicon carbide (SiC), two materials used in semiconductors. It looks not only at minerals within Europe but also at byproducts and waste from industrial activity as possible raw materials. This date is the day SEMI announced its participation. It is not necessarily the start date of the project itself or the date the participation agreement was signed. SEMI announcement

According to the European Commission's CORDIS listing, the project runs for 48 months, from June 1, 2026 to May 31, 2030. The total cost is listed as €9,573,137.50, and the EU contribution as €7,998,854.77. The roughly €9.57 million is the total cost of the whole project, not "about €9.57 million in EU grants." Also, May 31, 2030 is the currently planned end date, not a completed result. CORDIS project information

Testing two raw-material routes at pilot scale

Under the announced plan, the project will test the production of metallurgical-grade silicon and high-purity SiC at pilot scale through two routes: one using mineral resources within Europe, and one reusing industrial byproducts and waste. Pilot scale is a stage where processes are tested under conditions closer to real use than small lab experiments. It does not mean stable mass production. Even if the equipment runs, commercialization cannot move forward unless the required purity, yield, production cost, and environmental impact are all met at the same time. SEMI announcement

The plan also includes STMicroelectronics Silicon Carbide AB and Infineon Technologies comparing the resulting materials with commercial raw materials. The key point is that this is an evaluation planned for the future. It is not an announcement that the two companies have already qualified the quality, adopted it in products, or started mass production. The materials available now contain no measured results showing yield, unit production cost, mass-production qualification, or completed customer adoption. SEMI announcement

How are Si and SiC different?

Silicon (Si) is the most widely used base material in today's semiconductor industry. bifa, one of the participating research institutes, lists uses such as logic semiconductors, memory, and solar cells. Logic semiconductors are chips that handle computing and control, and memory chips store data. Silicon's strengths are its well-developed technology for making high-quality crystals and its large-scale manufacturing base. bifa project description

Silicon carbide (SiC) is a compound semiconductor made of silicon and carbon. It handles high voltages and temperatures well and helps reduce losses when converting power. This makes it important in power electronics such as electric vehicles and renewable energy equipment. Power electronics is the technology of efficiently converting and controlling electricity into the needed voltage, current, and frequency, rather than simply passing it through. Si and SiC sometimes compete, but they are not the same material in terms of uses or manufacturing processes. bifa project description

Being able to recover it is not the same as being usable in semiconductors

Metallurgical-grade silicon is a basic stage of silicon obtained by reducing ore. It cannot become a raw material for semiconductors unless its impurities are greatly reduced. In WACKER's description of its existing process, metallurgical-grade silicon is turned into a compound called trichlorosilane, impurities are removed by distillation, and high-purity polysilicon is then deposited using the Siemens process. For semiconductor use, a cleaning step is added as well. WACKER technical explanation

The Siemens process is a method in which a gas containing purified silicon is decomposed on the surface of heated thin silicon rods, growing high-purity polycrystalline silicon. The WACKER process described here does not show that ReSiLient will use the same method. Still, it helps us understand that there are several purification and evaluation steps between "we recovered silicon from waste" and "it became material pure enough to reliably make fine-featured semiconductors." Why supply chain efforts matter

bifa lists securing high-quality quartz as a raw material, processing capacity within Europe, and the environmental impact of existing processes as challenges. In other words, simply finding candidate raw materials in mines or waste is not enough. A semiconductor material supply chain only exists once purification facilities, energy, quality control, and evaluation by user companies are all connected. In this sense, it is meaningful that ReSiLient builds resources, processing, and comparative evaluation by users into a single plan. bifa project description

SEMI Europe announced that it will lead WP8, which covers social acceptance, dissemination of results, communication, and standardization. WP stands for "work package," a unit of work that divides a large research plan by role. Standardization is important for making material quality and evaluation methods easier to compare among stakeholders. However, starting standardization activities does not by itself guarantee material performance. SEMI announcement

Limits and what to watch next

What has been shown so far is a research and development plan. How much Europe's import dependence will fall, whether the materials will be cheaper than existing ones, and how much environmental impact will improve are not yet proven results. Future goals and outlooks from participating organizations need to be read separately from production and quality data that third parties can verify. Supply chain diversification trends matter for Japanese materials companies too, but no information has been found showing a specific impact on their orders or competitiveness.

Going forward, checking the following points will make it easier to judge the plan's progress.

  • Can Si and SiC of the target purity be made reproducibly from local minerals and secondary resources?
  • Compared with commercial raw materials, how close do electrical properties and impurity levels get?
  • Can the energy and total cost needed for purification be kept down, not just the recovery rate?
  • Will the results of environmental, circular economy, and techno-economic assessments be published as numbers?
  • After the pilot demonstration, can the project move on to mass-production equipment, customer qualification, and ongoing procurement?

The core of this news is that Europe is not only searching for new raw-material candidates for Si and SiC, but has also built quality comparisons by the companies that will actually use them into the plan. On the other hand, we can only judge that the supply chain has been strengthened after concrete results are in on quality, economics, environmental performance, and mass-production readiness.

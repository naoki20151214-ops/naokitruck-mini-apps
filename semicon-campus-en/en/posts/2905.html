Last time, we saw that cleaning makes up about one fifth of all process steps. So what exactly is the "contamination" we want to remove?

In fact, contamination is broadly divided into 4 types. That is because each type causes harm in a different way and is removed in a different way.

Yellow highlights = key points that appear on this course's tests

What you will learn in this article

  • The 4 types of contamination (particles, organics, metals, and native oxide)
  • What kind of harm each one does
  • How each one is removed (details in the next lessons)

① Particles

These are dust in the air and tiny fragments that come from equipment. As we saw last time, they can connect or block the thin lines of a circuit and cause shorts or open circuits. Lecture materials from KTH Royal Institute of Technology in Sweden say that "the size of a particle that causes a fatal defect is one half of the minimum line width." The Northeastern University article from last time said "about one quarter." Depending on the source, the guideline for the size of a particle that causes a defect is about one quarter to one half of the line width.

Evidence

KTH (Royal Institute of Technology, Sweden) lecture "Wafer Clean and Wet Processing" (Mikael Östling, 2012). The sources differ: KTH's materials say one half of the minimum line width, while Northeastern University's article says about one quarter.

Let's think in numbers

In a table in KTH's materials, the minimum line width shrinks from 65 nanometers (2007) to 45 nanometers (2010) to 32 nanometers (2013).

Using the one-half guideline, 65 ÷ 2 = 32.5 nanometers and 32 ÷ 2 = 16 nanometers. The thinner the lines get, the smaller the particles you have to worry about.

Quick quiz: Why do particles cause "shorts or open circuits"?

Answer: Because they connect the gaps between the thin lines of a circuit or block the lines.

② Organics

These include oil contamination, organic vapors in the air, substances given off by storage containers, and residue of photoresist (the material used to form circuit shapes with light). KTH's materials list organic vapors in the air, storage containers, and photoresist residue as the main sources of organic contamination.

Why are organics a problem?

If an organic film remains, the next film will not stick to it well. Also, in later high-temperature steps, the contamination may get driven into the wafer. KTH's materials also warn that "high-temperature steps push contamination into the silicon and thin films."

The basic way to remove them is to use strong oxidizing power to turn organics into carbon dioxide and water. Examples include a mixture of sulfuric acid and hydrogen peroxide (piranha solution) and methods that use ozone.

Quick quiz: What might happen if you move on to a high-temperature step with organics still on the wafer?

Answer: The contamination may get into the wafer or the thin films.

③ Metals

These include iron, copper, nickel, chromium, and alkali metals (such as sodium). KTH's materials explain that metal contamination comes from chemicals and from steps such as ion implantation and dry etching. As an example of the order of magnitude of metal contamination, they give the figure of about 10 to the 10th power (100 times 100 million) atoms per square centimeter.

Let's think in numbers

The area of a 300-millimeter-diameter wafer, with a radius of 15 centimeters, is 3.14 × 15 × 15 ≈ 707 square centimeters.

At 10 to the 10th power per square centimeter, the whole wafer has 10 to the 10th power × 707 ≈ 7 × 10 to the 12th power (about 7 trillion) atoms. Even an amount you cannot see is a very large number.

Why is even a tiny amount of metal a problem?

Metal atoms change how electricity flows in silicon. This causes transistor characteristics to drift away from the design. The basic way to remove them is to dissolve metals with an acidic solution and keep them from sticking again.

Quick quiz: Mainly from which steps and chemicals does metal contamination come?

Answer: From chemicals and from steps such as ion implantation and dry etching.

④ Native oxide

When silicon touches air or water, a very thin oxide film quickly forms on its surface. This is called native oxide. KTH's materials list the following 4 items as problems caused by native oxide.

  • Electrical contact resistance increases
  • Deposition that grows films selectively (CVD and epitaxy) becomes difficult
  • It becomes a source of metal contamination
  • Compounds of metal and silicon (silicides) become harder to form
Let's think in numbers

Native oxide is removed with hydrofluoric acid (HF) diluted with water. The dilution given in KTH's materials is HF : water = 1:50.

The total is 1 + 50 = 51, so the share of HF is 1 ÷ 51 ≈ about 2%. It is mostly water, a very dilute solution.

Why is it still a problem after you remove it?

Because native oxide forms again right away when it touches air or water. So after removing it, it is important to move on to the next step as quickly as possible.

Quick quiz: What is diluted to make the solution used to remove native oxide?

Answer: Hydrofluoric acid (HF), diluted with water.

Key points of this lesson

There are 4 types of contamination: particles, organics, metals, and native oxide. Each causes harm differently and is removed differently. That is why cleaning uses different solutions and methods for each type.

ContaminationMain harmHow it is removed (overview)
ParticlesShorts, open circuitsAlkaline solutions, ultrasonics, etc.
OrganicsFilms won't stick; contamination driven inStrong oxidation (sulfuric acid + hydrogen peroxide, ozone, etc.)
MetalsElectrical characteristics driftAcidic solution (hydrochloric acid + hydrogen peroxide)
Native oxidePoor contact; source of metalsDilute HF

Word review

  • Particle: A very tiny speck of debris on a wafer.
  • Photoresist: A light-sensitive material used when forming circuit shapes with light.
  • Native oxide: A very thin oxide film that forms on the surface when it touches air or water.
  • Hydrofluoric acid (HF): A chemical used in diluted form to remove native oxide.

Common misconceptions

Q. Does contamination just mean debris?

No. Besides particles, invisible organics, metals, and thin oxide films are also treated as contamination.

Q. Once it's clean, will it stay that way?

No. Some things, like native oxide, form again when exposed to air.

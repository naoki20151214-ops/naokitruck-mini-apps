On September 24, 2026, Advanced Chip and Circuit Materials (ACCM) announced that it will expand U.S. production capacity for advanced dielectric materials for AI hardware. The materials are used in AI accelerator boards, IC package substrates, high-speed network equipment, and more. This news is not about producing more compute chips themselves. It is best understood as a plan to strengthen the supply chain for the materials behind the boards and packages that hold those chips. Republished announcement

What was announced

ACCM's announcement covers four areas: skew-mitigation materials for ultra-high-speed signals, low- and negative-thermal-expansion laminates for large accelerator boards, IC substrates and package cores, and build-up materials. The announcement says the company plans to bring build-up and prepreg capacity of 87 million square feet per year online in November 2026, and laminate capacity of 20 million square feet per year in January 2027. Further expansions are also planned for Q3 and Q4 2027.

The important point is that these are operating plans presented by ACCM, not production results completed as of September 24, 2026. U.S. production, a typical three-week lead time, and a raw material supply chain independent of the traditional copper-clad laminate chain are also, for now, the company's own descriptions. Completion of operations, customer qualification for mass production, and the easing of supply constraints have not been confirmed by independent sources.

"Dielectric loss" and "skew": key issues for high-speed signals

The keys to understanding board materials are dielectric loss and skew. Dielectric loss is when part of the electrical energy turns into heat and other forms as a signal passes through a board's insulating material, weakening the signal. In AI servers, chip-to-chip traffic grows and signal speeds rise, so materials with high loss make it harder to deliver signals accurately over distance.

Skew is when multiple signals that should arrive at the same time arrive with slight time differences. ACCM describes SF1600 as an ultra-low-loss material without woven glass reinforcement, aimed at reducing skew caused by the glass weave pattern in 224G-class switches and backplanes. The company's official website lists 112G/224Gbps-class SerDes and PCIe 6/7+ among the applications, but this is ACCM's own product and technology description. It does not mean independent proof of performance in customer systems or a record of adoption. ACCM official website

Materials that reduce thermal expansion and warpage

Large AI accelerator packages combine different materials such as silicon, copper, resin, and organic substrates. If their coefficients of thermal expansion (CTE) differ, each temperature change causes a difference in how much the materials expand and contract, which can lead to internal stress and package warpage. Heavy warpage can affect the reliability of the connections and the manufacturing accuracy of the board.

ACCM's product pages describe HM001 as a material with near-zero CTE in the XY direction, HM50 as a material with negative XY CTE and a high elastic modulus, and SMC as a silicon-matched core. Negative CTE means the material shrinks in one direction when the temperature rises. By combining it to cancel out the expansion of other materials, designers can aim to reduce dimensional change and warpage in the whole structure. However, the real effect also depends on the layer structure, processing conditions, temperature cycles, connection materials, and more. Material property values on a product page alone cannot tell us about mass-production yield or long-term reliability. ACCM product information

What are build-up materials used for?

Build-up materials are a group of materials used to form fine wiring while stacking insulating layers on a substrate. Because they make it easy to add wiring layers, they are important for package substrates that must route many signals and power lines through a limited area. ACCM lists BU++ as an ultra-low-loss build-up material and says it supports both vacuum lamination and standard hot-press processes.

AI substrates must meet several needs at once: not only high-speed signal quality, but also wiring density, heat, mechanical stability, and compatibility with processing equipment. So increased output from materials makers may ease bottlenecks that sit outside chipmakers' production capacity. On the other hand, this announcement alone does not let us conclude that AI server shipments will rise, that chip shortages will end, or that a specific customer has adopted the materials.

What this news means, and its limits

The meaning of this news is that competition over AI chip performance is spreading beyond compute chips to substrate and package materials. Higher speeds make signal loss and skew a problem, and larger sizes make differences in thermal expansion and warpage a problem. On top of that, whether materials can be sourced reliably in the regions where they are needed also affects mass-production plans. ACCM's U.S. capacity expansion can be seen as a supply-side response to these challenges.

However, figures such as 87 million square feet per year and 20 million square feet per year are planned capacities from the announcement. Whether the equipment came online on schedule, whether it can mass-produce at stable quality, whether it has passed customer qualification, and whether it beats existing materials on cost or performance all need to be checked separately. Descriptions of applications and properties on ACCM's official website should also be read as the company's own information. ACCM company information

What to watch next

  • Whether the build-up and prepreg capacity planned for November 2026 actually came online.
  • Whether the 20 million square feet per year of laminate capacity planned for January 2027 was completed and entered stable production.
  • Whether the additional expansions in Q3 and Q4 2027 go ahead.
  • Whether customer names, mass-production adoption, completed qualification, or third-party tests are announced.
  • Whether, beyond supply volume, high-speed signal loss, skew, warpage, and long-term reliability are confirmed in real products.

Going forward, it is important to track announcements of completed equipment separately from actual mass production and customer adoption. This announcement shows the role materials play in the semiconductor supply chain in the AI era, but whether the plans lead to easing supply constraints or better product performance will be judged by results after operations begin.

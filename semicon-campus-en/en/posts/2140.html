Why Can Tiny, Invisible Particles Ruin Semiconductors? Particle Defects and Equipment Maintenance

What happens if a tiny piece of debris gets into the pattern while fine wiring patterns are being made on a wafer?
Depending on where the processing is happening, a line could be cut off, or lines that should stay separate could become connected.
However, finding a particle does not by itself mean the circuit is defective. You trace where it was found, which process steps it went through, and what later inspections revealed.

In a semiconductor fab, a "particle" is a tiny, particle-like foreign object on a wafer, equipment, or materials. Detecting something that looks like a particle in surface inspection and confirming an electrical defect in a finished circuit are separate stages.

What you will learn in this article

  • How particles can disturb the shape of a circuit and trigger electrical defects
  • How to read inspection maps and electrical test maps differently
  • The roles of cleaning, equipment maintenance, and condition control, as seen in published research

Tiny particles can disturb the shape of a circuit

Tiny particles are a problem not only because the particle itself pierces a finished chip. On a wafer, films are stacked, patterns are transferred, unwanted parts are removed, and other films and wiring are built. If particles or residue are present partway through, they can get in the way of forming the intended shape, depending on the process.

For example, when making a row of narrow trenches, if a foreign particle becomes a local obstacle, a trench could be cut off, or material could remain where things should be separate. This is an analogy to help picture the mechanism. Actual results depend on the particle's material, size, and location, the process, the pattern, and later processing.

Effects confirmed later in electrical testing include an "open," where wiring that should be connected is broken, and a "short" (or "bridge"), where wiring that should be separate becomes connected. However, detecting a particle alone does not mean these electrical defects have occurred. Besides particles, residue, scratches, and patterning misalignment are also defect candidates. Applied Materials, in its explanation of defect control, also lists particles, residue, scratches, bridges, and shorts as separate examples of defects.

Why control gets harder with scaling

As circuit spacing and structures get finer, even foreign particles that once had little effect may land on important locations. On the other hand, there is no uniform line such as "in an X nm process, a Y nm particle is always fatal." That's because the name of a process generation is not the same as the conditions under which an individual particle causes a defect.

Particle sources are not just air and people

Cleanroom cleanliness control is important, but sometimes you can't find the cause by looking only at the floor or the air. There are multiple particle candidates, such as reaction byproducts on the inner walls of manufacturing equipment, parts and consumables, materials, chemicals, and wafer transfer. Until they are actually investigated, all of them are "candidates," not causes.

For example, in etching, while the film on the wafer surface is being processed, reaction byproducts also form inside the equipment. Depending on conditions, deposits or particles from parts could be carried onto the wafer. So even if particles are found, you should not immediately blame an operator or a specific tool.

To narrow down the cause, you check records for things like "after which process step did it increase," "is it concentrated in the same chamber or material lot," and "did it change before and after cleaning or part replacement." Cleanroom standards that classify airborne particle concentration also do not directly determine the chemical makeup of particles or electrical defects on the wafer.

Inspection is divided into a stage for finding and a stage for identifying

One type of wafer inspection tool shines light on the wafer, captures light scattered by particles and defects, and records their position coordinates. Inspection methods may differ between mirror-polished wafers without patterns and wafers with circuit patterns. Hitachi High-Tech's explanation also introduces a flow in which, after detection, a defect review SEM is used for observation as needed.

What you get here first is "where a signal was found, and what its features were." Not every point that responds in optical inspection is a particle. To tell apart other candidates such as scratches and crystal defects, magnified image observation and material analysis may be combined.

Particle maps and electrical test maps show different information

A particle or defect map shows the coordinates and distribution of foreign particles and shape abnormalities detected in surface inspection. An electrical test map arranges electrical pass/fail results or measurements for each chip or test unit. They may be lined up using the same wafer coordinates, but they measure different things.

If both maps show abnormalities in similar places, that is a clue for investigating the cause. However, a correlation in location alone does not prove cause and effect. Possible causes are confirmed by combining particle identification, process history, tool differences, other inspection results, and reproduction checks as needed.

In published research, particle distribution helped narrow down where to check the equipment

A 2018 paper by Park, Kim, and Baek also focused on location bias, which you can't see just by counting particles. In the particle maps by process step described in the paper, areas of dense particles were seen in the etching step and the strip & clean step. The paper reports that when engineers examined the equipment using that distribution and the equipment structure as clues, they confirmed particles coming from an O-ring that was not properly secured in the chamber. This is an investigation case from a specific site.

In a separate analysis in the same paper, five months of data were collected from an actual etching process to evaluate the proposed monitoring method. The authors made 600 particle maps from 600 wafers and tested a metric that reflects not just particle counts but also how densely they cluster by location. The paper does not say the O-ring problem was identified from this 600-wafer evaluation data. The O-ring discovery is described as an equipment investigation guided by the per-process maps, and the 600 wafers are separate data for evaluating the method.

What this case shows is that looking at distribution in addition to particle counts can sometimes give clues about where on the equipment to check. In general failure analysis, detection locations, process history, differences in equipment and materials, and before-and-after maintenance are compared, and possible causes are confirmed one by one. The O-ring case here and this general approach to investigation should be considered separately. Neither proves an electrical defect in an individual chip from particle detection alone.

A separate 2022 study examined particles at the wafer edge in a specific oxide etching tool and experimented with electrostatic chuck conditions, plasma discharge procedures, and more. This is an example of particle countermeasures going beyond cleaning to include reviewing equipment parts and process conditions. It does not mean the conditions in the paper can be applied directly to other equipment.

Not just cleaning: reduce the sources and verify after maintenance

Wafer cleaning is a process to remove particles from the surface. Methods such as brushes and chemicals are chosen with care not only for the target particles but also to avoid damaging fine structures and materials on the wafer. Cleaning equipment makers publish examples of technologies such as particle removal and wafer edge cleaning.

However, cleaning the wafer and removing the reason particles keep coming from the equipment are separate things. If there are equipment-related candidates, deposits, parts, seals, and consumables inside are inspected under set safety procedures, and any needed cleaning or replacement is considered. Material receiving, storage, transfer methods, and chemical and cleaning conditions are also checked according to the process in question.

After maintenance, the decision to restart production is not based only on the fact that the work is finished. Particle increases and equipment and process conditions are checked using the confirmation methods set by the fab. What is measured and what criteria are used to judge recovery differ by equipment, process, and fab procedures.

Tracing the causes of particles connects to equipment maintenance and production engineering

Maintenance technicians compare records of inspection, cleaning, and part replacement with equipment condition, and support isolating abnormalities and confirming recovery using set procedures. Production engineering and process staff check which process conditions or materials increase defects, and inspection and quality staff compare surface images, maps, lot history, and electrical test results. How these duties are divided differs by company and fab.

What they have in common is capturing invisible abnormalities through records and measurements, forming hypotheses, and checking the evidence. Experience in inspection, part replacement, process improvement, and defect investigation in other manufacturing industries can help, but semiconductor-specific cleanliness, materials, vacuum, microfabrication, and safety procedures need to be learned separately.

Common misconceptions

Q. If one particle is found, is that chip defective?

No. Detection is the stage of finding a candidate surface abnormality. Location, process, the condition after later steps, and electrical test results are checked separately.

Q. If the patterns on the particle map and the electrical test map are the same, is the cause the particles?

You can't say for sure yet. A correlation in location is a clue for investigation, but candidates need to be verified with other process information, additional observation, and so on.

Q. Are no particles generated in a cleanroom?

No. Cleanliness control is important, but multiple candidates such as equipment, materials, and wafer transfer are managed. Air cleanliness classification alone cannot determine foreign particles on a wafer or whether a circuit passes or fails.

Summary: From detecting particles to confirming the cause

Particles can disturb circuit formation, depending on location and process.
A particle map is a clue for investigation, separate from proof of an electrical defect or its cause.
Inspection, cleaning, equipment maintenance, and process control are linked through records and checks to prevent recurrence.

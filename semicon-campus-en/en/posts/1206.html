Why is a turbomolecular pump paired with a roughing pump? How high vacuum is created

The vacuum in semiconductor equipment is not created by a single pump.
The TMP handles the high-vacuum side, while another pump handles roughing at startup and supports the backing pressure on the outlet side. They split the roles.
The reason is that the TMP is a pump that moves molecules toward the exhaust side in molecular flow.

The question in this article is "why is a turbomolecular pump (TMP) paired with a roughing pump or backing pump?" A TMP is not a part inside a finished chip. It is a device attached to the exhaust system of the chamber where wafers are processed. First we check where it sits in the vacuum system, then move on to how it moves molecules, why a two-stage setup is needed, and what to look at in maintenance.

A TMP is not "a fan that pushes air"

The word "pump" tends to make us picture a fan whose blades push a continuous flow of air. But what a TMP mainly handles is gas molecules flying apart from one another. As the vacuum gets higher, molecules collide with each other less often, and the mean free path (the distance a molecule travels before its next collision) can become longer than the typical size of the piping or chamber. In this state, collisions with walls and blades dominate over collisions between molecules.

This range is called molecular flow. Whether flow is molecular is not decided by pressure alone. Even at the same pressure, how molecules move changes if the pipe diameter, chamber size, or flow path shape is different. Deciding with a single number, like "below X Pa it is always molecular flow," overlooks the equipment dimensions.

In a TMP, molecules collide with the spinning rotor blades. The blades pass the rotor's momentum to the molecules and bias their direction so they are more likely to move toward the exhaust side than back toward the inlet side. The difference from a fan is that it uses molecular collisions and momentum transfer, not compression of a continuous airflow.

Molecules are moved through many rotor and stator stages

Inside a TMP, spinning rotor blades and stationary stator blades alternate. The rotor gives momentum to the molecules, and the stator points them in a direction that makes it easier to reach the next rotor stage. Rather than changing all the pressure at once in one stage, it stacks the transport of many stages to send molecules from the high-vacuum side to the exhaust side.

What each stage creates is a probability that molecules move toward the exhaust side. When this adds up over many stages, a pressure ratio forms between the inlet side and the exhaust side. So when reading pumping speed or compression ratio in a catalog, check which gas, which backing pressure, and which measurement conditions the value is for. Reading the pump as a high-volume blower leads to confusing these conditions.

Why can't a TMP alone pump down from atmospheric pressure?

A TMP takes in molecules from the high-vacuum side and moves them to the exhaust side. But if pressure close to atmospheric is applied to the TMP's outlet side, the conditions that let the rotor blades keep moving molecules to the exhaust side break down. The turbo stages of a TMP are not set up to handle everything from atmospheric pressure to high vacuum in one continuous unit.

So another pump is connected to the TMP's outlet. This is the pump called a backing pump, forepump, or auxiliary pump. The backing pump keeps the TMP's exhaust port side at low pressure and supports the backing pressure conditions that let the TMP handle the high-vacuum side. When starting the equipment from atmospheric pressure, roughing is also needed to remove the large amount of gas first.

A roughing pump and a backing pump do not always mean separate machines. The same dry pump may do roughing at startup and also maintain backing pressure on the outlet side while the TMP is running. The key is not the name but which role it plays in which pressure range.

Looking at the foreline and piping between the pumps

The piping from the TMP's exhaust port to the backing pump is sometimes called the foreline. Even if the pumping speed at the pump inlet is high, molecules have trouble getting through if there is thin, long piping, many bends, or narrow valves between the chamber and the TMP. The concept that describes this ease of flow is conductance.

Effective pumping speed is not decided only by the TMP's nameplate or catalog value. Pipe shape, valve opening, filters and traps, deposits in the foreline, and the pressure on the backing side are all linked. When thinking about pumping performance as seen from the chamber, evaluate it as an exhaust system including the resistance along the way, not as a number for the pump alone.

Gas type and backing pressure change how performance looks

A TMP's compression ratio is not the same for every gas. Light molecules such as hydrogen and helium do not necessarily get the same result from momentum transfer as heavy molecules. Also, high backing pressure changes the TMP's pumping conditions. So you cannot say "a TMP has the same compression ratio for any gas" or "if the TMP is oil-free, the whole vacuum system is always oil-free."

When reading a performance table, read the gas type, inlet pressure, backing pressure, piping configuration, and measurement method together. How light gases are handled and the conditions on the exhaust side should be checked in each model's documents and the actual equipment setup. Not creating model-specific limits from general information is the safe boundary.

In semiconductor equipment, the whole exhaust system is maintained

The exhaust system of semiconductor equipment is one system made up of the chamber, valves, piping, TMP, foreline, backing pump, and exhaust gas treatment equipment as needed. During a process, gas flow, pressure, plasma, reaction byproducts, deposits, temperature, and purge conditions change. The condition of one TMP alone sometimes cannot explain changes in pump-down time or ultimate pressure.

For example, when pump-down becomes slow, before treating only the TMP itself as a replacement candidate, check backing pressure, clogging or deposits in the foreline, valve opening and closing, piping condition, and leaks or byproducts on the chamber side along the same chain of cause and effect. Cooling and purge status are also things to check according to the model's documents.

The order to check first in maintenance

When an anomaly appears on site, first line up which process, which gas, which pressure range, and which time period the symptom appeared in. Next, follow the direction of exhaust and look at the TMP's rotation, cooling, and purge status, the foreline backing pressure, valve opening, and the condition of piping and traps. Finally, separate out the relationship with leaks, deposits, and process byproducts on the chamber side.

What matters here is not deciding thresholds for maximum backing pressure or rotation speed from general articles. These depend on the model, gas, operating mode, cooling, purge, and equipment configuration. In real work, give priority to the pump maker's and equipment maker's manuals, safety rules, and the equipment's official logs.

Common misconceptions

Q. Is a TMP an ultra-high-speed fan?

A. No. A TMP is a pump that works in molecular flow: rotor blades give momentum to gas molecules, and a multistage structure with stators in between builds up transport toward the exhaust side.

Q. Is the backing pump always a separate pump from the TMP?

A. No. In some setups, the same pump handles both roughing and maintaining backing pressure at the TMP outlet. Check by its role at startup and during operation rather than by its name.

Q. Is it enough to look only at the TMP's pumping speed?

A. No. Judge the effective performance as seen from the chamber, including gas type, backing pressure, piping conductance, valves, and the foreline.

Summary

In molecular flow, a TMP's rotor blades give momentum to molecules, and a multistage structure with stators in between moves them toward the exhaust side.
A downstream backing pump handles roughing from atmospheric pressure and maintaining backing pressure at the TMP outlet, which is why a two-stage setup is needed.
On site, look not at the TMP alone but at the chamber, piping, foreline, cooling, purge, and byproducts as one exhaust system.

On September 21, 2026, Teradyne announced Iris 100, a production-ready optical test platform for microLED devices. In connection with the announcement, it was exhibited at ECOC in Málaga, Spain, from September 21 to 23. The important point is that Iris 100 is not a new invention of microLED itself, but test equipment for measuring extremely small light-emitting elements on a mass-production line.Teradyne official announcement

What was announced

Iris 100 is a tool that measures the individual light-emitting elements in a microLED array. According to Teradyne, it uses a spectrometer and a high-resolution camera to examine the array's spectral response, the luminance of each pixel, and the uniformity of light emission. It is also designed to integrate with Teradyne's UltraFLEXplus semiconductor tester, combining optical tests that look at the light with electrical tests that check whether the device works correctly. It is said to support both wafer-level inspection and final test after assembly.Teradyne official announcement

Teradyne also says that parallel testing and image processing algorithms let it evaluate arrays containing millions of microLEDs at high throughput. Another aim is to detect dark pixels, pixels stuck on, and cluster defects (groups of nearby defects) early in the manufacturing process. However, figures such as actual measurement time, number of units processed, and measurement accuracy cannot be confirmed from this official announcement.

Why mass-production testing of microLEDs is hard

A microLED is an optical device made of many very small LEDs arranged side by side, each controlled individually. The elements are generally said to be under 100 micrometers in size, and in displays, one or several elements act as a pixel or subpixel. Shrinking them makes high-resolution displays possible, but the more elements there are, the harder testing becomes.Technical explanation from Instrument Systems

You cannot judge product quality just by whether the whole array lights up. For each element, you need to check brightness, color, peak wavelength, uniformity with its neighbors, and whether there are defects. Instrument Systems explains that mass production requires measuring many elements in parallel and performing fast, accurate optical inspection pixel by pixel while staying in sync with the production flow. Traceability, which links calibration values to national standards and the like, is also important.Technical explanation from Instrument Systems

What are spectrometers and luminance measurement?

A "spectrometer" is a device that splits light by wavelength and measures how much of each color component it contains. Even pixels that look the same color to the human eye may differ in display quality or in characteristics as communication components if their wavelength distributions differ. "Luminance" is a quantity related to perceived brightness. Iris 100 is said to check pixel-level luminance and spectra together.

Parallel testing and calibration traceability

"Parallel testing" is the idea of measuring many elements at the same time instead of one by one in order. If the number of microLEDs reaches the millions, sequential measurement alone takes far too long. On the other hand, simultaneous measurement raises challenges in camera resolution, optical layout, and data processing speed.

"Calibration traceability" means being able to trace which standard a measured value is based on. Teradyne says its NIST-traceable calibration workflow lets absolute measurements be compared across lots, wafers, and manufacturing sites. This may help keep the standard for "bright" and "dark" from drifting between factories and help with process comparisons. However, the actual calibration uncertainty and the results of cross-site comparisons have not been published.Teradyne official announcement

The connection to AI data centers

Teradyne lists not only AR microdisplays but also optical data interconnects for AI data centers as uses for Iris 100. The idea is that AI processing moves huge amounts of data between computing devices, so the performance and manufacturing quality of optical connection parts become important. In designs that use microLED emitters as light sources, testing to keep the light output and wavelength of each element consistent can become a manufacturing challenge.

However, what we can confirm is only that Teradyne describes this use as a target market and has announced matching test equipment. There is no independent data showing adoption at specific AI data centers, customer names, commercial shipment volumes, or the scale of replacing copper wiring with optical connections. The phrase "supporting AI optical connections" should not be taken to mean that large-scale deployment has already been completed.

Points to watch

Going forward, the first focus is whether examples of deployment on real mass-production lines and processing capacity by pixel count are shown. Second, how accurately color, luminance, and wavelength can be measured, and whether the measurements are repeatable, needs to be confirmed. Third, it is important whether customers or third parties release data on how much Iris 100 has improved early defect detection and yield.

This announcement is less an invention that directly boosts microLED performance and more a proposal for manufacturing infrastructure that links individual emitter testing, parallelization, integration with electrical testing, and calibration management to the mass-production process. If these work on real lines, they may help support mass production of AR displays and optical interconnects. However, to judge that potential as commercial results, we need to wait for future deployment records and independent performance data.

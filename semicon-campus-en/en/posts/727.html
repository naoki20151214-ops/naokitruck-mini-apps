The short answer first ABF is not part of the semiconductor chip itself. It is a thin film that insulates the layers of copper wiring from each other in the package substrate the chip sits on.

When you look into high-performance semiconductors for AI, you may come across the story that "Ajinomoto makes a semiconductor material." That material is Ajinomoto Build-up Film®, or ABF for short. In this article, we explain where ABF is, what it does, and why it is hard to replace, for people learning about semiconductors for the first time.

WhereIt is used between the layers of the package substrate, not inside the chip.
RoleIt electrically separates copper wires and serves as a base for making fine connections.
ChallengeIt must meet heat resistance, laser processability, and compatibility with copper wiring all at once.

1. First, where in a semiconductor is ABF?

Explanations of semiconductors often lump "chips" and "substrates" together. If you think of them separately, ABF's role becomes easier to see.

Semiconductor chipA small circuit that computes and controls
Package substrateA multilayer base that connects the chip to the outside
External boardPasses signals to the circuits of the whole device

ABF mainly works in the package substrate in the middle. Many layers of copper wiring are built inside the substrate, but simply stacking wiring would let electricity flow into layers where it should not go. So an interlayer insulating material that does not conduct electricity easily is placed between the wiring layers. ABF is the film used for this.

Key point for remembering the boundary: ABF is not the silicon material used to make transistors. It is a material that supports the package-side wiring that carries signals between the chip and outside circuits.

2. What does it do? Think of it as "walls between wires"

As an everyday comparison, ABF plays a role similar to the walls in an apartment building. Without walls, sounds and movement from the next room would mix in. In the same way, without an insulating layer between wires, the paths for electricity cannot be kept apart.

In a real substrate, ABF covers a layer of copper wiring, and the next wiring layer is built on top. By alternating copper, which conducts electricity, with ABF, which does not conduct easily, many connections can be placed in a limited area.

Thin ABF is described as about 10 micrometers per layer. If a human hair is about 80 micrometers thick, you can picture work that handles layers thinner than a hair over and over.

3. Why is it needed in high-performance semiconductors?

As CPU and GPU performance rises, the signal and power connections going in and out of the chip also become more complex. Instead of connecting the chip's terminals straight to the external board, fine wiring must be routed through multiple layers inside the package substrate.

In a build-up substrate that uses ABF, the insulating layer is processed to make tiny connection points, which are linked to the next wiring layer with copper plating and similar methods. In this way, the substrate converts step by step between the fine terminals on the chip side and the larger terminals on the device side.

Picture it this way: ABF is not a "material that computes." It is a base for adding more wiring between the computing chip and outside circuits without crosstalk.

4. How is ABF used? Laminating a film, then making fine circuits

With the traditional method of coating a liquid material, the evenness of the coating and the drying time are challenges. With film-type ABF, a thin, even insulating layer can be made through a process centered on laminating the film onto the substrate.

After that, the needed connection points are formed by laser processing, and copper is added to the surface to make circuits. The important point here is that ABF does not come with holes already in it. Fine connections are made in a later step, according to the processing conditions.

Quality depends not only on the material but also on how it works with the laser conditions and the plating process. In other words, the value is not just in delivering the film. It includes the film working reliably in the substrate maker's process.

5. Why can't other companies easily copy it?

The difficulty of ABF is that improving just one property is not enough. For high-performance semiconductors, it must meet at least the following conditions at the same time.

Withstands heatIts properties hold up against heat during operation and heat in manufacturing processes.
Can be laser-processedFine connection points can be made easily in the intended position and shape.
Works with copper wiringIt connects reliably in circuit-forming processes such as plating.

On top of that, it must combine thinness, insulation, durability, and processability, and be fitted to each customer's substrate process. The Ajinomoto Group has built up formulations that combine resins, curing agents, and inorganic fillers, along with a system for testing actual processing conditions with customers.

According to the official explanation, ABF has kept evolving to keep up with denser circuits ever since it was adopted by a major semiconductor maker in 1999. It is not just the material's name. A long history of "recipes" covering formulation, film production, mass production, and process support makes it hard to copy.

6. How should we understand the link between "Ajinomoto" and semiconductors?

When explaining ABF, thinking that "semiconductors are made from Ajinomoto seasoning" leads to misunderstanding. More precisely, research on resins, formulation, and materials that the Ajinomoto Group built up through food and amino acids led to the development of electronic materials.

ABF is not made by mixing food, and it is not a seasoning by-product turned directly into film. The approach of combining different materials evenly and reproducing the desired properties is also used in formulating electronic materials.

That is why the "recipe" comparison from cooking is useful. However, the real thing is an industrial product that handles polymer materials, inorganic fillers, and more. Behind the comparison are scientific processes that measure heat resistance, processability, and compatibility with copper wiring.

7. Common misunderstandings (FAQ)

Is ABF inside the transistors of CPUs and GPUs?

No. Its main role is interlayer insulation in the package substrate the chip sits on. Both its location and its role differ from the silicon material used to make transistors.

Is ABF needed in every semiconductor?

Not necessarily. ABF is a material used in package substrates for high-performance semiconductors and similar uses. Materials vary by application and package type.

Is the "global share of over 95%" a figure for all semiconductor materials?

No. It refers to the scope of insulating films for package substrates of high-performance semiconductors. It is not a share figure for all semiconductors or all materials.

Does ABF come with holes for wiring already made?

No. After the film is built into the substrate, the needed connection points and wiring are formed by processing such as laser drilling and by copper plating.

Summary: ABF is a film that supports "invisible wiring"

ABF is not a material that does computing inside the semiconductor chip. It is a film in the package substrate the chip sits on, used to insulate layers of copper wiring and build up fine connections.

As performance rises, it must meet heat resistance, laser processability, and compatibility with copper wiring all at once. Years of formulation, processing, and customer testing since 1999 have made ABF a technology that is "hard to copy from the material name alone."

Key point of this article: It is not that "Ajinomoto makes semiconductors." It is more accurate to understand that the company makes insulating film for semiconductor package substrates, starting from materials technology built up in the food and amino acid fields.

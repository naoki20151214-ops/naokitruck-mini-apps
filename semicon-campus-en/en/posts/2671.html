Have you ever left your phone sitting unused, only to notice later that the battery has dropped a little?

Even when the screen is off, the chips inside your phone keep using a tiny bit of electricity. One reason is "leakage current." This is electricity that keeps flowing a little even after a switch is turned off. In this article, we use the comparison of a "sluice gate" to explain why leakage current becomes a problem as chips get smaller.

The sluice gate comparison fits only in these ideas: "there is a set height where flow begins" and "water leaks through even a small gap." Water does not actually flow inside a real transistor.

Power drains even when you are not using it

Semiconductors contain huge numbers of tiny switches called "transistors." Transistors let electricity flow or stop it to create signals of 0 and 1. But sometimes a little electricity keeps flowing even though the switch should be off. According to EE Times Japan, this current while the switch is off is called off-current, or subthreshold leakage current, and when it increases, standby power consumption increases. Leakage current is electricity that keeps flowing a little even after a switch is turned off.

In the past, just making things smaller was enough

Semiconductors have improved in performance as transistors got smaller. From the 1970s to the 1990s, following a rule called "Dennard scaling," simply making things smaller made circuits faster with almost no increase in power consumption. This rule was published in 1974 by Dennard and colleagues at IBM. An article covering a talk by NVIDIA's Bill Dally explains that shrinking dimensions to 0.7 times doubles the number of transistors that fit in the same area and makes them 1.4 times faster. By also lowering the voltage to 0.7 times, power consumption stayed almost the same.

Also, at that time, the power used while switches were off was small enough to ignore. So there was almost no need to worry about leakage current.

From the late 1990s, leakage started to rise

From the late 1990s, however, things changed. As transistors got smaller, leakage current suddenly started to increase. Behind this was a particular reason related to keeping speed up. From the next section, we will look at that reason using a sluice gate comparison.

Think of the switch as a sluice gate

Imagine a transistor as a sluice gate on a river. When you raise the gate, water flows. But if you raise it only a little, no water flows. It starts to flow only after the gate passes a set height. This height where flow begins is the "threshold voltage." The force that lifts the gate corresponds to the voltage applied to the chip.

According to the article, the amount of current that flows is determined by the difference between the applied voltage and the threshold voltage. The bigger the difference, the more current flows. In sluice gate terms, the higher you lift the gate above the set height, the more strongly the water flows.

Why does lowering the voltage make chips slower?

To save electricity, chips have lowered their applied voltage each time they were made smaller. But the threshold voltage does not change much even as transistors shrink. As a result, the lifting height gets closer to the set height. In other words, the difference between the applied voltage and the threshold voltage shrinks, and less electricity flows. The EE Times Japan article explains that the current is proportional to the square of this difference. So when the difference gets smaller, the current drops even more sharply than the difference does.

When less electricity flows, it takes longer for the next switch to start working. This is because circuit speed depends heavily on the time it takes for electricity from one switch to drive the next switch. That is why lowering the voltage alone does not make a chip faster.

Check quiz: Why does less electricity flow when the voltage is lowered?

Answer: Because the difference between the applied voltage and the threshold voltage shrinks.

Lower the set height, and now it leaks

The idea that came up was to lower the threshold voltage. If the set height is lower, water flows well with just a small lift, so speed can be kept. A talk abstract from the Institute of Electronics, Information and Communication Engineers (IEICE) also describes how the supply voltage was lowered while lowering the threshold voltage as transistors were scaled down.

But when the set height is low, water gets through even a tiny gap. Water slowly leaks from a gate that should be closed. This is leakage current. The lower the threshold voltage, the more electricity leaks while the switch is off. This is why power drains even when you are not using the device.

Check quiz: What increases when the threshold voltage is lowered?

Answer: The electricity that leaks while the switch is off, in other words, leakage current.

If you don't lower it, power during operation goes up

So what if you don't lower the threshold voltage? Leakage does not increase. But then you can no longer lower the applied voltage, because you need to keep the voltage difference to get enough current. As a result, the power the chip uses while running goes up a lot.

Speed and standby power become a trade-off

In short: choose speed, and it leaks during standby. Hold down leakage, and it uses more power while running. Chips were caught in this dilemma. The EE Times Japan article explains this situation as the end of the old Dennard scaling.

To escape this dilemma, the shape and materials of transistors have been changed. The IEICE abstract also lists strained silicon, FinFETs, and other approaches as ways to improve performance. In upcoming articles, we will look at those approaches one by one.

Word review

  • Transistor: A tiny switch that lets electricity flow or stops it.
  • Leakage current: Electricity that keeps flowing a little even after a switch is turned off.
  • Threshold voltage: The voltage at which a switch starts to turn on. In the sluice gate comparison, it is the set height where flow begins.
  • Standby: The state when a chip is not processing anything.
  • Supply voltage: The voltage applied to a chip. In the sluice gate comparison, it is the force that lifts the gate.
  • Scaling (miniaturization): Making transistors smaller.

Common misunderstandings

Q. Does leakage current happen because the switch is broken?

No. Even a transistor that works normally lets a little electricity flow while it is off. The lower the threshold voltage, the more it increases.

Q. Does lowering the voltage always make a chip faster?

No. Lowering the voltage reduces the electricity that flows, so speed actually stops improving.

Q. Does lowering the threshold voltage solve the problem?

No. Speed can be kept, but leakage while the switch is off increases, so standby power goes up. Whether to lower the threshold voltage is a trade-off between valuing speed and valuing standby power.

Summary

Leakage current is electricity that keeps flowing a little even after a switch is turned off, and it started to rise sharply in the late 1990s.
Lowering the threshold voltage to keep speed increases the electricity that leaks while the switch is off.
If the threshold voltage is not lowered, the supply voltage cannot be lowered either, and power during operation goes up, so chips were caught in a dilemma.

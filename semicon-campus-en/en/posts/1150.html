Marvell announced plans to demonstrate 2nm optical interconnect technology for AI data centers at ECOC 2026. The demos include 400Gbps-per-lane optical PAM4, 800G ZR/ZR+ pluggables with MACsec, 1.6T ZR, and 1.6T O-band coherent-lite. However, what can be confirmed at the time of the announcement is that these demos are "planned." It has not been confirmed that the demos were completed, or that volume shipments or customer deployments have taken place. Marvell's official announcement

What was announced

The core of the announcement is using 2nm-generation designs in optical communication components that connect AI computers, and showing several technologies together that serve different distances and uses. According to Marvell, the things being connected include XPUs, CPUs, AI accelerators, and memory resources, which relates to scale-up, scale-out, and scale-across in AI data centers.

The dates need care. ECOC 2026 runs from September 20 to 24, 2026, but that is the event schedule. The investor page and the official press release list show the announcement date as September 21, 2026, while the newsroom text with the same content is dated September 20, 2026. A simple guide to optical DSPs and PAM4

An optical DSP is a semiconductor that converts electrical signals into optical signals and uses digital processing to correct waveform distortion and errors that occur as signals travel through optical fiber. Optical fiber alone does not make high-speed communication possible; the DSP shapes the signal at the sending and receiving ends so the other side can read the data correctly. As the number of AI servers and the traffic between accelerators grow, it becomes more important to do this processing with little power and area. Marvell's explanation of optical DSPs

PAM4 is a modulation method that represents signal voltage with 4 levels. Because one signal change can represent the equivalent of 2 bits, it is easier to reach the same speed with fewer electrical changes than with conventional 2-level methods. On the other hand, the gap between levels is narrower, making it more sensitive to noise and distortion. Marvell describes PAM4 DSPs as mainly for connections within data centers up to about 500 m.

The 400G/lane this time is a speed metric that leads to connections such as the 1.6T class by bundling multiple lanes. However, the number of lanes, effective throughput, bit error rate, fiber distance, and measured power consumption cannot be determined from the sources available this time.

How do coherent and coherent-lite differ?

The coherent method uses not only the amplitude of light but also multiple types of information such as phase, and performs complex signal processing in the DSP. It makes it easier to handle signals that have weakened or changed shape over long distances, and Marvell's technical explanation places it for connections from several tens of kilometers to over 1,000 km.

Coherent-lite is an approach that sits between long-distance coherent and the PAM4 used inside data centers. Marvell describes it as being for campus connections of about 2–20 km using the O-band. In other words, it targets areas such as links between buildings on the same site or between nearby data centers, where PAM4 cannot reach far enough and long-distance coherent tends to make the setup large. The background to 1.6T O-band coherent-lite is the description of Aquila, announced in 2024. Official announcement about Aquila

Why it matters for AI data centers

In AI processing, the accelerators that do the computing exchange huge amounts of data. Even with high-performance compute chips, if the connections between chips are slow, cannot reach far enough, or use too much power, the system as a whole cannot reach its full performance. That is why setups that use PAM4, coherent-lite, and ZR/ZR+ for different distances, within a data center, within a campus, and between data centers, are important.

Marvell says that moving to 2nm along with its optical and silicon design can improve performance, bandwidth density, and power efficiency. However, this is the company's claim. The phrase "industry-first" and the claimed benefits of low power and high performance cannot be independently compared with competing products based on this announcement alone. Nor can actual power, yield, reach, or effective speed be calculated from the name of the 2nm process generation alone. Marvell's explanation in the announcement

From optical DSP demos to notes that explain test conditions

When you read about 400G or 1.6T announcements, organize per-lane speed, configuration, and test conditions in the same table. Check planned demos and specs usable in volume production separately. How to tell plans from proven results

In March 2026, Marvell announced the 1.6T ZR/ZR+ DSP based on 2nm Electra and the 800G ZR/ZR+ DSP based on 2nm Libra, and said it planned to start customer sampling in the second half of 2026. Sampling here is a plan for a stage in which selected customers evaluate the products; it does not mean the start of volume production, volume yields, or broad adoption. Official announcement of Electra and Libra

Going forward, we need to check whether the planned demos at ECOC were actually completed, what the measured power consumption and bit error rate are, and under what distance and optical module conditions they were measured. Compatibility with standards, interoperability with other companies' products, volume production timing, and actual customer adoption also matter. The sources this time do not confirm any such independent verification results or customer deployments. No correction or revised release on this announcement has been found since the official announcement, but that does not guarantee there will be no updates during or after ECOC.

Summary

This news shows Marvell using 2nm optical DSPs to present a lineup of optical interconnects matched to distance, from 400G/lane PAM4 to 800G, 1.6T, and future high-bandwidth connections. It is worth watching as a direction for increasing communication capacity in AI data centers, but at this stage it centers on demo plans and the company's performance claims. Only when volume production, measured performance, interoperability, and customer adoption are confirmed can we judge whether it is a technology that changes real infrastructure.

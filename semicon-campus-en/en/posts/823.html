Airbags usually do not open during hard braking, yet in a serious crash they start to deploy in an instant.
That difference is not decided simply by "how strong the impact is."
The basic idea is that a MEMS accelerometer measures the car's motion, the ECU evaluates how that signal changes over time, and the system chooses which restraint devices are needed.

The short answer
An airbag is not "a switch that opens above a certain G." Sensors turn the car body's motion into electrical signals, and a decision-making computer called the ECU looks at the direction, how fast the signal rises, how long it lasts, the waveform, the estimated change in speed, and whether it agrees with the other sensors, to decide whether deployment is needed.

What happens inside the car at the moment of a crash?

When a car moving forward hits an object, the car body's speed changes a lot in a short time. The quantity that shows "how much the speed changed over time" is acceleration. In everyday talk we tend to call it "a strong impact," but what reaches the control unit is not a single feeling. It is a series of acceleration values, one for each moment in time. Plotted on a graph, they form a line of peaks and valleys, which we will call a time waveform here.

The waveform is not the same at every part of the car body. A sensor near the front of the car tends to pick up signals early from where the deformation starts, while a central sensor near the cabin tends to capture motion closer to that of the whole car body. In side impacts, lateral acceleration is used, and some models also use pressure changes inside the doors. In models that handle rollovers, rotation data also comes into play.

However, not every car has the same number or type of sensors. The layout, the axes measured, the data used, and the devices fired vary by model and year. The important thing is not to think of it as one "impact button" somewhere in the car being pressed, but to understand that the system can use time-series signals from several locations and physical quantities.

A MEMS accelerometer is "a tiny machine inside a chip"

MEMS are tiny machines and sensors made using semiconductor processing technology. A typical capacitive MEMS accelerometer for airbags has a tiny mass made of silicon, thin springs, moving electrodes, and fixed electrodes. It may look like an ordinary electronic part, but inside there is a mechanical structure that really does move, just very slightly.

When the car body and the sensor's housing suddenly change motion, the mass inside does not follow that change right away, because of inertia. It is similar to how luggage slides forward in a car that brakes hard. But this is just a comparison to help you understand. In reality, the mass inside the chip is held by springs and shifts only a tiny distance relative to the housing.

When the moving electrode attached to the mass shifts, the gap to the fixed electrode on one side gets narrower, and the gap on the other side gets wider. The ability to store electric charge between electrodes is called capacitance, and when the gap changes, the capacitance changes too. A differential method that compares the changes on both sides makes it possible to read electrically which way and how far the mass has moved.

What the MEMS knows here is "how the car body's motion changed." The sensor itself does not understand whether this is a frontal crash or a curb, or whether it is severe enough to need deployment. Keeping this division of roles in mind helps you avoid the misconception that "the sensor felt the impact and opened the airbag directly."

How is a crash recognized? The time waveform matters more than the peak value

It is easy to describe the airbag decision as "it opens above so many G," but that cannot explain the differences between hard braking, a bump, a localized hit, and a frontal crash. G is a way of expressing the size of an acceleration relative to standard gravity. The ECU evaluates not only that size, but also which direction it started from, how fast it rose, how long it lasted, and what shape it took as it changed.

Think about two waveforms with peaks of the same height. One is short like a needle, and the other is a bit wider and lasts longer. Their peak values are the same, but when you include time, they are different events. Adding up acceleration over time can sometimes give an estimate of ΔV (delta-V), which shows how much the car's speed changed during the crash. A short, large input and a longer-lasting input can lead to different estimated speed changes, even with the same peak.

That does not mean ΔV is the only factor, either. What public materials let us explain in general terms is the idea of using direction, waveform, estimated speed change, agreement with peripheral sensors, and so on. Which features are used, how they are weighted, and at what values the system fires are confidential details that automakers calibrate for each model.

What does the ECU decide?

ECU stands for Electronic Control Unit. Here it means a small computer that makes the airbag decision and fires the igniters. If the MEMS accelerometer is the "measurer," the ECU is the "decision-maker." It deals with unwanted high-frequency components and zero-point drift in the incoming signal, and evaluates when a possible crash began, its direction, the features of the waveform, and its severity.

Besides the central acceleration, it can also use peripheral sensors at the front and sides, and, in vehicles that have them, pressure and rotation data. The ECU makes its decision while checking that these inputs do not contradict each other physically. However, there is no fixed rule that "it will not open unless two independent sensors always agree at the same time." The sensors used and the safety-check methods differ by model.

Another important step after a crash is recognized is selection. Frontal, side, curtain, and other airbags protect in different directions. Depending on the model, the system also uses whether a seat is occupied, occupant classification, seat position, seatbelt buckle status, and so on, to choose which devices to activate and at what stage to inflate them. That is why not every airbag necessarily opens at once in the same crash.

Some ECUs include a separate safety check to prevent accidental firing, self-diagnosis, communication diagnostics, monitoring of the igniter circuits for breaks and shorts, and power monitoring. Some designs also store energy in capacitors so that the unit can keep processing and firing for a short time even if the crash damages the vehicle's main power or wiring. This does not mean that "airbags have their own dedicated battery."

The steps up to airbag deployment

When the ECU decides deployment is needed and the safety conditions are also met, it sends current to the target igniter circuits. The igniter starts the inflator, which fills the bag. In pyrotechnic types, a solid gas-generating material reacts at a controlled rate to produce gas. In stored-gas types, gas kept under high pressure is released in a controlled way. There are also hybrid types that combine both.

The gas flows into the folded bag, pushes the cover open, and the airbag deploys. The deployed bag cushions the occupant from hitting the car's interior directly. Frontal, side, curtain, and other airbags protect in different directions and have different bag shapes, so they cannot all be described as moving the same way.

In setups where the same decision also activates the seatbelt pretensioner, it quickly takes up slack in the belt and ties the occupant to the seat sooner. Airbags and seatbelts are not competitors. Together they form a supplemental restraint system that shares the job of catching the occupant.

Now the airbag's "measure → decide → activate" chain is connected.

Clearing up common misconceptions

Q1. Does an airbag always open above a certain G?

No. Besides the size of the acceleration, the system evaluates direction, rise, duration, time waveform, estimated speed change, agreement with peripheral sensors, and more. The specific decision values are confidential calibration data for each model.

Q2. Will an airbag never open during hard braking or over a bump?

You cannot say "never." Hard braking, bumps, localized hits, and crashes generally produce signals with different directions and shapes, and the ECU uses the time waveform and other data to tell them apart. The result depends on the crash conditions and vehicle design.

Q3. Does the accelerometer judge the crash and fire the airbag directly?

No. A MEMS accelerometer is the measurer that turns the car body's motion into an electrical signal. The ECU is what judges the type and severity of a crash and chooses which devices to fire.

Q4. Do all the airbags open in a crash?

No. Frontal, side, curtain, and other airbags protect in different directions. The ECU evaluates the crash mode and severity and, depending on the model, also uses occupant, seat, and belt data to choose the devices needed.

Q5. If a car has airbags, are seatbelts unnecessary?

No. Airbags are an SRS, a supplemental restraint system that supports seatbelts. Only when you sit properly and wear your seatbelt can each do its job.

What to know for safety

Airbags deploy extremely fast, so it is important not to sit closer than necessary to the steering wheel or dashboard, and to adjust your seat and seatbelt as the vehicle owner's manual says. For the front passenger airbag and child seats, also follow the model's manual and official safety guidance.

If the SRS warning light comes on, if an airbag deployed in a crash, or if you are unsure about the system's condition after a crash, do not take it apart or do electrical tests yourself. Talk to a dealer designated by the manufacturer or to a repair shop. Removing an igniter or inflator yourself, or hitting the bumper to test it, is dangerous.

Also, the fact that an airbag did not open does not by itself mean it was "broken" or that "the crash was minor." Whether deployment is needed is not decided by just one thing, such as how the car looks damaged, the driving speed, or peak G. It depends on vehicle-specific decisions that include crash direction, car body structure, occupant conditions, and more, so judgments after a crash should be left to experts.

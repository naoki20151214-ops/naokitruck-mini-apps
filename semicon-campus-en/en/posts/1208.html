How Do You Find a Helium Leak? Pinpointing Vacuum Leaks by Signal

A small leak in vacuum equipment may not be visible from the outside. Helium leak testing is not about finding a hole with your eyes. You apply helium from outside the equipment and detect the helium that enters the vacuum side through the leak path. Then you link the spot you sprayed with the time the signal appears to narrow down the cause.

What you will learn in this article

This article explains what a helium leak detector (HLD) actually detects, where the test targets are on semiconductor equipment, and how to tell real leaks, virtual leaks, and outgassing apart when the vacuum level is poor. It does not cover model-specific calibration values or maintenance procedures. Instead, it focuses on cause and effect, so beginners do not misread what a measurement means.

Helium leak testing uses a gas from outside as the clue

In the vacuum spray method, you first pump down the inside of the test piece from the HLD side. Next, you apply helium locally to spots where a leak is suspected, such as outer flanges, O-ring seals, welds, and pipe connections. If there is a path that links the outside to the vacuum side, the pressure difference pulls helium inside, and it travels through the piping to the detector. The detector does not see the shape of the hole. It sees a signal that matches the amount of helium arriving from the test piece.

Here, it is important not to confuse the spot you spray with the place where the signal rises. The spot where you hold the probe is not projected directly into the detector. The helium passes through one connected measurement system, including the test piece, test port, pumps, valves, vacuum gauges, analyzer tube, and calibrated leak, and is finally turned into a signal in the analyzer. The piping volume and how the pumps run also change how long it takes for the display to change after you apply helium.

The HLD picks out helium and displays it as a signal

In some HLD analyzers, the incoming gas is ionized, and helium ions are selected and detected based on differences in mass-to-charge ratio. The mass-to-charge ratio is the ratio of an ion's mass to its charge. In the magnetic deflection type, conditions are set so that helium ions with a mass-to-charge ratio of 4 reach the detector. In the counterflow type, the analyzer tube is kept at high vacuum while part of the helium is guided in, using the fact that a turbomolecular pump compresses different gases by different amounts.

One key point for beginners: the number on the display is not the "diameter of the hole." The displayed value expresses the signal from the selected helium that reached the detector as a leak rate, based on the measurement system's conditions and its calibration. Even if the screen shows a number, that number alone does not tell you the shape, material, or location of the leak path.

Use a calibrated leak to check how the signal relates to the displayed value

A calibrated leak is used before measuring. A calibrated leak is a reference that supplies a known, repeatable helium flow. By checking how the detector's signal relates to the value displayed for the calibrated leak, you build the basis for treating the number on screen as a measurement result. Calibration here does not mean setting pass/fail values for a specific tool on site. It is the idea of linking the signal to the display.

So if you apply helium to the same spot and get a different response each time, do not simply decide that the leak has grown. You need to check the conditions of the whole measurement system, such as connections, background helium, pump status, response wait time, and calibration status. This article does not go into operating steps or specific judgment values. It only covers the point that calibration is what lets you interpret the displayed value.

A worse vacuum alone does not prove a real leak

When the vacuum does not reach its target, it is easy to jump to replacing a seal or picturing a hole to the outside. But a pressure problem can have several causes. A real leak is a flow path that connects the outside to the vacuum inside. A virtual leak, on the other hand, is gas slowly escaping from a small trapped volume, such as the bottom of a blind hole or a space inside the equipment. Because it is not a hole through to the outside, spraying helium at one spot outside may not give the same location match as a real leak.

Outgassing is gas from walls, seals, deposits, or inside materials coming out into the vacuum. The change during pump-down or pressure rise may slow down over time. Gas can also enter by permeating through materials, so you cannot say, "There is a helium signal, so there must be a hole." Real leaks, virtual leaks, and outgassing need to be considered from both structure and time.

On site, combine pressure changes over time with local He response

There are two axes for isolating the cause. The first is how the pressure rise changes over time. The second is whether the spot where you sprayed helium and the timing of the HLD signal match up repeatably. If a real leak dominates, it is easier to explain the gas load from outside together with the local He response. With outgassing, a different trend may appear, such as the change slowing down over time.

However, response time cannot be reduced to one number across different models. The volume of the test piece, the length of the piping, the pumps, the valves, and the amount of helium sprayed all vary. If the local He response is weak, or its link to location does not repeat, keep virtual leaks, outgassing, permeation, and the state of the measurement system on the list of candidates. RGA (residual gas analysis) is used as supporting information to check which gases are in the vacuum.

In semiconductor equipment, vacuum integrity supports atmosphere control

In semiconductor manufacturing equipment, vacuum is not just a pressure reading. It is a way to control the process atmosphere. In an example of a single-wafer deposition system described by Tokyo Electron (TEL), a wafer whose oxide film has been removed is not exposed to oxygen or moisture. Instead, it moves to the next step through vacuum transfer and high-vacuum modules. This is an example of one specific tool, but it shows clearly why the inflow of unwanted gases must be kept low to keep the process stable.

In equipment maintenance, when you see a "poor vacuum" problem, combine the pressure history, local He response, calibration status, and RGA if needed. Whether the reading worsened only briefly, responded to helium at a specific spot, or showed a gas load that dropped over time changes where you should check next. The value of the test is that it does not squeeze an invisible problem into one number. It lets you read several clues through the same cause-and-effect logic.

When you describe leak testing as maintenance experience, the key point is that you recorded the pressure history, local He response, and calibration status separately. Do not decide to replace a seal just because the vacuum got worse. Write down how you narrowed down the candidates in the measurement system and in the equipment. Common misunderstandings

Q. Does an HLD look at the leak hole like a camera?

No. Helium applied from outside travels through the leak path to the detector, and that gas is mass-analyzed and turned into a signal. It does not take a picture of the hole's shape or diameter.

Q. If the vacuum is poor, is it always a real leak?

No. Virtual leaks from blind holes, outgassing from walls and materials, and permeation are also candidates. Combine the local He response with pressure changes over time.

Q. If a signal appears, can you immediately conclude that the sprayed spot is the cause?

Not right away. Check the response time of the measurement system, including piping and pumps, the background, and the calibration status, and see whether the match between location and time repeats.

Researchers at Kanazawa University and Germany's Diamond and Carbon Applications have demonstrated a "selective etching" technique that removes only part of a surface at the nanometer scale while keeping an atomically flat diamond (111) surface intact. The research paper was published online on September 12, 2026, and formally published on September 16. The university's news release came out on October 2. So September 16 is the paper's formal publication date, and October 2 is the date the results were introduced; they are not two dates for the same event. The results are based on Kanazawa University's announcement and the abstract of the published paper.

What was demonstrated

In this process, a Ti/Au metal mask was formed on the diamond surface, and hydrogen plasma with added nitrogen (H2+N2) was applied. By selectively removing the areas not covered by the mask, the process creates structures such as grooves or steps on a flat surface. According to the paper's abstract, the etching rate is about 1nm/min, Sq, which shows the roughness of the bottom surface after processing, is 0.25nm or less, and the depth is on the order of about 100nm.Paper abstract

Here, etching means a process that removes material little by little using chemical reactions or plasma. Plasma is a technique that uses electrical energy to create reactive particles, making it easier for atoms on a solid surface to react and be removed. The researchers explain that with hydrogen plasma alone, sideways etching tends to stand out, but adding nitrogen also advances vertical removal, making depth easier to control.

Under an atomic force microscope (AFM), steps and pits matching atomic layers were observed. An AFM is an instrument that scans a sharp probe across a surface to examine bumps and dips close to the atomic level. The researchers propose a "layer-by-layer" mechanism, in which sideways and vertical etching combine to remove atomic layers one by one. This is the researchers' interpretation of how the process works, and it does not mean it has been confirmed that the same mechanism holds under all conditions.Kanazawa University announcement

Why diamond processing matters

Diamond is a candidate for next-generation power semiconductors because it excels in high thermal conductivity, which helps release heat; a high breakdown field, which lets it withstand large voltages; and carrier mobility, which relates to how well it carries charge. Quantum sensing using NV centers in the crystal is another possible application. On the other hand, diamond is chemically stable, so fine processing is not easy, and mechanical polishing can introduce damage and defects to the surface.This announcement

Crystal faces and surface roughness are especially important. An atomically flat surface, like the diamond (111) surface, is a surface with very small bumps even at the atomic level. In a MOSFET, bumps at the boundary between the semiconductor and the insulating film can scatter charges and affect current flow. So it is not enough to make a flat surface; you also need a technique that forms grooves and steps without breaking that flatness.

In the past, the Kanazawa University research group worked on flattening surfaces while limiting mechanical damage, by heating diamond in contact with nickel and using the way carbon dissolves into nickel. This new research is not that flattening technique itself. Instead, it starts from an atomically flat (111) surface and uses a mask and plasma for selective 3D processing.Explanation of the 2021 flattening technique

How it could help semiconductor devices

Diamond power devices need steps that create grooves, steps, and interfaces with insulating films on the surface or inside, to control current paths and electric fields. If, as in this case, the etching rate is relatively slow at about 1nm/min and the bottom roughness stays small, it may become easier to design process conditions that approach the target depth. Low-roughness surfaces are likely useful in steps where interface quality and processing damage easily affect device characteristics. However, this is a technical interpretation drawn from these processing results, and it does not mean better performance in finished devices has been demonstrated.

Kanazawa University points to possible applications in forming fine structures for future diamond MOSFETs, power devices, and quantum sensors. But these are the researchers' outlook. From these materials, we cannot confirm the timing of commercialization, throughput on mass production lines, manufacturing cost, long-term reliability, or the electrical characteristics of finished devices. Between processing in a lab and stable mass production in a factory, there are several stages of verification, such as mask durability, uniformity across the whole wafer, and reproducibility of plasma conditions.Kanazawa University announcement

Limits of these results, and what to watch next

The first limit is the range where the numbers apply. The values of about 1nm/min, Sq of 0.25nm or less, and about 100nm are results under the conditions shown in the paper's abstract. They do not prove that the same performance can be achieved with every diamond wafer or shape. If substrate quality, crystal orientation, mask material, or plasma conditions change, the etching rate and roughness may also change.

Second, device performance after processing is still a separate issue to verify. Unless actual MOSFETs and power devices are made and their on-resistance, breakdown voltage, leakage current, thermal behavior, and long-term reliability are measured, we cannot judge how much the processing technique contributes to product performance. For quantum sensors, too, the effect of surface processing on NV center properties and noise needs to be checked.

Going forward, the points to watch are processing uniformity on larger wafers, support for complex mask patterns, balancing etching rate and surface quality, contamination control after mask removal, and integration into real devices. These results lay a foundation for moving from studying diamond as an "excellent material" to process technology that designs shapes at the atomic level. To judge practical use, we need to wait for device prototypes that follow this processing demonstration, and for independent evaluations of reproducibility and reliability.Introduction from the Kanazawa University Diamond Research Center

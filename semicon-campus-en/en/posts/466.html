What is die bonding?

Die bonding is the process of attaching a semiconductor chip inside a package. In English, it is called die bonding or die attach. After dicing cuts the wafer into individual chips, each chip is placed at a set position on a substrate, lead frame, interposer, or similar base and attached.

What matters here is not just attaching the chip, but placing it "in the right position, facing the right way, stably, and in a way that meets heat and electrical requirements." If a chip is slightly shifted, tilted, or has air gaps left in the bonding layer, it can cause problems later in wire bonding, encapsulation, or testing.

ASMPT describes die bonding as an important assembly process that attaches semiconductor chips to substrates. Materials makers such as Henkel and Indium also treat die attach materials as materials that affect package reliability and thermal performance. In other words, die bonding is a process where equipment, materials, temperature, pressure, and position accuracy all come together.

If you are reading on your phone, this is all you need to start. Die bonding is "the process of accurately attaching a cut chip to the base of its package." The key to quality is keeping misalignment, tilt, voids, and poor adhesion under control.

Where does it fit in the back-end process?

Semiconductor manufacturing is broadly divided into the front-end process and the back-end process. In the front-end process, transistors and wiring are made on a silicon wafer. In the back-end process, that wafer is cut apart, and each chip is assembled into a form that can be used as an actual component.

Die bonding is a typical process that comes right after dicing. Dicing cuts the chips apart, die bonding attaches them to a base, and then the chips move on to wire bonding or flip-chip bonding, encapsulation, and final testing.

DicingThe process of cutting the wafer into individual chips. It is important to pass chips to the next process with minimal chipping, cracking, and contamination.
Die bondingThe process of attaching the cut chips to a substrate or lead frame. Position, tilt, and bond condition are controlled.

The flow from dicing to die bonding

The detailed conditions for die bonding vary with the product, package structure, materials, and equipment. But for beginners, it is easiest to understand it as the following five steps.

1. Pick up the chip

After dicing, the chips are lined up on tape. The tool recognizes the target chip and gently lifts it with a vacuum nozzle or similar part. At this point, it is important not to crack the chip, not to scratch its surface, and not to get its orientation wrong. When a job posting mentions terms like "pickup," "chip feeding," or "transfer," the job may include work or equipment management around this step.

2. Align with the substrate or lead frame

The base that the chip is attached to has a set position where the chip must go. The tool uses cameras and other means to see the positions of the chip and the base, and aligns them so they do not shift. If alignment is loose, defects are more likely later in wiring connection or visual inspection.

3. Attach with adhesive, solder, sintering material, or similar

Methods for attaching the chip include epoxy adhesives, die attach film, solder, and silver sintering material. Which method is used depends on how well the product needs to release heat, the electrical connection, cost, and reliability.

4. Heat, cure, and bond

When adhesive or film is used, heat is applied to cure it. When solder or sintering material is used, controlling conditions such as temperature, time, and pressure is also important. If the temperature is too low, adhesion may be poor. If it is too high, it can put extra stress on the materials or the chip.

5. Check position, tilt, and voids

After attachment, the chip is checked to see whether it is in the correct position, whether it is tilted, and whether any voids remain in the bonding layer. Visual inspection, X-ray inspection, and checking tool data may be involved. Small oddities found here lead to yield improvement later.

A quick look at the different attachment methods

The attachment method used in die bonding varies with the type of product. As a beginner, you do not need to memorize every material name. It is enough to see the big differences: "attaching with adhesive," "attaching with film," and "using solder or sintering to release heat more easily."

For products that generate a lot of heat, such as power semiconductors, getting heat out of the chip is very important. Indium explains that thermal conductivity and void reduction are key points for die attach materials. Henkel also presents die attach films and pastes as materials that affect the reliability of wire-bonded packages.

What to look for in quality

Misalignment

This is when the chip is shifted from its set position. If the shift is large, problems may appear later in wire bonding or encapsulation, or the chip may be rejected in visual inspection. The tool's camera recognition, transfer, and substrate positioning are involved.

Tilt

This is when the chip does not sit level and is slanted. With tilt, the thickness of the bonding layer varies from place to place, which can affect how heat escapes and how connections are made in later processes. Even a difference that looks small can affect package reliability.

Voids

A void is an empty space left inside the bonding layer. If there are many air gaps, heat may have trouble escaping, or the bond strength may drop. Especially for products that need to release heat, how well voids are suppressed is important.

Poor adhesion

This is when the adhesive or solder does not work well enough and the chip is not firmly attached. The amount of material, heating conditions, pressure, and surface condition are involved. Poor adhesion affects reliability in later processes and during use.

The link to yield
Die bonding defects sometimes show up later as questions like "Why do chips in only this lot peel off easily?", "Why do they break easily from heat?", or "Why have visual defects increased?" That is why it is important to look at tool conditions, material lots, temperature, pressure, and inspection results together.

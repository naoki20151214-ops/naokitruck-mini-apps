On September 29, 2026, Teradyne announced "Magnum E2," a high-speed memory tester designed for testing next-generation memory used in AI and high-performance computing. It lists LPDDR6, DDR6, GDDR7, and NAND flash interfaces as targets, and says memory testing and logic testing can be handled on one platform. Teradyne's announcement

In short, this news is a product announcement: as AI memory becomes faster and more complex, the testers that check quality on production lines are also trying to expand what they can handle. However, an announcement is different from the product already shipping in volume and its benefits being proven at customer fabs. These materials do not list a shipment start date, customer names, price, measured throughput, or yield improvement rate.

What was announced with Magnum E2?

Magnum E2 is a tool that electrically drives DRAM and NAND flash interfaces and checks whether data can be read and written correctly. Teradyne's product page shows up to 64 test sites, 24.5K high-speed I/O pins, and configurations for volume production and for engineering. A test site here is a unit that lets multiple memory devices be measured in parallel. The more sites there are, the more devices can be inspected at once under the same conditions. Magnum E2 product page

Teradyne describes the following features for Magnum E2.

  • Support for LPDDR6, DDR6, GDDR7, and NAND flash interfaces
  • Support for multiple high-speed signaling methods: NRZ, PAM3, and PAM4
  • A programmable algorithmic pattern generator (APG)
  • Memory testing and logic testing on the same platform
  • Shared hardware and software from evaluation and qualification through high-volume production

Two key terms for beginners

NRZ and PAM3/PAM4

NRZ is a method that basically represents a signal with two voltage levels. It is close to the idea of digital "0" and "1" and is easy to understand, but as speeds rise, there is a limit to how much information one signal line can carry.

PAM is a method that sends information using multiple voltage levels. PAM3 uses three levels and PAM4 uses four. It may represent more states in the same amount of time, but the gaps between voltages become narrower, making it more sensitive to noise and waveform distortion. So a tester must be able to generate signals accurately and judge received waveforms in fine detail. Teradyne says Magnum E2 handles NRZ, PAM3, and PAM4 on shared hardware. Teradyne's announcement

Near-DUT Test (NDT)

DUT means "device under test." Near-DUT Test is a design approach that places digital instruments close to the memory chip being measured. When the wiring between the equipment and the chip is long, signal reflections, losses, and timing shifts are more likely. Moving the instruments closer is expected to reduce these effects and make high-speed signals easier to handle.

Teradyne says NDT improves signal quality during high-speed testing and can support performance and device yield. However, this is the manufacturer's product claim. No yield improvement rate from independent measurement or results at specific customer fabs have been published. Teradyne's product page

Why memory testers matter in the AI era

AI accelerators process huge amounts of data in a short time. To get the most out of them, not only the compute chips but also the bandwidth, latency, and power efficiency of the memory that feeds them data are important. The faster memory interfaces become, the more strictly signal timing and voltage must be checked during manufacturing.

On the standards side, JEDEC announced the publication of the LPDDR6 standard JESD209-6 on July 9, 2025. LPDDR6 is described as a high-speed, low-power memory standard that includes mobile and AI uses, and the announcement also mentions a dual sub-channel structure and 32-byte access granularity. JEDEC's LPDDR6 announcement

JEDEC also announced the publication of the GDDR7 standard JESD239 on March 5, 2024. GDDR7 is described as a standard for graphics, gaming, compute, networking, and AI, and it uses a PAM interface. The spread of such standards and signaling methods drives demand for testers that can handle multiple generations and methods. JEDEC's GDDR7 announcement

What the announcement tells us, and what it does not

What we can read from the Magnum E2 announcement is that memory test equipment is moving toward emphasizing "support for high-speed signals," "parallel measurement of many devices," "a shared platform from evaluation to volume production," and "integration of memory and logic." If testers fall behind, then even when new memory can be designed, pre-production inspection and isolating the causes of defects become difficult.

On the other hand, the following points cannot be confirmed from these materials alone.

  • Whether volume shipments of Magnum E2 have already started
  • Which customers have adopted or qualified it
  • What the actual test speed and throughput are
  • By what percentage yield improved
  • When it will contribute to revenue or profit
  • Whether DDR6 standardization or market launch is complete

In particular, the fact that Teradyne lists DDR6 as a supported target does not prove that DDR6 standardization is complete or when products will launch. The JEDEC background materials confirmed this time are about LPDDR6 and GDDR7, and the same conclusions cannot be extended to DDR6.

What to watch next

Teradyne has announced plans to exhibit Magnum E2 at SEMICON West from October 13 to 15, 2026. However, this is a future exhibition plan, not a volume shipment date or customer adoption date. Teradyne's announcement

Going forward, it is worth watching for the actual start of shipments, customer qualification, supported memory products, and independent performance data. It also matters what throughput and inspection quality specs such as up to 64 sites and 24.5K high-speed I/O pins deliver on real volume production lines. Even if demand for AI memory is strong, a tester announcement alone cannot confirm increased production capacity or improved yield.

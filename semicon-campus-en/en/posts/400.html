Overlay metrology is a technique in semiconductor manufacturing that measures whether the layer made earlier and the next layer made by lithography (exposure) line up in the correct position. Semiconductors are made by stacking many layers of circuitry on a single wafer, so even a tiny misalignment can keep wiring and contacts from connecting, which lowers yield.

What is overlay metrology?

In a word, overlay is how accurately the previous layer and the next layer line up.

In semiconductor manufacturing, the circuit is not made on the wafer all at once. You deposit a film, coat it with resist, expose it, etch it, clean it, and then make the next layer. By repeating these steps many times, a three-dimensional circuit is built up.

The problem is where the next layer is printed. If the next contact holes or wiring are not placed correctly on top of the wiring made earlier, the spots that should connect electrically end up shifted.

If you are reading on your phone, just remember this first. Overlay metrology measures "misalignment between stacked layers." It looks at the positional relationship between layers, not line width or film thickness.

Why is misalignment a problem?

A semiconductor circuit is not just a matter of drawing lines. Lines must connect to lines, wiring to contacts, and transistors to wiring, all at set positions.

For example, say there is wiring in the previous layer, and the next layer opens a hole down to that wiring to connect to it. If this hole is shifted slightly sideways, it misses the center of the wiring. This can raise resistance, make the connection unstable, or in the worst case cause a defect where there is no electrical connection at all.

The further scaling goes, the smaller the allowed misalignment becomes. A tiny shift that would not have mattered in the past can strongly affect yield in advanced processes. That is why overlay metrology is not just a check on the results of the lithography step. It is an important measurement for keeping a mass-production line stable.

Overlay metrology matters after lithography

Overlay metrology is closely tied to lithography. Lithography is the step that uses light to print circuit patterns onto the wafer. During this step, the exposure tool reads reference marks on the previous layer and aligns the next pattern to the correct position.

However, the actual position shifts slightly because of tiny wafer distortion, the condition of the tool, the resist and process conditions, temperature changes, and so on. So after exposure, overlay is measured to check "in which direction, and by how much, it is shifted."

The key point is that measuring is not the end. Overlay metrology results are fed back to the exposure tool and process conditions. In other words, overlay metrology looks like inspection, but it is actually also the starting point for process improvement.

A simpler way to say it
If lithography is "the step of pressing a printing block in the right place," overlay metrology is "the job of checking that the picture you print next is not shifted from the one you printed before." In semiconductors, this shift matters at the nanometer scale.

How it differs from CD-SEM and film thickness measurement

When you read articles about inspection and metrology, many similar terms come up: CD-SEM, film thickness measurement, defect inspection, overlay metrology, and more. Beginners easily get confused here, so it helps to separate their roles.

MeasurementWhat it looks atIn plain wordsRelated processes
CD-SEMLine width, hole size, pattern shapeMeasures how wide lines and holes areLithography, etching
Film thickness measurementThin-film thickness, uniformityChecks whether a film has the target thicknessDeposition, CMP, post-cleaning evaluation
Defect inspectionParticles, scratches, pattern abnormalitiesFinds foreign matter and anything oddAll processes
Overlay metrologyMisalignment between layersChecks how the previous and next layers line upLithography, metrology, yield improvement

Roughly speaking, CD-SEM looks at "width," film thickness measurement at "thickness," defect inspection at "abnormal spots," and overlay metrology at "position." All of them protect yield, but they look at different things.

DBO and SEM overlay are used at the leading edge

So far, to make it easy to picture, we have described overlay metrology as "looking at marks on the previous and next layers to measure misalignment." But in advanced semiconductors that use EUV lithography, patterns are extremely small, and in some cases simply measuring from an image is not enough.

That is where more advanced measurements such as DBO and SEM overlay become important.

DBOShort for Diffraction-Based Overlay. Light is shone on a fine diffraction grating pattern, and misalignment is estimated from the reflection and diffraction information.
SEM overlayAn approach that uses an electron beam to check misalignment in finer patterns and in structures closer to the actual device.
Feeding back to the processMeasurement results are fed back into exposure conditions, alignment, and correction models, and used to stabilize the next lot or next wafer.

Beginners do not need to understand the math behind DBO from the start. It is enough to remember that "overlay metrology includes not only image-based methods, but also advanced methods that use light diffraction or electron beams." Knowing this much makes it easier to see how terms connect when you read articles or job postings from metrology and lithography equipment makers such as KLA and ASML.

Connecting overlay, misalignment, and metrology tools to real work and further learning

In jobs that investigate misalignment, you link measured values with the history of the tool, temperature, and process, and sort out whether the tool side or the process side needs more checking. Maintaining metrology tools and setting measurement conditions are different areas of responsibility, so record the checks you did separately from the judgments made by others. There are two paths: building up basics in mechanics, controls, and measurement, and turning your experience into a conversation about technical roles.

The market value of people who understand overlay metrology

Overlay metrology is not just measurement work. You need to look at the measured values and connect them to exposure conditions, alignment, trends across the wafer, and process changes.

That is why people who understand overlay are a good fit for jobs in lithography, inspection and metrology, process improvement, and yield improvement. Especially in advanced semiconductors, alignment accuracy directly affects product performance and yield, so people who can feed metrology data back into the process are valuable.

LithographyLooks at exposure conditions, alignment, and scanner settings.
Inspection / metrologyHandles overlay, CD, film thickness, and defect data.
Yield improvementUses misalignment trends to trace defect causes back to the process.

KLA, ASML, Onto Innovation, Applied Materials, and others offer equipment and solutions related to lithography, inspection and metrology, and patterning control. When researching companies, you will understand more if you look at "which processes this company supports" and "how it connects measurement results to process improvement."

Why it leads to jobs at global equipment makers and technical roles

Overlay metrology is closely tied to ASML's lithography tools, metrology and inspection tools from KLA and others, and yield improvement on mass-production lines. In other words, it is not just a term. It is knowledge that tends to be valued in technical roles at equipment makers, device makers, and foundries.

Global equipment makers in particular have job openings that require both technical understanding and on-site skills, such as tool installation, customer support, process data analysis, and application support. Because these roles require English, travel, customer support, and equipment knowledge, pay can be in a higher range.

However, it is risky to think "I understand overlay, so I'll get a high annual salary." In reality, how you are evaluated depends on which experiences you can combine, such as lithography experience, handling metrology data, equipment maintenance, quality control, process improvement, and working in English. That is why it matters to check not just the company name in a job posting, but also terms in the job description such as "alignment," "metrology," "overlay," "process control," and "customer support."

What terms appear in job postings?

Overlay metrology sometimes appears by name in job postings, but it is often described with other terms. In particular, lithography, alignment, metrology, measurement, yield improvement, and process improvement are closely related areas.

If you are new to semiconductors, you do not need to aim to become an "overlay metrology expert" right away. First, it is important to look at the job posting to see which process the job is in, which tools and measurement data it handles, and whether you can be involved in improvement work too.

If you have a science background, manufacturer experience, or experience in quality control, measurement, data analysis, or equipment maintenance, knowing how overlay metrology works will help you read semiconductor job postings more accurately. In interviews, it will also be easier to explain in your own words "how lithography and metrology affect yield."

Summary: overlay metrology protects layer alignment

Overlay metrology is a technique that measures whether the previous layer and the next layer line up in the correct position. Semiconductors are made by stacking many layers of circuitry, so even a tiny misalignment can cause defects in wiring and connections.

CD-SEM looks at line width, film thickness measurement looks at thin-film thickness, and defect inspection finds abnormal spots. Overlay metrology, by contrast, looks at the positional relationship between layers. It is an important topic that links lithography, metrology, and yield improvement.

When you see terms like overlay, alignment, litho metrology, metrology, or yield improvement in a job posting, think of it as a job connected to what this article covers.

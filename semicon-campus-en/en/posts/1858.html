Difficulty: ★☆☆☆☆ Beginner
For people learning about semiconductor equipment for the first time

When you work with machines in a factory, they sometimes stop suddenly.

The same is true for the equipment that makes semiconductors.

One day, a tool suddenly stopped.

On the screen, you see messages like these.

  • Cooling water pressure is low
  • Temperature is high
  • Process power has stopped
  • Processing was aborted

That is four alarms at once.

So did four parts break at the same time?

Not necessarily.

This is a very important point for understanding equipment maintenance work.

The place named in an alarm is not always the place that broke

The first thing to know is this:

An alarm is not a message that tells you the cause.

That is the key idea.

What an alarm tells you is:

"I found an abnormal condition here."

That is all.

Let's think about a car as an example.

Say you are driving and the engine temperature gets high.

That does not necessarily mean

"The temperature gauge is broken!"

Not at all.

The coolant may be low.

The cooling fan may not be running.

The sensor that measures temperature could also be faulty.

In other words,

the symptom on the screen and the real cause may be different things.

That is the point.

The same goes for semiconductor equipment.

First, look at "what happened first"

Let's put the four messages from before in the order they happened.

  1. Cooling water pressure dropped
  2. Tool temperature rose
  3. Process power stopped
  4. Processing was aborted

Seen this way, the picture looks a little different, doesn't it?

At first, it looked like four separate failures.

But it may have been a single chain of events:

Cooling water pressure dropped
↓
The tool could no longer be cooled enough
↓
The temperature rose
↓
The tool stopped processing to avoid danger

That is a real possibility.

Of course, at this stage you still cannot say for sure that this is the true cause.

However,

you get much closer to the cause than by looking only at the "last alarm."

One abnormal event can trigger a series of other alarms, one after another.

There is a technical term for this:

Alarm Cascade

That is what it is called.

The name sounds difficult, but the meaning is simple.

Alarms appearing in a chain, starting from one abnormal event

That is all it means.

So should you just look for the "first alarm"?

Here, too, you need to think one step further.

For example, say the alarm

"Cooling water pressure low"

came first.

So can you decide that

the drop in cooling water pressure was the first abnormal event?

Is that right?

Actually, you still don't know.

That is because the cooling water pressure may have been dropping little by little before the alarm went off.

For example, it may have gone like this:

Normal
↓
A slight drop
↓
A further drop
↓
It crosses the set limit
↓
An alarm goes off

That could be the sequence.

In other words, what the maintenance technician wants to find is

not the first alarm that went off.

What they really want to find is

when the tool first entered a "different from usual" state

That is the goal.

This first change is sometimes called the

First Abnormal Event

in technical terms.

In plain words, it means roughly

"the first thing that went wrong"

and nothing more.

Rather than the technical term, it is easier to remember it as

"When did things start to go wrong?"

That works fine.

How do you find out "when"?

This is where the records stored in the tool come in.

Semiconductor equipment may record things like:

  • What time an alarm went off
  • What operations were done before that
  • What the pressure was
  • What the temperature was

and more.

While looking at those records, the maintenance technician lines them up like this:

10:02　Cooling water pressure drops slightly
10:03　It drops further
10:04　Temperature rises
10:05　Processing stops

Lined up this way,

then

not "the moment the tool stopped" but "the moment the problem started"

comes into view.

But are the numbers on the screen really correct?

There is one more problem here.

Say the screen shows

Cooling water pressure is low

as a message.

So has the water pressure really dropped?

You can't tell that right away either.

That is because the tool's screen is not looking directly inside the pipe.

Put very simply, the flow is:

Water pressure
↓
A part that measures it
↓
An electrical signal
↓
The tool's computer
↓
Shown on the screen

in that order.

A home thermometer works the same way.

Is your body temperature really 39°C?

Or is the thermometer broken and just showing 39°C?

You can't tell from the display alone.

With semiconductor equipment, too, you separate two questions:

Is something really wrong with the equipment itself?

Or

is something wrong with the part doing the measuring?

You think about these separately.

This is where words like Sensor and PLC come in

If you understand everything so far, the technical terms are not that hard.

A part that measures pressure or temperature is called a

Sensor

in English.

The information the sensor measures is sent to the tool as a

Signal

through wiring.

The entry point where that signal goes into the tool is called

I/O (input/output)

for short.

Then, one type of controller looks at that information and decides

whether things are normal
or abnormal
whether to run the tool
or stop it

That controller is called a

PLC

(programmable logic controller).

You don't need to memorize all of this at once.

For now,

Actual condition
→ Measure it
→ Send the information
→ The tool makes a decision

understanding just this flow is enough.

Look at the "change," not a single moment's number

Maintenance technicians don't look only at the numbers from the moment the tool stopped.

For example, did the cooling water pressure

stay at 5 the whole time and then suddenly drop to 2?

5
↓
4.8
↓
4.5
↓
4.0
↓
3.0

Or did it fall little by little like this?

This difference is very important.

So they look at

how the numbers changed over time

instead.

For example, a graph with time on the horizontal axis and water pressure on the vertical axis.

A record of changes over time like this is called

Trend Data

in technical terms.

So you don't need to overthink it.

It is just a history of numbers.

That's all.

Looking at it, you can sometimes see things like

"The water pressure started dropping 10 minutes before the tool stopped."

That kind of thing can become clear.

Then,

a failure you thought had "happened suddenly"

may turn out

to have been getting worse little by little for some time.

Equipment maintenance is a bit like detective work

Let's sum up the flow so far.

The tool stopped.

The first fact you could confirm was:

The cooling water pressure reading had dropped.

That is all.

You still cannot say that

the cooling water system broke.

Not yet.

So you ask,

Did the water pressure actually drop?

That is one possible cause.

At the same time,

Is the sensor that measures pressure faulty?

That is another possible cause.

Then you check each one using a safe method.

This way of thinking has names.

What you have actually confirmed is a

Fact

in this way of thinking.

A possible cause you have not yet confirmed is a

Hypothesis

for now.

Checking whether that hypothesis is correct is called

Verification

in this process.

And finally, when you can confirm

"This was the real cause of this stoppage,"

that is the

Root Cause

of the problem.

But memorizing these English words is not what matters.

What matters is this way of thinking:

Don't jump to conclusions based only on the symptoms you see.

List the possible causes.

Check them one by one.

That is the mindset.

If it runs after a Reset, is the repair done?

There is one more important point.

When the tool stopped,

you pressed Reset and it started running again.

At that point,

"It's fixed!"

is what you want to think.

But is it really fixed?

For example, say a breaker trips at home.

You reset the breaker and the lights come back on.

But if you never find out why the breaker tripped, it may trip again.

Equipment is the same.

Being able to restart the tool and

having the cause removed are two different things.

That is why, in equipment maintenance,

why the tool stopped
and whether the same thing could happen again

you check these things as well.

So when a tool stops, what do you look at first?

Let's sum everything up as simply as possible.

When a tool stops,

first make sure it is safe.

Then,

(1) Look at which alarms appeared

But don't decide where the failure is based on that alone.

Next,

(2) Look at what happened first

Check the times of the alarms and events.

Then,

(3) Look at whether the numbers were already changing before that

Check the history of pressure, temperature, and so on.

Also,

(4) Separate whether the equipment itself is at fault or the measuring side is at fault

Think about it this way.

Finally,

(5) Check the possible causes one by one

That is the flow.

A maintenance technician's job is not "to clear alarms"

When people think of equipment maintenance work,

they may picture "someone who replaces broken parts."

That image is common.

But in reality, the work that comes before that is very important.

The tool stopped.

An alarm appeared.

The numbers changed.

So,

why did that happen?

You work through this

one step at a time while looking at the tool's records and condition.

In other words, a maintenance technician is

someone who tracks down the real cause from the symptoms on the screen

as part of the job.

If you have worked in manufacturing

and have "looked for the cause when a machine acted strangely by checking its sounds, temperature, and movement,"

that way of thinking carries over to semiconductor equipment as well.

Summary

The one thing I most want you to remember from this article is this.

An alarm is not the answer.

An alarm is an entry point.

"Something is wrong here."

That is what it tells you.

From there,

When did it first change?
Is the equipment really at fault?
Is the measuring side working normally?
How did the other numbers change?

you check these one by one.

Then,

Symptom → Possible causes → Checks → Real cause

That is how you move forward.

This is the basic way of thinking used in equipment maintenance for semiconductor manufacturing equipment.

Now that you have finished reading, words like

Alarm Cascade, Sensor, Trend Data, and Root Cause

should make a little more sense than they did at the start.
